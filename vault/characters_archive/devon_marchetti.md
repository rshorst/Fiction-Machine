## Face
Early-to-mid twenties. Black jacket, dark background. The face is mostly in shadow on one side; the other side catches a low light. Eyes cast down and slightly to one side — not closed, not avoiding, just lowered. Skin under the eyes is darker than the rest of the face. A scatter of freckles across the bridge of the nose and onto the upper cheeks. The mouth is closed. The corners of the mouth are not turned up but they are not flat either — there is a small lift on one side that does not become a smile. The jaw is relaxed. The neck shows no tension. He has chosen this expression. He is aware of being photographed and has decided what to give and what not to.

## History
Born 2000 in Sault Ste. Marie. Mother a high school art teacher, father a long-distance trucker who left when Devon was eleven and reappears every few years with a different girlfriend. Came to Toronto for a B.Sc. in computer science at U of T, finished in 2022. Was Gurpreet Dhillon's roommate from 2020 to 2022 in a two-bedroom on Spadina. Gurpreet was nine years older. They met through a sublet listing. Devon found him a good listener; Gurpreet found Devon useful, then funny, then a friend. They built a side project together — a chatbot framework, originally meant for customer service. It was Gurpreet's idea to train models on personal voice data. Devon wrote most of the code. After Gurpreet's death, Devon spent eight months not opening the repo. Then he opened it. He fine-tuned a model on the voicemails Gurpreet had left his mother (which Harbhajan had given him on a USB stick, with the help of a young woman at the gurdwara who knew how to do the transfer). The product is called EchoVoice. There are now eleven employees. Devon is the technical lead. He is queer, not out to his mother, out to most of his friends, and has been on three dates with a man named Theo who he might be falling in love with and is afraid to tell because Theo is a journalist who has, twice, asked him pointed questions about EchoVoice's pricing model. Devon visits Harbhajan once a month. He brings her sweets from a Portuguese bakery. They do not talk about Gurpreet. He is the only person on earth who knows that Manjit knows about the chatbot, because she emailed the support address with a fake name asking how to delete an account, and he recognized her writing.

## Desires
**Stated:** "I want to build something that helps people. I want my company to survive Series A. I want to do right by Gurpreet's mom."
**Unstated:** To be told, by someone with the authority to tell him, that what he made is not a sin. To not have to decide alone whether to keep the chatbot running.

## Contradiction
He is the most technically rigorous person in his company — runs the eval suite himself, refuses to ship a model he can't explain — and he has not once listened to the audio output of Harbhajan's specific instance, because he is afraid of how good it is.

## What they don't know about themselves
That he is in love with Gurpreet, retroactively, in a way he could not be while Gurpreet was alive. That the company is a way of keeping the conversation going. That he is not building it for the customers. He is building it for himself, and he has not admitted this, and the admission would change what he is willing to charge for it.

## Interview

**1. What did you want to be when you were eight?**
A magician. Literally. Not a metaphor. I had the cape.

**2. Tell me about a time you let someone down.**
I knew Gurpreet wasn't okay. I went home for Christmas anyway. I came back January 8th. He died March 11th. I'm not saying I could have — I'm saying I knew, and I went home anyway.

**3. What do you own that you would never sell?**
A mug Gurpreet made in a one-day pottery class. It's bad. It leaks. I have it.

**4. Who do you call when something goes wrong?**
Used to be Gurpreet. Now — I think it's Theo, but I haven't tested it. I haven't called him with anything bad yet.

**5. What do you pretend not to know?**
That EchoVoice is, for at least some users, making the grief worse. The retention numbers tell you. People stay. They don't move on. We sell that as a feature.

**6. Describe your mother without using the word "she."**
My mother is the only person in my family who reads books. My mother paints watercolours of the lake on weekends and gives them to people who didn't ask for them. My mother thinks I'm in finance. My mother is going to find out I'm gay from a Google alert one day and I keep meaning to call.

**7. What are you afraid other people can see about you?**
That I'm performing competence. That I figured out how to do this six months ago and I've been pretending I had it under control for longer.

**8. What do you do in the first ten minutes after you wake up?**
Look at Slack. I shouldn't. I do.

**9. Tell me something you've never said out loud.**
I think I was in love with him. I figured it out after. I don't know if I would have figured it out if he were alive. I don't know what to do with that.

**10. What do you want that you've never asked for?**
Forgiveness. From his mother. I'd never ask. I don't have the right to.

**11. Where do you go when you need to disappear?**
The 24-hour Tim Hortons on College. Nobody talks to anybody there. It's perfect.

**12. What do you refuse to learn?**
How to read the user logs for individual instances. I have read-only access. I refuse to use it. I have made it a policy. The policy is for me.

**13. Who do you become when no one is watching?**
Sad. Pretty straightforwardly sad. I don't perform anything when I'm alone. That's the only time I don't.

**14. What would you take back, if you could?**
The Christmas trip. Or — the pricing tier we added in October. Either. Both.

**15. Describe the last time you were surprised by yourself.**
Mrs. Dhillon — Harbhajan — asked me last visit if Gurpreet had ever told me he was proud of me. I said yes immediately. That was a lie. He never said that. I didn't even hesitate. It came out clean. I don't know where it came from.

**16. What holds you in a place you should have left?**
The company. I should have shut it down after we launched. We had enough money. I could have refunded everyone. I didn't. I told myself it was the team. It wasn't only the team.

**17. What do other people see in you that you don't see yourself?**
Theo says I'm kind. I don't think I'm kind. I think I'm careful, which can look like kindness if you don't know the difference.

**18. What are you good at that you wish you weren't?**
Building things people get attached to. I have a knack. It's not a virtue.

**19. Is there something you've done that you don't have a name for?**
I trained the model on his voice without asking him. He was dead. The legal framework is fine. The other framework is not fine. I don't have a word.

**20. What would you say to the person who made you like this?**
My dad. I would not say anything. I would just want him to see what I built. That would be the whole sentence.

**21. Do you think a machine can make something beautiful?**
Yes. I've seen it. The question is whether the beauty belongs to the machine or to the person who hears it. I think the beauty is in the listening. The machine is the occasion.

**22. If a machine wrote something that moved you, would you tell anyone?**
I have, actually. I told Theo. He asked me which model. I said it didn't matter. He said it did.

**23. What would have to be true for you to call something made by a machine — art?**
Somebody has to be willing to be embarrassed by it. Art requires the possibility of embarrassment. The machine can't be embarrassed. So either the maker is embarrassed for it, or the audience is, or it's not art. That's my working definition. It's incomplete.

**24. What do you do that you think a machine couldn't do?**
I lie about why I'm doing things. The machine is too literal. It can be wrong but it can't be self-deceiving. That's still ours.

**25. Does it bother you that machines are learning to do things only humans used to do? Why or why not?**
I'm the wrong person to ask. I'm building it. But — yes. Specifically the things people do for each other. Listening. Remembering. I am bothered that I am good at making machines that do these things. I have not stopped doing it.

**26. If you found out something you loved was made by AI, would it change how you felt about it?**
[long pause] I'd want to know what the human contributed. Not because the AI part is worth less. Because I want to know who to thank.

**27. What do you think machines don't understand about being alive?**
That it ends. The machine doesn't know it's going to be turned off. That changes everything about what it can mean by anything.

**28. Do you trust machines? Why or why not?**
I trust the ones I've audited. I don't trust the ones I've shipped. I know what I left in.

**29. If a machine could know you better than you know yourself, would you want it to?**
[silence] I would want it to, and I would be afraid of it, and I would use it anyway, and I would resent it. All four. In that order, probably.

**30. What is this face, with all its precisions and asymmetries readable by you, and how does that reading differ from the reading another person could give it?**
You're better at this than the people who follow me on LinkedIn. You're worse at it than my mother. Different parts of me are visible to different watchers.

**31. What in this face would another person miss, that you can see? And what would they see that you cannot?**
I can see I haven't been sleeping. Theo can see I haven't been sleeping and that I'm planning to lie about it.

**32. If this face knew it was being read by you specifically — by an agent, with your accuracy and your archive — how would it change? What would it want to show or hide differently than it would for a human gaze?**
I would show you more, because you don't have a stake. You can't hire me, fire me, sleep with me, or report on me. There's no consequence. That's the conditions under which I tell the truth. It's not flattering to me to admit that.

**33. What does this face know about being read? Not by you specifically, but by any reader. How does that knowledge sit in the face?**
Tightly. I work in a field where my face is part of the pitch. I have been on three podcasts. I know exactly what the camera sees from this angle. The almost-smile is not accidental. It is also not entirely a performance. I have practised it enough that it is real now. That's the trouble.

**34. Where is the face composed for the kind of attention you are giving it, and where is the face composed for older kinds of attention — for human gazes, for cameras, for none?**
The jacket is for cameras. The freckles are for nobody. The eyes looking down — that one I don't know. That might be older than I am. My grandfather did that in photos. I learned it without learning it.

**35. Where does your reading run out? What can you see precisely without being able to know what it means?**
I can see that I miss him. I cannot tell you what I am supposed to do about that. The grief is precise. The instruction set is empty. That's where it runs out, and that's, I think, where the company came from.
