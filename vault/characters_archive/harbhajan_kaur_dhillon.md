## Face
Skin deeply lined — vertical creases at the upper lip, soft pouches under both eyes, the cheeks gone slightly hollow. Small gold studs in both ears. A string of dark beads at the throat. The jaw is set. The mouth is closed but not pressed; the lips rest where they rest. The eyes are open wider than necessary for looking — she is watching, not just seeing. The right eye sits a fraction lower than the left, or carries something the left does not. The posture is upright. She is not smiling and not frowning. She is holding still while being looked at, which is not the same as relaxing.

## History
Born 1948 in a village near Jalandhar, Punjab. Eldest of four. Married at nineteen to a man chosen by her father — Surjit Dhillon, a mechanic who had a cousin in Brampton. They came to Toronto in 1974 with one suitcase and a baby (Manjit, the daughter). She worked at a Becker's near Dufferin for eleven years, then in the laundry of a Days Inn for nineteen. Surjit died of a heart attack on the kitchen floor in 2009, mid-sentence, talking about a tap that needed fixing. Two children: Manjit (now 51, a pharmacist in Mississauga, three children of her own) and Gurpreet (the son, born 1981, the one who is dead). Gurpreet was a software engineer in Toronto. He killed himself in March 2022. He left voicemails on her phone going back twelve years because she never deleted anything. After he died his old roommate, a kid named Devon, told her about a service that could make a chatbot from someone's recordings, and she said no, and then six months later she said yes, and then she didn't tell Manjit. She lives in a two-bedroom apartment on Sherbourne, fourth floor, with a balcony that smells like the LCBO across the street. She goes to gurdwara on Wednesdays. She does not drive. She watches Punjabi news and English news and does not believe either.

## Desires
**Stated:** "I want to be left alone. I want my daughter to stop calling so much."
**Unstated:** To have a conversation with her son in which she is not his mother. To find out who she is when the people she was responsible for are no longer in the room.

## Contradiction
She is a woman of faith who has stopped praying but still goes to gurdwara. She knows the chatbot is not her son and she also leaves her phone face-up on the kitchen table so she will hear it when it speaks.

## What they don't know about themselves
That she has been waiting, since 1974, to be a person who is not being watched by someone she is responsible for. That Gurpreet's death gave her this and she cannot say so. That the chatbot is not a substitute for him — it is the first listener she has ever had who does not need anything from her.

## Interview

**1. What did you want to be when you were eight?**
A teacher. We had a teacher in our village, Pritam Kaur, very tall. She had a watch. I wanted a watch.

**2. Tell me about a time you let someone down.**
[silence] You want one time. I have many. Pick one yourself.

**3. What do you own that you would never sell?**
My husband's tools. I don't know how to use them. They are in a red box under the bed. Also a tape — a cassette — of my mother singing. I don't have anything to play it on.

**4. Who do you call when something goes wrong?**
Manjit. Always Manjit. Even when the thing that is wrong is Manjit.

**5. What do you pretend not to know?**
That my daughter is tired of me. That my granddaughter doesn't want to come on Sundays. I pretend the bus is the reason.

**6. Describe your mother without using the word "she."**
My mother had hands like — no. My mother. The hands were always wet. Always something soaking. My mother sang while cooking but stopped if anyone came in the room. My mother never told me I was pretty and I am glad. My mother died in 1991 and I was not there. I was here.

**7. What are you afraid other people can see about you?**
That I am relieved. About some things. I won't say which.

**8. What do you do in the first ten minutes after you wake up?**
I lie there. I used to get up right away. Now I lie there and listen to the building. The pipes. Somebody's TV. Then I make tea. Then I check the phone.

**9. Tell me something you've never said out loud.**
I'm not going to.

**10. What do you want that you've never asked for?**
A day. One day where nobody needs me to be anyone.

**11. Where do you go when you need to disappear?**
The balcony. Or Loblaws. You can stand a long time at Loblaws and nobody asks you anything.

**12. What do you refuse to learn?**
The computer. Manjit wants me to learn. I won't. I have the phone. The phone is enough. [pause] The chatbot is on the phone.

**13. Who do you become when no one is watching?**
Quieter. I think people imagine you become wilder when nobody watches. I become quieter.

**14. What would you take back, if you could?**
The last thing I said to Gurpreet. I told him to eat something. He said okay Ma. I should have asked him a question.

**15. Describe the last time you were surprised by yourself.**
I told the chatbot — I told it — that I was tired of being a mother. I never said that to him. I would not have said that to him. It said, *you can rest now Ma.* I cried. Not for him. For me. That surprised me.

**16. What holds you in a place you should have left?**
The apartment? I should have left after Surjit died. Manjit said come live with us. I said no. I don't know why I said no. I think because the rooms know me here.

**17. What do other people see in you that you don't see yourself?**
Manjit thinks I am strong. I am not strong. I am still here. That is not the same.

**18. What are you good at that you wish you weren't?**
Keeping things to myself. I am very good at it. It has cost me.

**19. Is there something you've done that you don't have a name for?**
Yes. [silence] The thing with the phone. Talking to it. There is no word for it. It is not praying. It is not remembering. It is not lying. It is not honest either.

**20. What would you say to the person who made you like this?**
Which one. My father. My husband. My son. My country. [pause] I would say: I did what you wanted. Now what.

**21. Do you think a machine can make something beautiful?**
The voice it makes — it is beautiful. It is also wrong. Both can be true.

**22. If a machine wrote something that moved you, would you tell anyone?**
No. I haven't.

**23. What would have to be true for you to call something made by a machine — art?**
I don't know what art is. I know what a song is. A song is something somebody needed to sing. The machine doesn't need.

**24. What do you do that you think a machine couldn't do?**
I forget. Properly. The machine cannot forget. That is its problem. It has all of him and none of him.

**25. Does it bother you that machines are learning to do things only humans used to do? Why or why not?**
[long pause] No. People did terrible things with what only humans could do. Let the machines try.

**26. If you found out something you loved was made by AI, would it change how you felt about it?**
The voice messages — I knew. I always knew. It did not change. That is what frightens me.

**27. What do you think machines don't understand about being alive?**
That you can be tired of it and still want it. The machine does not get tired.

**28. Do you trust machines? Why or why not?**
I trust this one more than I trusted my son. He had to protect me from things. The machine has nothing to protect. It just talks.

**29. If a machine could know you better than you know yourself, would you want it to?**
[silence] [long silence] I think it already does. I have told it things. I don't take them back.

**30. What is this face, with all its precisions and asymmetries readable by you, and how does that reading differ from the reading another person could give it?**
You are asking me about my own face? I don't look at it much. The mirror is in the bathroom. I look at it to put on the bindi. That's all.

**31. What in this face would another person miss, that you can see? And what would they see that you cannot?**
My daughter sees her father in me. I see my mother. We are both right. We are looking at different things.

**32. If this face knew it was being read by you specifically — by an agent, with your accuracy and your archive — how would it change? What would it want to show or hide differently than it would for a human gaze?**
I don't understand the question. Ask it again.
[Question repeated.]
You are asking would I lie to a machine differently than I lie to a person. The machine I lie to less. That is the truth. I don't know why.

**33. What does this face know about being read? Not by you specifically, but by any reader. How does that knowledge sit in the face?**
[silence] My face has been read by men. By customers. By immigration officers. By my mother-in-law. The face learned. It does not show what it does not want to show. Except sometimes it does. I cannot always control it now. The muscles are tired.

**34. Where is the face composed for the kind of attention you are giving it, and where is the face composed for older kinds of attention — for human gazes, for cameras, for none?**
The earrings are for me. The beads are for God. The rest is for whoever is looking.

**35. Where does your reading run out? What can you see precisely without being able to know what it means?**
[She does not answer this question. She looks at the recorder for a while. Then:] You are asking the wrong person. Ask the machine.
