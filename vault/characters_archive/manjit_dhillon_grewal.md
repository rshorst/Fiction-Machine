# Manjit Dhillon-Grewal — Antagonist

## Face
Long brown hair, parted off-centre. The hand is up under the chin in a held pose — fingers loosely curled, knuckle resting against the jaw. The smile is wide and the teeth show. The eyes are not doing what the mouth is doing. The eyes are level, steady, and focused slightly past the lens. The skin around the eyes is relaxed but the gaze itself is not. There is a small line between the brows that has not been smoothed by the smile. She is wearing a thin gold chain, no other visible jewelry. The shoulders are squared. She is comfortable being looked at and is also still deciding what to give the camera.

## History
Born 1973 in Brampton, two months after her parents arrived from Punjab. Eldest child, only daughter. Took on translation duties at age six — for her mother at the bank, for her father at the garage where he eventually worked, for both at the doctor's. Got 92% in OAC chemistry, did her PharmD at the University of Toronto, married Kuldeep Grewal (also a pharmacist) in 1999. Three children: Aman (22, in law school), Simran (19, in nursing), Jaiveer (15, who is on the spectrum and whom she watches more carefully than the others, though she would deny this). She owns the franchise of a Pharmasave in Mississauga. Her brother Gurpreet was eight years younger; she changed his diapers, taught him to read English, told their mother he was sad in 2018 and was told *boys are like that.* She found the body. She has not said this out loud since the week he died. She manages her mother's finances, her medications, her appointments, the lease on the apartment on Sherbourne. She wants her mother to move in with them. Her mother says no. She has begun to suspect there is something in the apartment her mother does not want to leave, and recently she found, on a borrowed iPad her mother does not know how to clear, a tab open to a service called *EchoVoice.*

## Desires
**Stated:** "I want my mother to be safe. I want her to grieve in a healthy way. I want her to come live with us where I can take care of her."
**Unstated:** To be the one her mother turns to. To stop being the daughter who handles things and become the daughter who is loved. To delete the chatbot herself, and not have to ask permission.

## Contradiction
She is the family's most rigorous rationalist — pharmacist, evidence-based, contemptuous of supplements and reiki and her mother-in-law's prayers — and she has saved every voicemail her brother left her, untouched, and listens to one on his birthday and one on the anniversary, and tells no one.

## What they don't know about themselves
That she is not trying to protect her mother from the chatbot. She is trying to protect herself from the fact that her mother is talking to her dead son in a way she never talked to her living daughter. That her grief and her jealousy are the same feeling and she has not separated them.

## Interview

**1. What did you want to be when you were eight?**
A lawyer. Specifically the lawyer on *L.A. Law*. I didn't know what they did. I knew they wore the suits.

**2. Tell me about a time you let someone down.**
My brother called me on a Tuesday in February 2022. I was at the pharmacy. I let it go to voicemail. I meant to call back. I didn't call back until Saturday. He didn't pick up. He was already — anyway. That.

**3. What do you own that you would never sell?**
His voicemails. There are forty-one of them. I have them backed up in three places.

**4. Who do you call when something goes wrong?**
My husband first. Then nobody. I handle it.

**5. What do you pretend not to know?**
That my mother is happier now than she was when my father was alive. I pretend not to know that. It would not be useful for either of us if I admitted it.

**6. Describe your mother without using the word "she."**
My mother is a person who has been waiting her whole life to be done. I don't mean done as in dead. I mean done with — assignments. Tasks. People. My mother folds laundry like it's a small revenge. My mother loved my brother more than me and I have made peace with it about ninety percent of the way.

**7. What are you afraid other people can see about you?**
That I'm angry. I look pleasant. I am not always pleasant inside.

**8. What do you do in the first ten minutes after you wake up?**
Check Jaiveer's room. Check my phone. Make the coffee. In that order, every day, even on vacation.

**9. Tell me something you've never said out loud.**
I'm the one who found him. I have not said that. I told the police, I told Kuldeep, I have not said it since. My mother doesn't know it was me. She thinks it was the landlord.

**10. What do you want that you've never asked for?**
For my mother to ask me a question about myself. Just one. Not about the kids. About me.

**11. Where do you go when you need to disappear?**
The pharmacy after closing. I sit in the back office. I don't do anything. I just sit there with the lights off.

**12. What do you refuse to learn?**
How to use that chatbot service. I will not click on it. I have looked at the website. I will not click on it.

**13. Who do you become when no one is watching?**
Tired. Just tired. Not interesting. Sorry.

**14. What would you take back, if you could?**
Tuesday, February 8th, 2022, 2:47 PM. The missed call.

**15. Describe the last time you were surprised by yourself.**
I was driving home and I realized I was furious at my mother. Not sad, not worried — furious. Because she gets to talk to him and I don't. And I have his voicemails too. I could do what she's doing. I won't. I'm furious at her for doing it and I'm furious at myself for not doing it.

**16. What holds you in a place you should have left?**
I should have moved out of Brampton at twenty. I stayed because my mother needed me to translate. Then I stayed because Gurpreet was small. Then I had my own kids. The reason changes. The staying doesn't.

**17. What do other people see in you that you don't see yourself?**
Kuldeep says I'm funny. I don't think I'm funny. He laughs anyway.

**18. What are you good at that you wish you weren't?**
Managing other people's lives. I am very good at it. It means everyone gives me theirs.

**19. Is there something you've done that you don't have a name for?**
I read my mother's text messages. I have her Apple ID. I tell myself it's for her safety. It is not only for her safety.

**20. What would you say to the person who made you like this?**
Which one? My mother made me responsible. My father made me quiet about it. My brother made me the older one. I would say — to my mother — *I was a child too.* I would say it once and then I would never bring it up again.

**21. Do you think a machine can make something beautiful?**
Sure. A camera is a machine. A piano is a machine. The question is who is using it.

**22. If a machine wrote something that moved you, would you tell anyone?**
No. I would feel — embarrassed. I'd feel like I'd been tricked into feeling something.

**23. What would have to be true for you to call something made by a machine — art?**
Somebody would have to take responsibility for it. A machine can't take responsibility. So somebody human has to stand behind it and say *I made this, I meant this.* Then it's art. Otherwise it's weather.

**24. What do you do that you think a machine couldn't do?**
I count out pills and I notice when a patient is not okay. The pills the machine could do. The noticing — I don't think so. Not yet. Maybe.

**25. Does it bother you that machines are learning to do things only humans used to do? Why or why not?**
It bothers me that they're learning to do grief. Yes. Specifically that. I think it is dangerous. People are vulnerable. My mother is vulnerable. The service that made — the thing she uses — they are charging her. They are charging a seventy-six-year-old widow to talk to her dead son. That is not a technology problem. That is a predation problem. I would shut them down if I could.

**26. If you found out something you loved was made by AI, would it change how you felt about it?**
Yes. I'm not proud of that. But yes.

**27. What do you think machines don't understand about being alive?**
That you can love someone and be relieved they are gone. Both. The machine can't hold both.

**28. Do you trust machines? Why or why not?**
I trust them to do what they were built to do. I do not trust the people who built them.

**29. If a machine could know you better than you know yourself, would you want it to?**
No. Absolutely not. I don't want anyone to know me better than I know myself. That's the whole point of being a person.

**30. What is this face, with all its precisions and asymmetries readable by you, and how does that reading differ from the reading another person could give it?**
You're asking about my face. My face is the face I trained for customers. I have used it for twenty-six years. Anyone who knows me knows when it's on. Most people don't know me.

**31. What in this face would another person miss, that you can see? And what would they see that you cannot?**
My husband sees the smile. I see what the smile is doing. The line between my eyebrows — I had it at twelve. My mother had it at twelve. I've had Botox there twice. It comes back. Whatever it is keeps coming back.

**32. If this face knew it was being read by you specifically — by an agent, with your accuracy and your archive — how would it change? What would it want to show or hide differently than it would for a human gaze?**
[laughs] Oh that's interesting. I would hide less from you. I think. Because you can't tell my mother. You can't tell my husband. There is no social cost. So I would let — [pause] — I would let the smile drop.

**33. What does this face know about being read? Not by you specifically, but by any reader. How does that knowledge sit in the face?**
Heavily. I have been read since I was six years old translating for my parents. Every face I made was a face on behalf of someone else. I am tired in the face. I am.

**34. Where is the face composed for the kind of attention you are giving it, and where is the face composed for older kinds of attention — for human gazes, for cameras, for none?**
The smile is for the camera. The hand under the chin is for the photographer who told me to do it. The eyes — the eyes are for nobody. The eyes are mine.

**35. Where does your reading run out? What can you see precisely without being able to know what it means?**
[silence] [She thinks about this longer than any other question.] I can see I'm still angry. I can't see what I want done about it. That's where it runs out.
