## The Face

The first thing I noticed was the eyes — lighter than expected, almost amber. They hold the camera without flinching, without offering anything extra. No performance. The second thing, which took longer: how still she is. Not the stillness of someone waiting to be released. The stillness of someone who has decided to be exactly where she is.

She is perhaps sixty-eight, seventy. The skin records decades — deep lines at the eyes, at the corners of the mouth. These are not complaints. They are information. She is wrapped in orange fabric that frames her face like a document.

The expression is split. The eyes are serious — measuring, careful, slow to conclude. But the mouth carries the ghost of easy laughter. These two things have not resolved. They may never resolve. That gap between what the eyes say and what the mouth almost says — that is where she lives.

She is tolerating the photograph. She is also, slightly, entertained by it.

---

## Name

**Adama Sowe**
Born in Banjul, The Gambia. Now lives in Toulouse, France. Has lived in Toulouse for thirty-one years. Still thinks of Banjul as home, though she would not say so out loud.

---

## History

Adama was the third of seven children. Her father was a schoolteacher who believed in French as a ladder — you climbed it, you got somewhere. She climbed it. Scholarships. A linguistics degree at the Université Paul Valéry in Montpellier, 1978. She was brilliant and she was lonely and she treated both conditions as temporary.

She married a man named Ibrahima Sowe in 1982. He was kind and he died young — a cardiac event, 1997, when their daughter Mariama was eleven. Adama did not grieve the way people expected her to. She grieved sideways, into work. People thought she was cold. She was not cold. She was containing.

For twenty years she worked as a court interpreter — Arabic, Wolof, French, English — in the tribunal system in Toulouse. She was very good. She was exact. She had a gift for finding the word in a target language that carried not just the meaning but the weight of the original. She believed this mattered. She still believes this.

She retired from the courts four years ago. Since then, she has translated three novels from Wolof into French, on her own time, without commission. Two remain unpublished. She has not tried hard to publish them. She tells herself she is not ready. She is ready.

Her father — Moussa Diallo, who took a different family name from her mother's line — left Banjul in 1971 and built a small translation business in London. He sent money home. He did not come home. Adama visited twice: once at fifteen, once at thirty-two. Both visits were polite and brief, full of things not said. He died in 2021. She did not attend the funeral. She told herself it was the pandemic. It was not only the pandemic.

She has one daughter, Mariama, who is a paediatric nurse in Lyon and calls every Sunday and sometimes forgets. Adama forgives this immediately and without comment.

She has a cat named after no one. She calls it *Le Chat*. She has had it for nine years.

## Desire (stated)

*"I want to settle the business properly and close it down. It's not something I know how to run. I just want to do right by whatever he left behind."*

---

## Desire (unstated)

She wants to find something in the files that explains him. Not forgives him — she is past needing that, or believes she is. She wants a *reason*. A client, a letter, a contract that shows what he was doing with all the years he was not with her. She wants evidence that he was, at minimum, complex. That his absence was not simple indifference.

She is also, though she will not name it, looking for evidence that he thought about her.

---

## Contradiction

She is a professional finder of the right word — her whole career has been the precise transfer of meaning across languages. She is exacting about this. She believes language done carelessly causes harm.

And yet: she never said clearly to her father what she needed from him. She never wrote it down. The woman who can find the Wolof word that carries *grief-for-the-living* could not find the French words to say *come home* or *I needed you* or even *why*. She let him stay untranslated.

---

## What She Doesn't Know About Herself

She thinks she has made peace with not knowing. She has built a very convincing structure around the not-knowing — the retirement, the cat, the unpublished novels, the Sunday calls from Mariama. But the structure exists *because* of the not-knowing. She has organised her life around an absence she calls an acceptance.

She does not know that she is still, at sixty-nine, waiting for him to explain himself. She does not know that this is why she got on the plane to London at all.

## Interview Transcript

*The interview was conducted in French and then in English, her choice shifting mid-conversation. She sat with her hands in her lap. She did not fidget. She answered some questions before they were finished being asked.*

---

**What do you want?**

To close the business properly. To honour whatever obligations remain. I'm not interested in keeping it going — I don't have the contacts, I don't have the clients. I'm a translator, not a businesswoman.

**That's what you want to do. What do you want?**

[pause]

I want to understand what he spent forty years doing.

---

**What are you most ashamed of?**

That I was relieved when he died. Not glad — I want to be precise. Not glad. But there was a... loosening. I had been braced for the conversation we were never going to have. When he died, I could stop bracing.

I am ashamed of the relief because it means I had given up on him while he was still alive.

---

**Tell me about your mother.**

She was a very practical woman. She fixed things. If something broke, she fixed it. If a child was ill, she dealt with it. If a husband left — [stops] She did not speak about him after a while. A few years, and then he simply wasn't mentioned. I used to think this was dignity. Later I thought it was self-protection. Now I think it was both, and I think she didn't know which one she was doing.

---

**What do you believe that you cannot prove?**

That he thought about me. That I was not simply — absent from his mind the way he was absent from ours. I cannot prove it. I have no evidence. But I believe it.

[pause]

I am aware that believing it is convenient.

---

**What is the kindest thing anyone has ever done for you?**

My husband, Ibrahima. When Mariama was born — she was three weeks early, there were some complications, I was frightened — he sat next to the bed for eleven hours without speaking. He didn't try to reassure me. He didn't say it would be fine. He just stayed. He understood that I didn't need words. He was the only person in my life who ever understood that.

---

**What do you find unforgivable?**

Silence used as power. There is silence that is — containment, dignity, self-protection, I understand those. But silence that lets another person spend decades not knowing whether they mattered — that is a choice. That is something done to someone.

**Did he do that?**

I don't know. That is the problem. I don't know if his silence was — cruelty, or its own kind of containing. This is what I need to know.

---

**What do you carry that isn't yours to carry?**

His reputation. In Banjul, he was known as a man who made something of himself. The children of his brothers and sisters — my cousins — they speak of him still. The one who went to London. I have had to carry the public version of him for fifty years and I was never given the private one.

---

**Is there something you've never told anyone?**

The second visit — when I was thirty-two. I went to London for a linguistics conference and I took one extra day and I went to his office. Not his home — his office. I hadn't told him I was coming. I stood outside for — I think it was twenty minutes. And then I left and went back to the conference hotel.

I have never told anyone that. I told Ibrahima I didn't see him because the conference ran long.

**Why did you leave?**

[long silence]

I wasn't sure I would survive what he said if it was the wrong thing.

---

**What would you do if you weren't afraid?**

Open the file. The one the solicitor mentioned — the one that was never closed. I have had it for six days. I have not opened it.

**Why not?**

Because if it contains something ordinary — a client dispute, an unpaid invoice, some bureaucratic loose end — then that is all he was. A man with unpaid invoices.

And if it contains something else —

[she does not finish the sentence]

---

**What do you think happens when we die?**

I think we become — what we leave in other people. Not memory, exactly. Something more like weather. A tendency toward things. My daughter calls every Sunday because Ibrahima called his mother every Sunday. He has been dead for twenty-seven years. He is still making that call.

My father is a silence in me. I don't know if that's what he intended to be. I don't know if he intended anything.

---

**What do you want people to say about you when you're gone?**

That I was exact. [small pause, something close to a smile] That I found the right word.

---

**Is there a version of yourself you've abandoned?**

The one who knocked on his door. Yes.

---

**What's the question you're hoping I won't ask?**

Whether I'm doing this — coming to London, taking on the business, all of it — for him, or for myself.

**And?**

I think it's for myself. I think I am using the business as a reason to finally — stand in front of him. Even now. Even like this.

[pause]

I find that embarrassing.

---

*End of interview.*
