## Face
Late seventies, possibly early eighties. White hair worn loose, not pinned, with a thin pearl hairband. The smile is wide and asymmetrical — the right corner of the mouth lifts higher than the left. The eyes are dark and do not soften with the smile in the same proportion; they are appraising. Skin shows the lines of long expression — at the eyes, around the mouth, across the forehead. She is not retreating from the camera. She is also not arriving toward it. She is sitting still while it happens. The left side of the mouth holds back something the right side does not.

## History
Born in Tehran, 1944. Father a judge under the Pahlavi monarchy, mother from a Qajar-adjacent family with money that thinned each generation. The eldest of four. Educated at a French lycée in Tehran, then sent to Lausanne at seventeen to study literature, which she did not finish. She came home in 1963 and married, at twenty, a man eleven years older — Bijan, an architect — whom she had met at a wedding and decided about within an hour. Her family disapproved mildly and then loved him.

Three children: a daughter Roya in 1965, a son Kaveh in 1968, a son Daryoush in 1972. The family lived in a house in north Tehran with a garden that had a pomegranate tree and a stone fountain that never quite worked. She taught French part-time at a girls' school. Bijan designed apartment buildings and one small museum.

In 1979 they stayed. In 1981 they left. They got out through Turkey with two suitcases and the children, and an aunt of Bijan's who was already in Toronto sponsored them. The house in Tehran was taken. The pomegranate tree she has not seen since.

In Toronto Bijan could not get his architectural credentials recognized for almost a decade and worked as a draftsman and was furious in a quiet way that became how he was. Soraya cleaned houses for two years and then taught French at a private school in Forest Hill for twenty-six years. The children grew up Canadian. Roya is a pediatrician in Vancouver. Kaveh runs a furniture store in Toronto. Daryoush died in 2003 at thirty-one, of an overdose, after eight years of trying not to.

Bijan died in 2018, of a heart attack, in the kitchen, while making tea. She found him.

She lives now in a two-bedroom apartment in North York. She has six grandchildren. She volunteers two mornings a week at a settlement agency that helps newcomers, mostly Iranian, mostly women, mostly recent. She speaks four languages. She has not been back to Iran. She has been asked many times if she will go and she gives a different answer depending on who is asking.

## Desires
**Stated:** To live to see her oldest granddaughter graduate from university. To finish translating a small book of her aunt's poems into English — she has been working on it for nine years.
**Unstated:** To be asked about Daryoush. By someone who knew him. Anyone. Just to say his name out loud to a person who would recognize it.

## Contradiction
She is the family's unshakable center, the one everyone calls, and she has not slept through a night since 2003.

## What they don't know about themselves
That her warmth is not a habit and not a performance. It is a discipline. She decided, sometime in the year after Daryoush, that she would not become bitter, and she has held to that decision every day since, and she does not know that this counts as faith. She thinks she lost her faith decades ago. She did not.

## Interview

1. **What is your name, and is it the one you were given?**
Soraya. Yes. After the queen — not the new one, the second one, who could not have children and was put aside. My mother thought it was romantic. I thought it was a bad omen. I had three children, so. We were both wrong, in our ways.

2. **Where do you live, and is it where you mean to be?**
North York. In an apartment building with a view of other apartment buildings. It is where I live. *Mean* to be is a different question. I mean to be in a garden in Tehran in 1976. I am not. So this is where I mean to be.

3. **What did your mother do?**
She was beautiful and she ran a household. That was a profession in her time. She did it well. She managed eleven people — children, servants, her mother-in-law — and she never raised her voice. I raise my voice. I got that from my father.

4. **What did your father do?**
He was a judge. He believed in the law. He believed in it after it stopped being possible to believe in it. He died in 1980. I think it was a kindness, the timing.

5. **What are you good at that nobody pays you for?**
[laughs] Translating. Properly translating, not for the school. I am translating a book of poems by my aunt, who is dead. Nobody will publish it. I am still doing it.

6. **What are you bad at that you wish you weren't?**
Forgiving my son for being dead. He did not do it on purpose. I know this. I know this. I am still angry with him. I would like to not be.

7. **When did you last cry?**
This morning. Briefly. I was making tea and I thought of my husband and I cried for perhaps a minute and then I drank the tea.

8. **What do you lie about?**
About whether I will go back. People ask. I tell them what they want to hear. Sometimes I say yes, soon. Sometimes I say no, never. The truth is I do not know and I do not want to be asked.

9. **Who have you not forgiven?**
The pharmacist. In 2002. Daryoush went to him and he gave him what he asked for and he should not have. He is probably also dead by now. I have not forgiven him. It is not fair. I have not forgiven him.

10. **Who has not forgiven you?**
My daughter. For something specific. We had an argument in 2009 and I said something about her marriage and she has been polite to me since. Polite is the word. She calls every Sunday. She is polite.

11. **What is the worst thing you've ever done?**
[long pause] When my son was nineteen, the first time he was in trouble, I told him he was breaking my heart. I said it that way. *Breaking my heart.* I made it about me. He apologized to me. He should not have had to apologize to me. That is the worst thing.

12. **What is the best thing you've ever done?**
I left. In 1981. I did not want to. Bijan wanted to and I did not and I went anyway because the children. That was the best thing. The children are alive. Two of them. Two of them are alive.

13. **What are you afraid of?**
Dementia. Specifically. I am not afraid of dying. I am afraid of forgetting. I have things I am keeping. I do not want to put them down.

14. **What do you want that you have not told anyone?**
[silence]
I want someone to talk to me about Daryoush. Not to comfort me. Just to talk about him. To say: I remember when he did this. Nobody does. His friends are gone, scattered. His brother and sister will not. My husband is dead. I would like to hear his name in someone else's voice.

15. **What do you do when no one is watching?**
I talk to my husband. Out loud. In Persian. I tell him what the grandchildren are doing. I tell him what is in the news. I do not believe he hears me. I do it anyway.

16. **What is something you believe that most people you know do not?**
That a country can be lost. People here think a country is a place. A country is also a time. The Iran I am from does not exist. It is not that I cannot go back. There is nowhere to go back to.

17. **What is something most people believe that you do not?**
That grief becomes lighter. It does not become lighter. You become stronger, or you do not. The weight is the same.

18. **Describe a room you remember.**
The kitchen in our house in Tehran. Blue tiles, a particular blue, with a yellow flower in the center of each. A copper pot on the wall that my mother-in-law had given us. A radio on the windowsill. The window opened onto the garden and you could smell the jasmine in May. My daughter learned to walk in that kitchen. She fell and hit her head on the corner of the table and there is still — there was still — a small dark mark on the tile from the blood. I do not know if the tile is still there. I assume someone has replaced it.

19. **If you could go back, would you?**
To Tehran? No. To 2002, the week before? Every day. Every day I would go back. I know what I would do. I have rehearsed it. I would not say *breaking my heart.* I would say: tell me what is happening. Tell me. I am listening. That is what I would say.

20. **What question were you hoping I wouldn't ask?**
None of them. I am old. You can ask me anything. I have decided what I will answer and what I will not. You did not get past me on any of them. Did you think you did?
