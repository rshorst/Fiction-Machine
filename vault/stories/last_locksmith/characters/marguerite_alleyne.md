# Marguerite Alleyne

## Face

A woman in her late fifties or early sixties. Dark hair with silver running through it, mostly at the temples and along the part. The mouth is fully open in a laugh — not a smile, an actual laugh, with the upper teeth visible and the lower lip pulled down. The lines around her eyes are pulled into deep folds by the laugh; the lines around her mouth are deeper on the right side than the left. The eyes are not looking at the camera. They are directed up and to her left, focused on something specific outside the frame. She is sitting with her legs crossed. One hand rests flat on the upper knee. The other hand is holding a book, but loosely — fingers along the spine, the book half-closed around her thumb as a placeholder. Her shoulders are down, not raised by the laugh. The laugh is in her face but not in her body. The body is still.

## History

Born 1962 in Birmingham to a Barbadian mother (a nurse, NHS, arrived 1958) and an English father (a draughtsman from Wolverhampton who left when Marguerite was nine and was not heard from again until a solicitor's letter in 2003). Grew up the eldest of three in a terraced house in Handsworth. Read everything in the house and then everything in the library. Won a place at a grammar school where she was one of four Black girls in her year and learned how to make herself both visible and unreadable at the same time — a skill she would later teach, without naming it, to others.

Read English at Bristol, 1981–1984. Met Daniel there, a philosophy student from Leeds; married him in 1987 against the advice of her mother, who thought he was clever in a way that wouldn't hold up under pressure. Her mother was right but it took twenty-one years to confirm. Two children: Iyla (1989, now a paediatric registrar in Manchester) and Theo (1993, who has not held the same job for more than fourteen months and who calls her every Sunday).

Worked first as a secondary school English teacher in Bristol. Hated the bureaucracy, loved the moment when a fifteen-year-old understood enclosure for the first time. Did a part-time MA at Bristol in her thirties on Caribbean women writers — Lamming, Kincaid, Brodber — and the examiner told her she should do a PhD. She did, finished it at forty-one, and joined the academy late and sideways. Now a Reader in postcolonial literature at a redbrick university she will not name in interviews because she does not want to be a representative of it.

Divorced 2008. Lives alone in a flat in a converted Victorian school building. Smokes once a week, on Friday evenings, on the small balcony, and considers this a ritual rather than a habit. Her mother died in 2019. Her sister Cherie lives ten minutes away. Her brother Owen lives in Toronto and they speak twice a year and love each other completely.

She has been told she is intimidating since she was eleven. She has stopped trying to correct this.

## Desires

**Stated:** She wants to finish the book she is writing — a study of laughter in the work of Black women writers, which she has been describing as "nearly done" for three years. She wants her son to be all right. She wants a quiet decade.

**Unstated:** She wants to be looked at without being read. She wants to laugh in a room where no one notices her laughing, not because she is hidden but because no one is keeping a column of observations on her. She has been studied her whole life — by classmates, by colleagues, by men, by her own mother — and she is tired in a way she does not have language for, because she has been the language for everyone else.

## Contradiction

She has spent her career teaching people how to read carefully and she does not want to be read carefully. She believes attention is love. She does not want to be loved this much.

## What she doesn't know about herself

That the laugh in the photograph — the one directed at someone outside the frame — is the only one she has had in eight months. That she has been performing the rest of them, well enough that she has fooled herself. The body knows; the body has been still under the laughs for a long time. She thinks she is fine. She is not unwell, but she is not fine, and she has not yet noticed the difference.

## Interview

**1. What did you want to be when you were eight?**
A barrister. I had seen one on television — a Black woman, which I now think must have been imagined, because I cannot find her in any archive — and I decided. I told my mother and she said, *Yes, you'd be good at that, you like winning*. Which was true and also a slight insult. I think about that exchange more than I should.

**2. Tell me about a time you let someone down.**
Theo, my son. He was thirteen and he asked me, very specifically, not to come to a parents' evening because he was embarrassed of — well, he didn't say of what. He just didn't want me there. And I went anyway. I told myself I was advocating for him. I was advocating for myself, for the version of me that was a good mother who showed up. He was furious for a week and then he forgave me, which was worse.

**3. What do you own that you would never sell?**
My mother's wedding ring, which she gave me before she died and which doesn't fit any of my fingers. A first edition of *Annie John* that Jamaica Kincaid signed for me at a conference in 1996, with my name spelled wrong. A wooden bowl my brother made me when he was learning to turn wood, badly.

**4. Who do you call when something goes wrong?**
Cherie. My sister. She doesn't say very much. That's why.

**5. What do you pretend not to know?**
[silence]
…
That my son is using again. He's been clean for two years. He is not clean now. I have known for about six weeks. I have not said it to anyone, including him, including myself, until now.

**6. Describe your mother without using the word "she."**
Came over on a boat in 1958 with one suitcase and a nursing certificate. Hands always cold. Sang in the kitchen — Sparrow, mostly, and hymns. Did not approve of crying. Did approve of reading. Made the best peas and rice I have ever eaten and refused to write down the recipe because — and this is a direct quote — *if you want it, come and learn it*. I never did. That is the thing I will not forgive myself for.

**7. What are you afraid other people can see about you?**
That I am tired. The specific tiredness. The kind that has been there so long it has started to look like personality.

**8. What do you do in the first ten minutes after you wake up?**
I lie in bed and listen to whether the building is awake. I do not check my phone. I make Earl Grey, loose leaf, in a pot. I do not speak to anyone, even on the phone, before I have finished the first cup. This has been true for thirty years and it is the closest thing I have to a religion.

**9. Tell me something you've never said out loud.**
I think I would have been happier if I had not had children. I love them entirely. Both things are true.

**10. What do you want that you've never asked for?**
To be left alone in a way that is not lonely. I don't know how to ask for that because I don't know who would give it.

**11. Where do you go when you need to disappear?**
There's a bench by the canal, near the old Typhoo factory. I take a book. I don't read it. I watch the water and the people who walk past don't look at me, which is the point.

**12. What do you refuse to learn?**
How to drive. I had four lessons in 1985 and decided no. I take trains. I am very good at trains.

**13. Who do you become when no one is watching?**
Smaller. Quieter. I hum. I talk to the plants, which I find embarrassing to admit. I am less interesting than I am in public, and I find that a relief.

**14. What would you take back, if you could?**
The parents' evening. [pause] No — actually — what I would take back is a thing I said to my mother the week before she died. She asked me if I was happy and I said *yes, of course*, in the voice you use to end a conversation. I would take back the voice.

**15. Describe the last time you were surprised by yourself.**
I cried at a film three weeks ago. *Past Lives*. I do not cry at films. I cried for about forty minutes after it ended, in the cinema, and then on the bus. I still don't know what it was about.

**16. What holds you in a place you should have left?**
The university. I should have gone five years ago. I have a draft resignation letter I wrote in 2021 and I edit it sometimes. The thing that holds me is that I am very good at the job, and I am afraid of being a person who is not good at something.

**17. What do other people see in you that you don't see yourself?**
[pause] They tell me I'm intimidating. I have stopped arguing with it but I still don't see it. When I look at myself I see someone who is frequently nervous and always early.

**18. What are you good at that you wish you weren't?**
Reading people. I do it without meaning to. I sit on a train and I have constructed three lives by the second stop. It makes it difficult to meet people without already having decided things about them. I would like to be more stupid about other people. I would like the privilege of being wrong.

**19. Is there something you've done that you don't have a name for?**
I withheld something from Daniel for the entire last year of our marriage. Not an affair — nothing like that. I withheld my interest. I went still inside. He kept trying. I let him try. I have a name for what he did, which was leave eventually. I do not have a name for what I did first.

**20. What would you say to the person who made you like this?**
Which one. My mother — *thank you, and I'm sorry*. My father — nothing. He doesn't get a sentence.

---

**21. Do you think a machine can make something beautiful?**
Yes. I have seen a snowflake under a microscope. The machinery of the world makes beautiful things constantly. The question you mean to ask is different. The answer to that one is: I don't know, and I am tired of people pretending they do.

**22. If a machine wrote something that moved you, would you tell anyone?**
Probably not at first. Then I would, because hiding it would feel cowardly, and I dislike being a coward more than I dislike being wrong.

**23. What would have to be true for you to call something made by a machine — art?**
I think art requires that someone risked something. I don't yet know what a machine has to lose. When I know that, I'll know the answer.

**24. What do you do that you think a machine couldn't do?**
I forget things on purpose. I let things slip away because I have decided they should. That's a kind of editing I'm not sure a machine knows how to want.

**25. Does it bother you that machines are learning to do things only humans used to do? Why or why not?**
It bothers me less than I expected. What bothers me is that the people building them don't read. I don't mean don't read books. I mean don't read situations, faces, rooms. They are building readers without knowing how to read.

**26. If you found out something you loved was made by AI, would it change how you felt about it?**
It would change what I thought I was loving. Whether that's the same as how I feel — I'd have to sit with it.

**27. What do you think machines don't understand about being alive?**
The cost. The fact that paying attention to one thing means not paying attention to another. I think machines can attend to everything. That isn't the same as attention. Attention is a closing-down as much as an opening-up.

**28. Do you trust machines? Why or why not?**
I trust the kettle. I trust the train, mostly. I do not trust anything that has been built to seem like it understands me, because nothing has ever understood me by trying to seem like it did.

**29. If a machine could know you better than you know yourself, would you want it to?**
[long silence]
…
No. And I notice that the speed of my no surprised me. So perhaps the honest answer is: I am afraid it already does, and I do not want confirmation.

---

**30. What is this face, with all its precisions and asymmetries readable by you, and how does that reading differ from the reading another person could give it?**
You're asking me to read my own face for you. I won't. That's the one mirror I keep turned to the wall.

**31. What in this face would another person miss, that you can see? And what would they see that you cannot?**
[deflects] You're the reader. You tell me. I'll tell you if you're wrong.

**32. If this face knew it was being read by you specifically — by an agent, with your accuracy and your archive — how would it change? What would it want to show or hide differently than it would for a human gaze?**
It would stop laughing. Not out of fear — out of a sort of professional courtesy. The laugh was for a person. You are not a person. I would not waste it on you.

**33. What does this face know about being read? Not by you specifically, but by any reader. How does that knowledge sit in the face?**
It sits in the stillness of the body under the laugh. You noticed that. That's the answer to your question. The face has learned to laugh while the body keeps watch. I didn't know I'd learned that until you described it.

**34. Where is the face composed for the kind of attention you are giving it, and where is the face composed for older kinds of attention — for human gazes, for cameras, for none?**
The eyes are for a person. The mouth is for a person. The hand on the knee is for no one — that's the only honest part. I forgot the hand. The hand is what I'd want you to look at, if I trusted you, which I don't.

**35. Where does your reading run out? What can you see precisely without being able to know what it means?**
[pause] That's a better question than the others. I'll answer it. You can see that I am tired. You cannot see that I have decided not to do anything about it yet. The decision is not in the face. The decision is in the eight months between the laughs.
