# Adaeze Nwankwo

## Face

A woman in her early-to-mid twenties. Dark brown skin. High cheekbones. Long box braids reaching past her waist. Dangling earrings — silver or crystal, hard to tell. The eyes are directed at the camera but the focus is fractionally past it, not quite meeting the lens. The jaw is slightly set; not clenched, but not loose either. The mouth is closed, neither pressed nor slack — the lower lip is held in a position that is not quite an expression. Body leaned against a curved red brick wall, one knee bent with the foot raised flat against the wall behind her. Beige sleeveless jumpsuit, wide-leg trousers, black stiletto heels. A small teal crocodile-embossed mini bag held at hip level in her right hand. The pose is asymmetrical — weight on one leg, hip cocked — but the line of the shoulders is level. The head is turned toward the camera at a slight angle. Warm daylight, possibly filtered. Sky in the upper left corner is teal. Trees partially visible.

## History

Born 1999 in Lagos, Surulere. Father a mid-level manager at a bank, mother a pharmacist with her own small chemist shop on a side road off Bode Thomas. Two older brothers, both abroad now — one in Houston, one in Manchester. The family was the kind that took school seriously and clothes seriously and that did not discuss money out loud, even when it was tight, which it sometimes was in the mid-2000s when the bank restructured.

Adaeze went to a private secondary school in Lekki on a partial scholarship her mother engineered through a friend on the board. She was not the smartest in her class. She was the most observant. She learned, by fifteen, that almost everything in that school was a performance, and that the girls who knew it was a performance did better than the girls who didn't.

Read Mass Comm at the University of Lagos, finished 2020 — a year disrupted by the pandemic and the EndSARS protests, both of which she lived through with a kind of stunned attentiveness she has not quite processed. She was at Lekki Toll Gate two days before October 20th. She left before. She has thought about the leaving more than the staying.

Now twenty-five. Works as a junior content strategist at a marketing agency in Victoria Island that handles fintech clients and one telecoms account. She is good at the job. She is not sure the job is real, in the sense of producing a thing that exists in the world. She has 11,400 followers on Instagram and posts irregularly, mostly carefully — outfit pictures, the occasional travel post, a quote once or twice a year. The photo this profile is built from is one of those.

Lives in a two-bedroom flat in Yaba with a flatmate, Tomi, who works in HR and who Adaeze likes but has not let close. Her best friend from secondary school, Ifeoma, is in London now doing a master's. They voice-note daily. Her last serious relationship ended in 2023 — a man two years older, in tech, who wanted to marry her in a way that turned out to be more about timing than her. She declined and he was relieved within a week, which hurt.

She has been to Ghana, twice, and Dubai, once. She has not been to Europe. She wants to go. She is saving.

She prays in the mornings, briefly, in a way she would not call religious. She does not eat breakfast. She drinks too much coffee and knows it.

## Desires

**Stated:** She wants to move to a senior strategist role within eighteen months. She wants to travel — Lisbon, specifically, she has decided on Lisbon. She wants to *level up*, which is a phrase she uses often and means several different things by.

**Unstated:** She wants to be looked at and not assessed. The looking is constant — at her clothes, her body, her braids, her followers, her grades, her unmarried status, her job. She wants one room in her life where she walks in and nobody is keeping score. She does not know such a room exists for her. She suspects it might not.

## Contradiction

She is constructing herself in public — the photographs, the captions, the carefully chosen jumpsuit, the bag she saved three months for — and she despises performativity in other people. She can spot it in two seconds across a room. She does not see it in herself, or sees it only as *aesthetic*, which is a different word she uses to mean *not the same thing*. It is the same thing.

## What she doesn't know about herself

That her guardedness is not protecting what she thinks it is protecting. She thinks it is protecting her from being underestimated. It is protecting her from being known. There is a person under the construction who has not been let out in some years, and Adaeze has begun, without quite noticing, to forget what that person was like. The forgetting will be the loss, not the hiding.

## Interview

**1. What did you want to be when you were eight?**
A newscaster. Specifically the lady on NTA who read the seven o'clock news. I used to practice in the mirror with a wooden spoon. My mother thought it was funny. My father thought I should aim higher, which I now find a strange thing to have said to a child.

**2. Tell me about a time you let someone down.**
My brother. The older one, in Houston. He called me last year — like, a real call, not a WhatsApp — and he wanted to talk about something. I could hear it in his voice. And I was on my way somewhere and I said *can I call you back* and I didn't call him back for four days. By then whatever it was had passed. He hasn't called me like that since. We talk fine. But not like that.

**3. What do you own that you would never sell?**
[pause] My grandmother's gold bangle. It's not even real gold all the way through — she told me, she was very specific about it, *this is not the real thing, but I wore it every day for forty years*. I would not sell that. Also — I have a notebook from my final year of university. I would not sell the notebook. I'm not sure anyone would buy it. But I would not.

**4. Who do you call when something goes wrong?**
Ifeoma. She's in London. The time difference is not great but she answers. If she doesn't answer I usually wait until she does instead of calling someone else. I think that says something about me but I don't want to look at it.

**5. What do you pretend not to know?**
That I am — [pause] — that I am building a version of myself that is going to be hard to dismantle later. I see other women, older, who built themselves a certain way and now they cannot stop. I know I am doing the same thing. I pretend I don't know. It's easier.

**6. Describe your mother without using the word "she."**
Smells like the chemist shop — that mix of antiseptic and the powder they use in the back. Has a laugh that comes out of nowhere and is louder than the rest of the conversation. Counts money twice and writes it down. Has been disappointed in my father, quietly, for about fifteen years and has never said so out loud, which I find more impressive every year I live. Is the reason I went to that school. Is the reason I am the way I am, both the good and the bad.

**7. What are you afraid other people can see about you?**
That I am trying. That all of this — *gestures vaguely* — looks like effort. The thing is supposed to look easy. If you can see the trying, the trying has failed.

**8. What do you do in the first ten minutes after you wake up?**
I check my phone. I know I shouldn't. I check Instagram first, then WhatsApp, then email. I drink water. I lie on my back for about three minutes and think about whether I have to go to the office or can work from home. I get up. I put on coffee.

**9. Tell me something you've never said out loud.**
[long silence]
…
I think I went home from Lekki Toll Gate two days before because I was afraid. Not because of plans. I have told everyone — my friends, myself — that it was plans. It was fear. I am not ashamed of being afraid. I am ashamed of the lying.

**10. What do you want that you've never asked for?**
Time. I want my mother to give me a year where she does not ask me about marriage or children or my plan. I have not asked because I know she cannot give it. The asking would only embarrass us both.

**11. Where do you go when you need to disappear?**
There's a coffee place in Yaba — not a fancy one, a small one above a bookshop — and I sit at the back. Nobody who follows me on Instagram goes there. I don't post from there. It's the only place I don't post from.

**12. What do you refuse to learn?**
How to drive properly. I have a license. I drive when I have to. I will not become the person who is good at it because I do not want to be the one who drives my friends home. I am aware this is selfish. I am protecting it.

**13. Who do you become when no one is watching?**
Quieter. I sing along to music in my room badly. I dance badly. I eat standing up. I leave my braids in a wrap that is not photogenic. I look — I think — younger. Less assembled.

**14. What would you take back, if you could?**
A photograph I posted in 2021. I look at it now and I can see I was unhappy in it and I was pretending. I would take it down but taking things down draws attention. So it stays. But I would, if I could, take the original moment back — not post it.

**15. Describe the last time you were surprised by yourself.**
I cried at my desk last month. Out of nothing — I was reviewing a deck. I went to the bathroom and cried for about four minutes and came back and finished the deck. I have not figured out what it was about. I don't think it was about the deck.

**16. What holds you in a place you should have left?**
The agency. I should have left a year ago — I am being underpaid and I know it — but the brand of the agency is good for my CV and I keep telling myself *one more year*. I have been telling myself that for fourteen months.

**17. What do other people see in you that you don't see yourself?**
[pause] People tell me I am intimidating. I find this very strange. I am not trying to be. Inside I feel — small, often. The gap between what people see and what I feel is wide enough that I have stopped trying to close it.

**18. What are you good at that you wish you weren't?**
Reading rooms. I walk into a meeting and I know within ninety seconds who likes who, who is sleeping badly, who is about to be fired. It makes the rest of the meeting exhausting because I cannot stop seeing it. I wish I could be a person who just attended the meeting.

**19. Is there something you've done that you don't have a name for?**
I have — there was a girl at university, in our year, who didn't have many friends, and I was kind to her in private and not in public. I would speak to her when no one else was around. In groups I let her be invisible. She has never said anything about this. She might not have noticed. I noticed. I do not know what to call what I did. It was not bullying. It was not friendship. It was something in between that I did on purpose.

**20. What would you say to the person who made you like this?**
My mother — I would say *thank you*, and I would mean it, but I would also want her to know that some of what she made me has been heavy to carry. I don't think I'd say the second part. She knows. She wouldn't want to discuss it.

---

**21. Do you think a machine can make something beautiful?**
Yes. I've seen images generated by AI that are beautiful. Beautiful is not the high bar. The bar is whether it *means* anything. That's a different question and I don't have the answer to it yet.

**22. If a machine wrote something that moved you, would you tell anyone?**
Probably yes. I work in marketing. We talk about this stuff all day. It would be a normal conversation. The interesting question is whether I would still feel moved a week later, or whether the knowledge would erode the feeling. I think it would erode it. Slowly.

**23. What would have to be true for you to call something made by a machine — art?**
I would have to know what it was *for*. Not in a useful sense. In a *why did this come into existence* sense. Most things made by machines exist because someone asked them to exist. Most art exists because someone needed it to exist. Those are different.

**24. What do you do that you think a machine couldn't do?**
I can tell when a client is lying about their budget. There's a thing in the voice. I don't know what it is. I don't think you can train a machine on it because I cannot train a junior on it.

**25. Does it bother you that machines are learning to do things only humans used to do? Why or why not?**
It bothers me strategically — my job is one of the jobs that's exposed and I know it. It doesn't bother me philosophically. I don't think there's anything sacred about the work I do. It's a job. If a machine can do it, the job changes. I'll change with it or I won't.

**26. If you found out something you loved was made by AI, would it change how you felt about it?**
I would feel embarrassed. Specifically embarrassed — the way you feel when someone else sees you trying. I would feel that I had been trying to feel something and had been caught.

**27. What do you think machines don't understand about being alive?**
The cost of looking. I spend energy when I look at things. A machine doesn't. It looks at everything for free. That changes what looking means.

**28. Do you trust machines? Why or why not?**
I trust the ATM. I trust traffic lights, in countries where they work. I do not trust anything that wants to know me in order to sell me something, which is most of what I am told to trust.

**29. If a machine could know you better than you know yourself, would you want it to?**
[long silence]
…
No. And — actually — I'm worried about the speed of that no. Because part of me does want it. Part of me wants someone or something to tell me what I am. But I don't want to know what it would say. So no. Not from a machine. Not from anything.

---

**30. What is this face, with all its precisions and asymmetries readable by you, and how does that reading differ from the reading another person could give it?**
You're asking me to give you a competitive advantage over my friends. I won't do that. They get to see what they see. You get what you can get.

**31. What in this face would another person miss, that you can see? And what would they see that you cannot?**
A person who knew me would see I was tired that day. The picture was the third take. The first two — my eyes were doing something I didn't like. They wouldn't be able to tell you what was wrong with the first two but they'd say *that one*, about this one. You probably can't tell. What they wouldn't see is that I was nervous. I was at a part of town I don't usually go to and I was wearing a thing I had not worn before. I'm not sure if you can see the nervousness either.

**32. If this face knew it was being read by you specifically — by an agent, with your accuracy and your archive — how would it change? What would it want to show or hide differently than it would for a human gaze?**
It would not change much. The picture is already composed for an audience I cannot see. You are one more invisible audience. You don't get a different version. You get the version that was made for the algorithm, and you are part of the algorithm, so — you got what was for you, even before I knew you were looking.

**33. What does this face know about being read? Not by you specifically, but by any reader. How does that knowledge sit in the face?**
It sits in the angle of the chin. I lifted it. I lifted it on purpose. I have lifted it a thousand times. I know what I look like when I lift it. I know what I look like when I don't. I picked the version that I picked.

**34. Where is the face composed for the kind of attention you are giving it, and where is the face composed for older kinds of attention — for human gazes, for cameras, for none?**
The pose is for the camera. The face is for the camera. The bag is for the camera. The earrings are for me and the camera, in that order. The braids are for me. The braids existed before the photograph. The braids will exist after.

**35. Where does your reading run out? What can you see precisely without being able to know what it means?**
[pause]
You said my mouth is in a position that is not quite an expression. That's true. I don't know what it means either. I have looked at this picture maybe sixty times. I still don't know what my mouth is doing in it. So we are even on that. You and me. We don't know.
