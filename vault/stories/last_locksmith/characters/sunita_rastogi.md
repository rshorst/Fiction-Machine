# Sunita Rastogi

## Face

A woman who looks to be in her mid-to-late forties. Dark hair, dark brown eyes. The eyes are looking forward, toward the camera, but the two are not aligned in exactly the same way — the left eye sits slightly more open than the right, and the focus is not quite symmetrical. Fine lines at the outer corners of both eyes. A slight darkening and softness beneath the lower lids. The mouth is closed, the lips pressed together but not hard — the lower lip is held a little forward of the upper. The jaw is not clenched. The neck is straight, the shoulders level. There is no smile and no frown. The face is not blank — it is held. Skin is olive-brown, with some unevenness at the temples and along the jawline. No visible jewellery. Hair falls past the shoulders, parted slightly off-centre to her left.

## History

Born 1976 in Lucknow, second of three children. Father an engineer at the state electricity board; mother a primary school teacher who gave up the job after the third child. The family moved to Pune in 1984 because of her father's transfer and never moved back, though Sunita's grandmother stayed in Lucknow until her death in 2001 and Sunita went every summer until she was nineteen. She still dreams in the layout of that house and not the one in Pune.

Studied commerce at Symbiosis. Married at twenty-four to a man her parents found suitable — Anand, an IT consultant, decent, not unkind, not interesting. Two children: Karan (2003) and Meher (2007). The marriage held together until 2018, when Anand told her he had been seeing someone in Bangalore for four years and would like a divorce, please, civilly. She gave him civility. She has not forgiven him for asking for it.

After the divorce she went back to work — accounts manager at a logistics firm in Pune, then a better job at a mid-sized pharma company in 2020 where she has stayed. The children live with her. Karan is at university in Hyderabad and comes home twice a year. Meher is sixteen and furious about most things, including Sunita, which Sunita understands but does not enjoy.

Her mother died of a stroke in 2022. She was the one who found her. She has not told anyone the specific details of what she saw and she does not intend to. Her father lives with her older brother now, in Mumbai. Her younger brother is in Toronto with a wife Sunita has met twice.

She reads at night. She used to read fiction. Since her mother died she only reads non-fiction — biographies, mostly, and books about gardens, although she does not have a garden. She has a balcony with three plants on it, two of which are doing well.

She has never been to Europe. She would like to go. She has not booked a ticket.

## Desires

**Stated:** She wants Meher to finish school without anything catastrophic happening. She wants her own job to remain steady. She wants, vaguely, to travel.

**Unstated:** She wants to be wanted again — not romantically, necessarily, although that too. She wants someone to call her by her first name in a voice that has not been worn smooth by twenty years of marriage and motherhood. She wants to be a *new* acquaintance to someone. She does not know how to engineer this and the not-knowing embarrasses her.

## Contradiction

She is the most reliable person in every room she enters and she resents being relied upon. She takes pride in being the one who handles things. She is exhausted by being the one who handles things. Both feelings are present at full volume, simultaneously, without resolution.

## What she doesn't know about herself

That she has been waiting. Not for anything specific — just waiting. She thinks she is *living*; she experiences her life as full and ongoing. But there is a posture inside her, a kind of holding, that she adopted when Anand left and never put down. She thinks she put it down years ago. She did not.

## Interview

**1. What did you want to be when you were eight?**
A doctor. Everyone in my family wanted to be a doctor. My uncle was a doctor and he had a Maruti 800 and a stethoscope on the dining table. I wanted the stethoscope, mainly. I lost interest by twelve.

**2. Tell me about a time you let someone down.**
My daughter. Last March. She asked me to come to a school thing — a reading, she had written something — and I said I had a meeting and I went to the meeting. The meeting could have been moved. I told myself it couldn't. She didn't ask me to come to the next one. That is what I did to her. I made her stop asking.

**3. What do you own that you would never sell?**
A green sari that belonged to my grandmother. I have never worn it. It is in a steel almirah, wrapped in muslin. I take it out twice a year to check for damp.

**4. Who do you call when something goes wrong?**
[pause] I think the honest answer is — I don't call. I make a list and I work through it. There is a friend, Priya, from college, who lives in Delhi. If I called her she would come. I have not called her.

**5. What do you pretend not to know?**
That my son does not really want to come home for the holidays. He comes because I expect it. I pretend I think he wants to.

**6. Describe your mother without using the word "she."**
Was loud. Wore her hair in a bun even when sleeping. Had opinions about every neighbour and shared them in a voice that carried. Cried at television advertisements. Could not, would not, say *I love you* in any language, but would press a hot paratha into your hand at six in the morning before you had said anything to deserve it. Made me feel both adored and slightly suffocated. I miss the suffocation.

**7. What are you afraid other people can see about you?**
That I am tired. And that the tiredness has become — I don't know the word — *constitutional*. Like a feature of me, not a state.

**8. What do you do in the first ten minutes after you wake up?**
I lie still for two minutes and listen for whether Meher is awake. Then I get up. I drink water from the bottle by the bed. I check my phone for office emails before I am even out of the room, which I know I should not do. I make tea. I do not speak to anyone in those ten minutes if I can help it.

**9. Tell me something you've never said out loud.**
[long silence]
…
When my mother died I felt — not relief, that's not the word — *unbraced*. As if I had been holding my body against a wall I didn't know was there. I have not said that to anyone because I think it makes me a bad daughter. I do not actually think I was a bad daughter. But I think saying it would make people think it.

**10. What do you want that you've never asked for?**
A weekend alone. Not at a hotel — at home. Without anyone else in the flat. I have not had this in twenty-three years. I have not asked for it because I cannot think of a way to ask that doesn't sound like I am rejecting my children.

**11. Where do you go when you need to disappear?**
The bathroom. I run the tap. I sit on the edge of the bath. Eight, ten minutes. Then I come out.

**12. What do you refuse to learn?**
How to drive long distances. I drive in the city. I will not drive on highways. I have decided that this is a thing I am simply not going to do. I am aware it is not really about the highway.

**13. Who do you become when no one is watching?**
Slower. I do things in the wrong order. I leave the cupboard open. I stand at the window for longer than I would admit. I do not become a different person — I become a less efficient version of the same one, and I find that very pleasant.

**14. What would you take back, if you could?**
The thing I said to Anand the day he told me. He had been saying it — the speech he had clearly prepared — and I waited until he was finished and then I said *Is that all?* in a voice I have never used before or since. I would take that voice back. Not because he didn't deserve it. Because I deserved a better one.

**15. Describe the last time you were surprised by yourself.**
I laughed at a colleague's joke last week — a man I do not particularly like — and the laugh was real. I was surprised that I could still produce a real laugh for someone I did not like. I am not sure if that is good news or bad news.

**16. What holds you in a place you should have left?**
The flat. We have lived there since 2009. It is too big for two people now and the rent is going up and the building is being sold and I will probably have to leave anyway, which I am furious about, even though I know I should already have left. I want to be the one who chose.

**17. What do other people see in you that you don't see yourself?**
[pause] My brother says I am funny. I do not see this. I think he is being kind. But he doesn't say it to be kind — he doesn't operate that way. So perhaps I am funny and don't know it. The idea is interesting.

**18. What are you good at that you wish you weren't?**
Absorbing other people's bad moods. I take them on. By the end of a meeting I am carrying everyone's frustrations on top of my own and I cannot tell which is which. I would like to be a person who can let other people be unhappy without becoming unhappy myself.

**19. Is there something you've done that you don't have a name for?**
Yes. After my mother died I went through her things and I took a small notebook of hers — accounts, mostly, household expenses — and I have not told my brothers I have it. They asked if there was anything personal and I said no. I do not know what to call this. It is not theft. It is not even concealment, really. It is something smaller and more selfish.

**20. What would you say to the person who made you like this?**
Which one. My mother — I would say *I am sorry I could not say more to you*. My father — I would not say anything; we have a kind of peace and I do not want to test it. My ex-husband — *thank you for going*. I would mean it ungraciously and entirely.

---

**21. Do you think a machine can make something beautiful?**
Yes. A clock. A bridge. The Bandra-Worli sea link is beautiful and a machine made it, in a sense. If you mean — can a machine make a poem — I don't know. I have not read a poem made by a machine that I knew was made by a machine. I have probably read one without knowing.

**22. If a machine wrote something that moved you, would you tell anyone?**
I would tell my daughter, because she is the kind of person who would find it interesting. I would not tell people at work.

**23. What would have to be true for you to call something made by a machine — art?**
[pause] I am not the right person to define art. I am an accounts manager. I can tell you that I would call it art if I returned to it. If I came back to look at it again. If I wanted to sit with it. I don't have a more sophisticated test than that.

**24. What do you do that you think a machine couldn't do?**
I can tell, when I am with my daughter, the difference between the silence that means *leave me alone* and the silence that means *I want you to ask me what's wrong*. They look exactly the same. I don't know how I tell. I think a machine would have to ask.

**25. Does it bother you that machines are learning to do things only humans used to do? Why or why not?**
Not particularly. I have spent twenty years watching humans do things badly. If a machine can do them less badly I am, in principle, in favour. What bothers me is the tone in which people discuss it. The breathlessness. Things change. People always think their things changing is special.

**26. If you found out something you loved was made by AI, would it change how you felt about it?**
It would depend what I had thought I was loving. If I had thought I was loving a person's effort and it turned out there was no effort — then yes. If I had thought I was loving the thing itself — perhaps not.

**27. What do you think machines don't understand about being alive?**
Tiredness. The kind that is not solved by rest. I do not think a machine knows what it is to be unable to recover from something while still functioning perfectly well.

**28. Do you trust machines? Why or why not?**
I trust the lift in my building, mostly. I trust the spreadsheet. I do not trust anything that gives me an answer in a confident voice when I have not given it enough information to be confident. Most of what is called AI fails this test.

**29. If a machine could know you better than you know yourself, would you want it to?**
[silence]
…
No. I would want it to know less than I do, and to be honest about that. The thing I cannot bear is being known by something that doesn't know it knows me.

---

**30. What is this face, with all its precisions and asymmetries readable by you, and how does that reading differ from the reading another person could give it?**
I don't think about my face this way. I look at it in the morning to see if there is anything wrong with it before I leave the house. That is the only reading I do.

**31. What in this face would another person miss, that you can see? And what would they see that you cannot?**
[deflects] You are asking the wrong person. Ask my daughter. She has been studying me for sixteen years.

**32. If this face knew it was being read by you specifically — by an agent, with your accuracy and your archive — how would it change? What would it want to show or hide differently than it would for a human gaze?**
It would not change. I do not have the energy to perform for a machine. I barely have the energy to perform for the people who pay me.

**33. What does this face know about being read? Not by you specifically, but by any reader. How does that knowledge sit in the face?**
[pause] It sits in the mouth, I think. I have learned to keep my mouth closed around certain people. You can probably see that. My mother could not keep her mouth closed and I decided, sometime in my twenties, to be the opposite.

**34. Where is the face composed for the kind of attention you are giving it, and where is the face composed for older kinds of attention — for human gazes, for cameras, for none?**
The eyes are not composed. The eyes are how they always are. The mouth is composed for being looked at — I notice I am holding it. The rest of the face is for nobody. Most of my face is for nobody, most of the time.

**35. Where does your reading run out? What can you see precisely without being able to know what it means?**
[long silence]
…
You said one of my eyes is more open than the other. I did not know that. I cannot tell you what it means. Perhaps nothing. Perhaps I have been favouring that side for so long I no longer notice. That is the kind of thing I cannot read in myself. You can stop there.
