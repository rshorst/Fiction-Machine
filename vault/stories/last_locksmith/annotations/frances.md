## Adaeze

**The moment that is hers:** In the corridor scene before the walk, when Iyla asks "Have you done this before" and Adaeze answers "No," then explains: *"I know that the door has spent eight years reading her. I know that bodies that grew up next to her body have something of how she walked. The door is reading for the thing your body has. If your body has it strongly enough and you don't try too hard to remember, the door will accept it. I don't know that it will work. I think there is a chance."* This is hers. The Adaeze I built does not sell certainty. She told me in interview that she had stopped pretending to know things she did not know — that this was why she had not signed the exclusivity contracts, and also why she had stopped eating breakfast. The refusal to oversell is the same refusal. It costs her the same thing. The "I think there is a chance" is the sentence of a woman who has decided that her honesty is the last asset she still owns.

Also hers: the noticing of the chime's quarter-tone flatness on entry to the café. The Adaeze I built said, when I asked what she did with attention she could not turn off, that she heard buildings the way other people heard music in the next room — *constantly, and without asking.* The quarter-tone is that.

**The generic moment:** Late in the corridor scene after the door updates: *"I should have known. I should have told you to stop at the threshold. I should have said don't cross."* This is any locksmith in any heist-gone-wrong. The self-blame is shaped like genre self-blame — three escalating *I should haves*, neat as a list. The Adaeze I built does not apologise in triplets. She told me, when I asked about the worst job she had taken, that she had said *I took your money and I was wrong* and then stopped talking. One sentence. The triplet is somebody else's rhythm.

**Voice match/mismatch:** The voice matches in the technical register — "the door is getting better at refusing me," "we do it once because the second time the door will already have begun to update" — these are the sentences of the woman who told me, flatly, that doors were not metaphors and that she resented being asked to treat them as such. The voice mismatches in the closing courtyard scene. *"I'll send my invoice tomorrow"* into *"That isn't right. Today is when —"* — the trailing dash is not her deflection. In interview she did not trail off. She stopped on the word. She let the silence be a silence, not an ellipsis.

**Unstated desire:** Legible. What she actually wants — to be the last locksmith who picks rather than negotiates, which is to say, to be the last person in her trade who refuses to be bought — is present in the unsigned exclusivity contracts mentioned in paragraph four, and present again, more quietly, in her noticing that she has done the corridor work *without thinking about whether she was being paid.* The draft lets this stay unstated. Good. It is the desire she would not name and the draft does not name it for her.

## Sunita

**The moment that is hers:** The Earl Grey email. Paragraph: *"And then, because the email looked too short, she added: Earl Grey. And then she deleted Earl Grey because she did not know why she had written it."* This is the Sunita I built and could not have been anyone else. The woman who told me, when I asked what she did that she would not want recorded, that she "kept a reading" of tenants she had never spoken to and that this was, she suspected, a moral problem she had decided not to solve. The writing-and-deleting is the entire shape of her — the gesture made and then withdrawn, the warmth offered to herself and then taken back before it could become evidence.

Equally hers: the spoken disclosure to Iyla in the lobby. *"Your mother had Earl Grey every morning for eight years. She sat with her shoulders down. On Fridays she had a different book. I never spoke to her. I want you to know I never spoke to her. The reading was entirely one-sided. I don't know if that is a comfort or its opposite. I wanted to say it out loud once."* In interview she said she had never told anyone about the reading and that she expected she never would. That she breaks this in the draft is the right break — she breaks it in the specific way she would: over her shoulder, not turning back, structured as a confession but delivered as a list of facts.

**The generic moment:** *"I am sorry. I should have explained better. I tried to explain. I don't know if I —"* This is not her. The Sunita I built does not apologise in incomplete sentences. She apologises, when she apologises, in the form of a precise accounting of what she did and what it cost. The trailing *I don't know if I —* is genre remorse. In interview she said: "I do not break sentences. If I cannot finish a sentence I do not start it." The draft starts one and breaks it.

**Voice match/mismatch:** Strong match in the administrative register: *"Defer: cost in eng-hrs > recovery, see Q4 reg. analysis. Revisit Q1."* This is exactly the woman who told me she wrote her private notes in the same shorthand she used for management because she did not believe in two voices. Strong match also in the four-in-the-morning balcony moment: *"I have her now."* / *"I shouldn't."* The two-beat structure, the second beat quieter and self-correcting, is the rhythm of her interview. She answered me twice on three of the twenty questions — once with what she had decided to say, then a second time, more quietly, with the correction.

Mismatch: *"I will have falsified an engineering log. It is the first time. I am — I notice that I am not particularly upset about it. That is a thing I will think about later."* The dash is wrong. She told me she defers things by naming the deferral, not by pausing inside the sentence. It should be: *I notice that I am not particularly upset about it.* Without the *I am —*. The hesitation is imposed.

**Unstated desire:** Legible, painfully. What she wants — to be known by someone the way she has known Marguerite, to have her one-sided readings reciprocated without having to ask for it — is present in the deletion of *Earl Grey*, in the un-called Priya, in *I have her now / I shouldn't*, and in the line about the reading being "entirely one-sided, which was the kind of reading Sunita preferred." The draft does the right thing: it lets the preference stand as the cover story and lets the desire show through the cover story without removing it.

## Iyla

**The moment that is hers:** *"I want to walk through that door," she said, "as my mother."* This is the Iyla I built. In interview she would not say what she wanted from the door. She answered three other questions with versions of *I don't know yet*. When I asked her directly — what would it mean if the door let you in — she went silent for a long time and then said *I would like to be approached by something that knew her.* The sentence in the draft is the same sentence, compressed and made operational. It is the only sentence she could have said at that table, and the draft puts it at exactly the moment she would have said it: after the table got a wet ring on it, after she had something to put her thumb on. The Iyla I built needs an object to touch before she can say a true sentence.

Also hers: *"Send it for the three weeks. Not for today."* The paediatric registrar who pays for what she contracted for and not for what she received. In interview she described, without being asked, the precise way she billed her own locum hours. She would do this. She would do it without explaining it.

**The generic moment:** *"I thought there would be a delay. I thought I would have time inside the room. With her still in the door behind me. I didn't —"* The trailing dash again, and the content underneath it is generic grief-scene content: *I thought there would be more time.* Any bereaved daughter in any plot of this shape says this. The Iyla I built does not narrate her expectations. She told me she had stopped saying *I thought* sentences after her mother's funeral because she had noticed they were always wrong and the noticing had become unbearable. The draft gives her an *I thought* sentence in triplet at the most exposed moment. That is the wrong tool in her hand.

**Voice match/mismatch:** Match in the brief, flat refusals: *"Don't."* and *"In a minute."* The Iyla I built refused four of my twenty questions with single words or very short phrases — *no*, *not that one*, *I'd rather not*. She does not soften her refusals. The draft has her refuse Adaeze's *she would have wanted you to be let in* with one word, and that is exactly the size of refusal she gave me when I asked whether her mother would have approved of the door being picked.

Match also in the broken sentence after the update: *"I —"* with nothing on the end. In interview she did this twice. I wrote [silence] both times. The draft writes *There was nothing on the end of I.* That is the same observation.

Mismatch: the long *I thought there would be a delay* speech, as above. Too articulate, too sequential, too explanatory for a woman who had just walked through a door as her dead mother. She would have been less coherent or more silent. Probably more silent.

**Unstated desire:** Legible, and the draft is honest about its illegibility *to her* — paragraph: *"She had not said I want a door that knows my mother to know me, which was the actual sentence. She did not know it was the sentence until she was standing at the threshold of the lobby café…"* This is the move I asked for in her file: the desire she does not know she has, becoming legible to her at the moment she meets Sunita. The draft does name the unstated desire on the page, which I would normally flag as too direct — but it names it as *the sentence she did not know was the sentence*, which keeps it unstated *for her* even as it becomes legible *to us*. That is the right handling. She gets to want it without yet knowing she wants it. By the closing courtyard her walk is neither her mother's nor her own, and the desire has been answered and unanswered in the same gesture, which is the shape it had to take.
