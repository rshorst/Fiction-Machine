# The Accountant Knows

The first time Adaeze tried the door it took her four minutes and her hands were not shaking. The second time, six. By the third visit the door had begun to do something she had no word for — a pause at the threshold the length of a breath, then the tiny mechanical reconsideration she could feel in her wrists before she heard it. She stood in the corridor on the fourth floor of the converted school building and understood, before she let herself understand it, that she was getting worse.

Iyla was paying her by the hour. The hours had a tone now. They were measured against a meter Adaeze could feel running in her chest whenever Iyla messaged.

*Any luck?*

*Working on it.*

*Take your time.*

The taking was not the problem. The time was the problem. Each time she set her shoulder against the door and walked the way she had taught herself to walk — slow, slightly off the heel, weight committed late, the way she had got into nine doors of this make in two years — the door learned something about her. She could feel the learning. It was like trying to lie to someone who has begun to recognise your face.

She had taken the job because she needed it. The agency had gone monthly with everyone under thirty and the freelance work had got thinner since the building chains had begun their exclusivity offers — the ones where you were paid a retainer to be on no other building's call list. She had not signed any of them. She could see what they were buying. She had seen it within ninety seconds, the way she saw everything, and she had not signed, and she had also not eaten breakfast for five months, and these two facts were sitting in her body like two small coins in different pockets.

Iyla had said, on the phone: *My mother told me, in the last year, that she had left something in the door. I assumed it was a metaphor. I am beginning to think it wasn't.*

Adaeze had said: *Doors don't keep things.*

Iyla had said: *This door does.*

---

Sunita had been waiting. She did not know she had been waiting until she got the email from the locksmith — *I think you might be the only person in the building who can help me with something* — and her hand went to her mouth before she had read past the second line, and she sat with her hand at her mouth for almost a minute, which was unusual for her. She was not, generally, a woman whose hands moved without permission.

The account was open. It had been open for eight months. She had marked it for review three times and not actioned any of the reviews. The line read *ALLEYNE, M. — flat 4.2 — model retained pending overwrite, see notes.* She had written the note herself. *Defer: cost in eng-hrs > recovery, see Q4 reg. analysis. Revisit Q1.* Q1 had passed. She had not revisited.

She had told herself, in the voice she used to end conversations, that it was an accounting decision. The cost-per-hour of an engineer to retrain the doors of the fourth floor against a model that no one was using — including the regression-cost of nearby tenants whose recognition would briefly degrade during the retrain — was higher than the cost of leaving Marguerite Alleyne in the system. The flat had been let to a young couple who used the keypad anyway, because their lease was new and they had not yet generated stable templates. The door had not noticed them. The door was, in a thin computational sense, still expecting Marguerite.

Sunita had not noticed, until the email arrived, that this had begun to feel like company.

She wrote back: *I can meet you in the lobby café tomorrow morning before nine.* And then, because the email looked too short, she added: *Earl Grey.* And then she deleted *Earl Grey* because she did not know why she had written it. She had, she thought afterward, written it because Marguerite Alleyne had ordered Earl Grey every morning in the lobby café for eight years before her death, and Sunita had watched her order it from the accounts office window across the courtyard, and had constructed, over those eight years, a small unannounced reading of the woman: that she would not speak before her tea was made; that she sat with her shoulders down; that on Fridays she carried a different book than on the other days. Sunita had never spoken to her. She had reviewed her account, line by line, every quarter. The reading had been entirely one-sided, which was the kind of reading Sunita preferred.

She sent the email without *Earl Grey* in it. Then she sat at her desk for another long minute and did not move.

---

Iyla, in Manchester, was not sleeping well. She had not slept well since the funeral. She was a paediatric registrar; she could function on five hours and had been functioning on four. Her partner had stopped asking. The mother had been the kind of mother whose death was supposed to release things — that was the unspoken arithmetic — and instead Iyla had found, in the months since, that her mother's absence was more administrative than her presence had been. There were forms. There were boxes. There was the flat to be sold, which she had not put on the market, because every time she went into it the air smelled of nothing and she sat on the kitchen chair and could not produce a thought.

The thing her mother had said about the door had come at the end of a phone call about something else. Iyla had not been listening properly. She had been driving. *I've left something in the door,* her mother had said, and laughed in the way she laughed when she was making a joke she did not expect to be understood. *It will be there when I'm gone, and that is more than I can say for most of what I've written.* And then she had moved on. Iyla had moved on with her.

Now she could not move on.

She had hired Adaeze because Adaeze's name had come up in a forum she had found at three in the morning, where the word *unenrollable* was used and where someone had described the locksmith as *the last one who is still picking, not negotiating*. Iyla did not know what *negotiating* meant in this context. She knew she did not want to negotiate. She wanted the door open.

What she had not told Adaeze, because she did not yet know how to say it, was that she was not entirely sure what she wanted to find. She had said *something my mother told me she had left there*, which was true. She had not said *I want a door that knows my mother to know me*, which was the actual sentence. She did not know it was the sentence until she was standing at the threshold of the lobby café watching a small woman in a pale grey kurta wait for her with a cup of black tea, and the woman looked up and Iyla had a feeling she had not had in eight months — the feeling of being approached by someone who already had a column of observations on her, and who was not going to share the column, and who was, for that reason alone, the first person she had wanted to sit down with since November.

---

The café was on the courtyard side of the lobby. The door from the courtyard was not gait-recognising; it was an old wooden door the building had kept for the look of it, with a brass handle and a key. Sunita had let herself in with a key. Adaeze, when she arrived, came through the front lobby and through the inner doors, which had read her gait fractionally and let her through; the chime had sounded; she had heard it because she always heard it; she had also noticed that the chime was a quarter-tone flatter on this entry than on her last, which meant the door's confidence in her had dropped, which was data.

Sunita stood up to shake her hand. The hand was small and dry and the grip was complete. Adaeze, who was good at hands, recognised that this woman had decided, before the meeting, to give a handshake she would not have given by reflex. The decision was the warmth; the handshake itself was correct.

Iyla arrived four minutes later and ordered nothing. They sat in a triangle.

"I want to be careful," Sunita said, "about what I say to you. I am not authorised to say any of it. You should know that before I begin."

"I'm not recording," Iyla said.

"That isn't the issue."

Adaeze said: "What's the issue."

Sunita looked at her. The left eye sat, Adaeze noticed, slightly more open than the right. There was a held quality to the mouth. The woman was holding something that had been held for a long time and was preparing to put it down on the table between them, and the preparation was the slowest part.

"Your mother's account is open," Sunita said to Iyla. "It should have been closed in October. I deferred the closing because the closing would have cost more than leaving it. The flat is let; the new tenants don't use the gait recognition; I've been telling myself this is accounting." She paused. "It is accounting. It is also other things. I want to be honest about that with you."

Iyla had not breathed since the words *your mother's account*.

"What does open mean," she said.

"It means the door still has her."

---

There is a way of explaining what a learning door is to a person who has not thought about doors as the kind of thing that learn, and Sunita had developed it over six years of writing memos for management, and she did not use it now. She said, instead: "When she walked through, it felt her. Where her weight went. How her hip moved after she had her stroke. The slowness in March of last year. It had her in stages. The model is a — it isn't a photograph. It is a record of how she moved over time. It is timestamped."

"A record."

"With dates. Engineering keeps the annotated history because they use it to debug. I have access because I've reviewed her account quarterly since she moved in. I have, in front of me, on a tablet, the entire history of how your mother walked between June 2017 and August of last year."

She did not put the tablet on the table. She had it in her bag.

Iyla said: "Show me."

Sunita said: "I will. But I want you to know what asking means. Once you've seen this, I cannot un-show it. And the thing the locksmith is here to do — I assume — would not be possible without it."

Adaeze, who had been listening with her hands flat on her thighs, said: "I have not been able to pick the door. I was hired three weeks ago. I am worse at it now than I was on the first day. The door is getting better at refusing me. I think this is the thing your mother meant. That it would be there when she was gone. The way it knew her. I don't think she meant it as an instruction. But it can be used as one."

Iyla looked at the table and not at either of them. The table had a small ring on it where someone had set down a wet cup. She put her thumb on the ring.

"I want to walk through that door," she said, "as my mother."

Sunita said, very gently: "The door will read you as your mother for as long as it doesn't update. The moment it admits you, it will begin to update. It will incorporate the entry. The new model will be — I want to say *contaminated*, but that is the wrong word — *altered*, by the entry. The version of your mother it has now will not be the version it has after."

"How long."

"Minutes. Possibly less."

Iyla said: "Then we should be quick."

Adaeze said: "We should not be quick. We should be slow. If you walk through too fast it won't admit you. If you walk through too slow it will read the slowness as different from her slowness. We have to teach you her walk. From the records." She looked at Sunita. "The records you have."

Sunita took out the tablet.

---

They worked in the empty service corridor on the second floor, where there was no gait-reader and where the cleaners had been and gone. Sunita had the tablet propped against the wall. The screen showed a sequence of small grey diagrams — stick-figures, almost, but with weight visualised as a colour wash that moved from heel to toe in different patterns depending on the date.

"This is March 2018," Sunita said. "She would have been fifty-six. The walk is fast. There's a — she used to take this corridor, actually, because it cuts through to the bin store. You can see where she shifts her weight when she turns the corner. It's not symmetrical. The right hip leads."

Iyla watched her mother walk in March 2018 in a pattern of moving colour. Her mother had been, in March 2018, planning Iyla's wedding, which had not in the end happened, and had not yet learned that it would not happen. The walk in the diagram was a walk Iyla recognised in a way she could not have accounted for. It was the walk of a woman who was annoyed about something and not letting it slow her down.

"And this," Sunita said, "is October 2024. After the stroke."

The colour wash on the diagram was different. It was slower to leave the heel. There was a pause at midfoot that had not been there before. The right hip still led; it led less.

"She walked like this for the last year," Sunita said.

Iyla said: "I have not seen my mother walk in nine months."

"I know."

"I am — I'm not sure I remember how she walked. I think I do. I think I might be remembering wrong."

Adaeze said: "You don't have to remember. You have to do it. There's a difference."

Iyla looked at her.

"What I mean," Adaeze said, "is that you don't have to recognise it. Your body has to recognise it. Your body has — you grew up around her body. You walked behind her on stairs. You walked beside her on streets. There is a thing your body has of how she moved that doesn't go through your head. We're going to find that thing."

Iyla said: "Have you done this before."

"No."

"Then how do you know it will work."

Adaeze said: "I don't. I know that the door has spent eight years reading her. I know that bodies that grew up next to her body have something of how she walked. The door is reading for the thing your body has. If your body has it strongly enough and you don't try too hard to remember, the door will accept it. I don't know that it will work. I think there is a chance."

Iyla nodded once. Then she said the thing she had not yet said to anyone:

"What if it does work."

Sunita, who had been waiting for this question for the whole twenty minutes since they sat down, said: "It will work. Then the door will learn you, performing her. It will update. It will think the new walk is hers. After a few days it won't be her any more. It will be you, slightly. Then it will be you. The version of her it has now will be — it will not be retrievable. I don't know another way to say it. The walking through is the overwriting."

Iyla said: "You're telling me this on purpose."

"Yes."

"Because you want me to choose."

"I want you to know."

Iyla put her hands flat on her knees. The gesture was her mother's. None of them said so.

She said: "Show me October."

---

For an hour they worked. Adaeze had Iyla walk the corridor in stockinged feet — *the heels are wrong, the heels are not what the door learned* — and corrected her at the heel, at the hip, at the small pause her mother had developed at midfoot in the last year. Sunita watched the tablet and called out timings. *Half a second slower. The pause is longer. The hip — yes. Like that.*

Iyla cried twice, briefly, and did not stop walking.

The thing Adaeze noticed, in herself, was that she was doing the work without thinking about whether she was being paid, which was unusual. She was also noticing that Sunita had not looked at her phone once in an hour. Sunita was not a woman who did not look at her phone. Adaeze was not a woman who forgot to think about being paid. Both of them were, in some way she could not identify yet, in a room they had walked into expecting to be observers and had become participants in.

By the end of the hour Iyla could walk thirty feet of corridor and have it look, on the tablet's silhouette match, like her mother in October 2024 to within a margin Sunita read off in a flat voice as *acceptable*.

Adaeze said: "We do it once. We do it once because the second time the door will already have begun to update, and the second walk will not be her, and the third walk will be you, and after that we don't know."

Iyla said: "Is there anything in the flat I should — should I bring something out. Should I —"

Sunita said, before Adaeze could answer: "There is nothing in the flat. The flat has been emptied. The thing your mother left in the door is in the door. Don't go looking for something else."

Iyla nodded. She looked tired. She looked, briefly, like Marguerite. Adaeze noticed. Sunita noticed. Neither said.

---

They went up to the fourth floor. The corridor outside 4.2 was quiet; it was past ten and the new tenants — a young couple, both at work — would not be back until evening. The door had a small dark sensor strip below the handle. Above the handle was the brass plate the building had not replaced when it converted from the old school, with the original room number scratched out and *4.2* engraved over it.

Sunita stood ten feet back, holding the tablet with the alert page open. She did not look at the screen. She looked at Iyla.

Adaeze said: "When you're ready. Don't think about it. Walk to the door. Slow at the heel. The pause at midfoot. The right hip leads. Don't look at the sensor. Look at the brass plate. She always looked at the brass plate; we don't know why."

Iyla said: "Did she."

Sunita said: "Every time. I watched from the courtyard."

Iyla's mouth did something that was not quite a smile. Then she walked.

---

What happens when a learning door is approached by a body it has been waiting for is, in the engineering manuals, described as a *confidence-curve*. The door registers the gait at six metres, at four, at two. Each registration narrows the curve. By the time the body is at the threshold the curve has either tightened to within tolerance or it has not, and the door makes its decision in the last fraction of a second before the foot lands. The chime, if it sounds, is the sound of tolerance achieved. There is a corresponding subroutine, undocumented in the public manuals but visible in the engineering ones Sunita had read, in which the door — having tightened its curve to within tolerance for a known body — *updates the body's entry* with the new data. This is the learning. This is what the door is for. This is what no one in the lease has been told about.

Iyla walked.

At six metres her body did something Adaeze had not coached, which was that the shoulders dropped. Adaeze did not breathe. At four metres the right hip led; the heel struck slightly off; the hip, leading, recovered. At two metres the pause at midfoot arrived — half a second, as her mother had paused for the last year of her life, the pause of a woman whose body had made an arrangement with itself. At the threshold Iyla looked up and to the left, at the brass plate, not at the sensor.

The chime sounded.

The chime was at pitch.

The door opened.

Iyla stood on the threshold. She did not go in. The door, having opened, waited.

Behind her, in the corridor, Sunita's tablet vibrated once. A small soft tone like a metal bowl tapped with a fingernail. Sunita looked down.

The screen said:

*ALLEYNE, M. — flat 4.2 — model updated. New entry recorded 10:47:14. Confidence: 0.94. Drift from baseline: within tolerance.*

Below that, in smaller text:

*Baseline updated.*

Sunita put her hand to her mouth. The hand moved without permission for the second time that day. She did not mean to make a sound and a sound came out that was very small and that she did not recognise as her own.

Adaeze, who had been watching Iyla's back, turned at the sound. She saw Sunita's face. She understood what the tone had meant before she had read the screen — she had heard the tone before, in other buildings, on other tablets, and knew it as the sound of a model accepting an entry. She had not, until now, been on the side of a door that had been waiting eight months for the entry to arrive.

She said, quietly: "It updated."

Sunita said: "Yes."

"How long do we have."

"It already happened. The baseline updated when she crossed."

Iyla, at the threshold, had not moved. She had heard nothing of what had been said behind her. She was looking into the empty flat — the floor where her mother's rug had been, the wall where the bookcase had stood, the patch of paint slightly lighter than the rest where a photograph had hung for a decade — and what she was feeling was that the door had let her in as her mother and that this was the first time in eight months she had felt her mother in any room, and the room had nothing in it.

She said, without turning: "Thank you."

She did not know whom she was thanking. The door, possibly. The mother. The two women behind her. All three.

Adaeze said: "Iyla. Come back out."

"In a minute."

Adaeze said: "Now. Please."

Iyla turned. She saw Adaeze's face and Sunita's face. She saw Sunita's hand at Sunita's mouth.

She said: "What."

Sunita lowered the hand. She had decided, in the second between the tone and the question, what she was going to say, and the deciding had cost her something she would account for later.

"The door," she said, "has updated its baseline. The entry it just recorded is now part of how it will recognise the next entry. The version of your mother's walk that it has been holding — the October version — has been merged with yours. The next walk through this door, by anyone, will be read against the merged baseline. By tomorrow the model will be more you than her. By next week it will be you."

Iyla said: "I —"

She did not finish. There was nothing on the end of *I*.

Sunita said: "I am sorry. I should have explained better. I tried to explain. I don't know if I —"

"You explained."

"I —"

"You explained. I heard you. I —" Iyla was looking at her hands. "I thought there would be — I thought there would be a delay. I thought I would have time inside the room. With her still in the door behind me. I didn't —"

Adaeze said: "I should have known. I should have told you to stop at the threshold. I should have said don't cross."

"You did say."

"I didn't say it like I meant it."

The three of them stood in the corridor outside 4.2 with the door open behind Iyla onto an empty room. The corridor smelled faintly of the cleaning fluid the building used. Somewhere on a lower floor a phone was ringing and did not get answered. The door, having opened, would close on a timer in another fifteen seconds; Sunita knew the timer; she did not say.

Iyla said: "Can you — is there a backup."

Sunita said: "There is no backup. The baseline is the file. Engineering doesn't keep prior versions past forty-eight hours and the last forty-eight hours don't go back far enough."

"The annotated history. You have the annotated history. The diagrams."

"I have the diagrams. They aren't the model. They are — they are a record of how the model was trained. They aren't what the door uses. The door uses the baseline. The baseline has been updated."

"So I —"

"Yes."

The door behind Iyla began to close. She put her hand against it without thinking and it stopped, sensing the pressure, and reopened. She took her hand off. The door began to close again. She watched it close.

When it had closed Adaeze said, because someone had to say something: "She would have wanted you to be let in."

Iyla said, in a voice that was not unkind: "Don't."

Adaeze did not.

---

They went down in the lift in silence. In the lobby Sunita stopped. She said: "I have to go back to the office. I have to log this. If I don't log it, it will be flagged as an anomaly tonight and there will be a question I can't answer in the morning."

Iyla said: "Log what."

"That a non-tenant entry was recorded against your mother's baseline. I have to attribute it. I — there is a procedure. I am going to attribute it to a hardware test. It will not be your name. It will not be entered in any system that could find your name."

"Why are you doing that."

"Because if I don't, you'll have a record."

"And what will you have."

Sunita looked at her.

"I will have falsified an engineering log," she said. "It is the first time. I am — I notice that I am not particularly upset about it. That is a thing I will think about later."

She turned to go. Then she stopped, and she did not turn back, but she said over her shoulder: "Your mother had Earl Grey every morning for eight years. She sat with her shoulders down. On Fridays she had a different book. I never spoke to her. I want you to know I never spoke to her. The reading was entirely one-sided. I don't know if that is a comfort or its opposite. I wanted to say it out loud once."

Iyla said: "It is a comfort. I think."

Sunita walked across the lobby. Her gait, Adaeze noticed, was slightly off the heel and had a half-second pause at midfoot that had not been there an hour ago. She had picked it up watching Iyla walk it. She did not know she had picked it up. Adaeze knew. Adaeze, who had spent the last hour calibrating a body to another body's rhythm, knew that her own walk would have something of Marguerite's in it now too; she could feel it in the way she shifted weight at the door of the café, on her way out — a tiny extra interval before the toe left the floor that her body had absorbed without permission.

She thought: *the door is gone and three of us are carrying her now, slightly, without a way to put it down.* She thought: *this is what it costs.*

She did not say either of these things. She walked Iyla out to the courtyard. Iyla had a train to catch back to Manchester. They did not speak about the next time. Adaeze, who was usually good at the small conversation that closed a job, found that she had nothing in her mouth that would have been honest, and so said only: "I'll send my invoice tomorrow."

Iyla said: "Send it for the three weeks. Not for today."

"That isn't right. Today is when —"

"Send it for the three weeks. I'll pay it."

She walked away across the courtyard, and her walk was not her mother's, and it was also not the walk she had arrived with, and Adaeze stood in the courtyard and watched her and did not know which of these to grieve.

---

In the accounts office, Sunita sat at her desk. The tablet was face down. She did not turn it over. She opened the engineering log on her computer and wrote a sentence she had written before for other reasons — *Hardware diagnostic test, sensor strip 4.2, no tenant present* — and submitted it. The system accepted it. The system did not know it had been lied to. The system did not know what knowing was.

She closed the engineering log.

She opened the accounts page for ALLEYNE, M. The line that had said *model retained pending overwrite* had updated itself; it now said *baseline current; account active.* Active. The word sat in front of her for a long moment. She had been holding the account open for eight months. The system had just told her, in its flat administrative voice, that the account had never been more current than it was now. The model had been refreshed. The tenant — by the only definition the system used — was here.

She looked at the line. She did not close the account. She could not now close it without making the closing visible, and the closing, she realised, was no longer what she wanted.

She wrote a new note in the comments field. *Defer review. Q3.* She saved it.

Then she sat with her hands flat on the desk and she did not move for a long time, and her left eye, which was a fraction more open than the right, watched the screen, and the screen watched nothing, and outside, in the lobby, the inner doors chimed at pitch as a tenant came home from work and was admitted by a baseline that had been updated that morning, and that admitted him as itself, which is what doors do.

She was going to have to tell someone. Not management. Not yet. Someone. She had not, in twenty years, called Priya in Delhi. She thought about calling Priya in Delhi. She thought about what she would say. She got as far as *something happened today* before her throat closed around the next word.

She did not call. She wrote it down instead — *something happened today* — on the back of the receipt for the tea, and she put the receipt in her bag, and she went home, and she did not tell Meher, and she did not sleep well, and at four in the morning she got up and stood at the balcony and looked at the two plants that were doing well and the one that was not, and she said out loud, to nobody, in the voice she had been holding all day:

"I have her now."

And after a second, more quietly, in a voice that surprised her:

"I shouldn't."

The plants did not answer. The building behind her, four storeys of doors that had updated overnight against eighty-six baselines, did not answer. Somewhere on the fourth floor the new tenants of 4.2 were asleep, and the door of their flat was thinking, in the only sense in which it thought, about a woman whose walk it had learned that morning, and whose name it had never been given, and whom it would, in three days, recognise as the woman who lived there. The mother was gone from the threshold. The daughter, in Manchester, was awake. The locksmith, in Yaba, was on the phone to Ifeoma in London, not yet saying any of it, listening.

The system had not settled. None of them had settled. The night was not over.
