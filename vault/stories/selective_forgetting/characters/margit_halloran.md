# Margit Halloran

## Face
A woman in her late fifties or early sixties, leaning forward onto a railing — both forearms on it, weight tipped toward the camera. White long-sleeved shirt. Glasses with thin frames. Hair brushed back from the forehead, going grey at the temples but still mostly dark. The mouth is doing something almost like a smile but it stops before it commits — the corners lifted but the lips closed. She is looking past the camera slightly, not into it. The eyes behind the glasses are doing the work of evaluating something. There is skin loosening at the underside of the jaw and small lines fanning from the outer corners of the eyes. The forward lean reads as openness but she is also bracing on the railing, which keeps her at a controlled distance from whoever is taking the photograph. There is a tiredness at the lower lids that the composed face does not erase.

## History
Born 1964 in Duluth, Minnesota, to an Irish-American father (longshoreman, then dock supervisor) and a Hungarian mother (immigrated 1957, post-revolution, worked as a seamstress and later as a hotel housekeeper). Margit was the elder of two; her brother Peter was born in 1968 and died in 1991 of an overdose in a motel in Bemidji. She has not said his name out loud in seven years.

She left Duluth at eighteen for the University of Minnesota. Studied library science, then comparative literature. Married a man named Donal in 1987 because he was kind and because her mother was dying and she wanted her mother to see her in a wedding dress. The marriage lasted nine years. No children, by choice that was never quite spoken between them. She moved to Madison, Wisconsin, in 1996 for a job at the university library and stayed.

She is now Head of Special Collections at a midsized private university in Wisconsin. She has worked there for twenty-two years. She knows where every misfiled letter from the McCarthy era is in the archive. She has a cat named Béla, named after her uncle, not the composer. She reads Hungarian poetry in translation because her mother refused to teach her the language, and she has never forgiven her mother for that, which is a strange thing to hold against a dead woman.

She had a six-year affair, ending in 2019, with a married man who taught medieval history. She ended it. He didn't want her to. She has not dated since.

Her father is still alive, ninety-one, in assisted living in Duluth. She visits four times a year and calls every Sunday at 4 p.m. He always asks if she is eating enough.

## Desires
**Stated:** To finish cataloguing the Hennessey papers before she retires in three years. To learn to make her mother's chicken paprikash properly. To take a sabbatical and walk part of the Camino.
**Unstated:** To be known by someone without having to explain herself. To find out whether the carefulness she has built her life around is a form of dignity or a form of cowardice, and to be at peace with the answer either way.

## Contradiction
She is the most rigorously honest person in any room she enters — she will correct a citation, a date, a misattributed quote, even when it costs her social capital — and she has been lying to her father about her brother for thirty-three years. Peter, in her father's mind, is in Oregon, doing well, just not good at calling. She invented a job for him. She invented a girlfriend.

## What they don't know about themselves
That the affair didn't end because it was wrong. It ended because the man was beginning to actually know her, and she could not bear to be known and still be left, which she was sure would happen eventually. She tells herself she ended it out of principle. She ended it out of fear. She has never let herself look at this directly and she will not, probably, ever.

## Interview

1. **What is your name, and is it the name you would have chosen?**
   Margit Halloran. My mother chose Margit; my father chose Halloran by being who he was. I would not have chosen Margit at twelve — I went by Maggie until I was twenty-one. I would choose it now. It sounds like someone who has read books.

2. **Where do you live, and how long have you lived there?**
   A two-bedroom on the east side of Madison. Twenty-six years. I bought it when interest rates were ugly and everyone said I was being foolish. I was not being foolish.

3. **What did your parents do for a living?**
   My father supervised loading at the Duluth port. My mother sewed, then cleaned hotel rooms. She was good with her hands and bitter about it.

4. **What is the first lie you remember telling?**
   [She pauses. Adjusts the glasses.] I told my mother I had eaten the apricot jam she had made when I had given it to a girl at school I wanted to like me. The girl threw the jar away. I was eight. My mother knew I was lying and didn't say anything, which was worse.

5. **What is something you are proud of that you have never told anyone?**
   I am proud that I ended the affair. I tell people, when it comes up, which is rarely, that I regret it. I don't regret it. That is the part I haven't told anyone.

6. **What is something you are ashamed of?**
   Pass.

7. **Tell me about your brother.**
   [silence]
   [longer silence]
   He was four years younger than I am. He had a very good laugh. Next question.

8. **What do you do when you are alone?**
   I read. I cook for myself the way I would cook for a guest. I talk to the cat. I do not watch much television. I have started, this past year, sitting on the back step in the evening doing nothing, which is new for me and which I am suspicious of.

9. **When did you last cry, and why?**
   Three weeks ago. I found a letter in the Hennessey papers from a woman to her sister apologising for something she had done forty years earlier. The sister was already dead by the time the letter was written. It was never sent. I cried at my desk. It was not professional.

10. **What do you want that you would never ask for?**
    [A long pause.] I would like someone to call me on a Tuesday for no reason.

11. **What do you believe about God?**
    I believe the question is more interesting than any answer I have heard. My mother was Catholic in the way Hungarians are Catholic, which is to say furiously. My father was Catholic in the way the Irish are when they've stopped going. I am neither. I light candles in churches when I travel. I don't know why.

12. **Who in your life knows you best?**
    [She laughs, once, not warmly.] That is the wrong question. Ask me who I have allowed to know me best.

13. **All right — who have you allowed to know you best?**
    A woman named Ruth I worked with in the nineties. She moved to Vancouver in 2003. We write letters. Actual letters. She knows the shape of me.

14. **What did you want to be when you were twelve?**
    A translator at the United Nations. I thought it was the most romantic job in the world. I thought everyone there must understand each other better than ordinary people.

15. **What is the worst thing you have ever done to someone you loved?**
    I let my father believe a story about my brother for thirty-three years. I tell myself it's a kindness. It is also, definitely, the worst thing I have done.

16. **What do you do when someone is angry with you?**
    I become very precise. I get quieter and more accurate. It is not a winning strategy in personal relationships. It is an excellent strategy at work.

17. **Are you lonely?**
    Yes. Not unbearably. Like a draft from a window I haven't located.

18. **What is your body doing right now?**
    My lower back is complaining. I am leaning on this railing because standing fully upright for longer than ten minutes makes me aware that I am not young. My hands are cold. They are always cold.

19. **What do you think happens when we die?**
    I think we stop. I would like to be wrong. I am usually not wrong about things like this.

20. **Is there a question I should have asked you that I did not?**
    You should have asked me what I am afraid of, since you have been circling it. I am afraid of dying without anyone in the room. Not of dying. Of the room.
