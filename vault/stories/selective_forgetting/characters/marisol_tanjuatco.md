# Marisol Tanjuatco

## Face
A woman in her late twenties or early thirties. Medium warm-beige skin, smooth, with a slight sheen under the lighting. Dark hair, almost black, pulled back tight against the scalp; a few loose strands have come down across the forehead and the side of the face. Almond-shaped dark brown eyes, eyelids defined. Full lips, neutral, slightly pressed together; there is a gloss on them. The nose has a relatively flat bridge and a softly rounded tip. Small minimalist earring on the left ear. Black crew-neck top. The lighting on the photograph is split — cooler on one side of the face, warmer on the other — which is a property of the photograph rather than of her, but it is what is there. She is looking at the camera directly. The eyes are doing the watching; the mouth is softer than the eyes. She is holding still, and the stillness looks like a decision rather than a state.

## History
Born 1994 in Quezon City, Philippines. Her father was a civil engineer who worked on overseas contracts — Saudi Arabia, then Qatar, then back home, then Singapore. Her mother was a nurse who eventually trained as a midwife. Marisol is the middle of three: an older sister, Patricia (Trish), now a corporate lawyer in Makati, and a younger brother, Emman, who has Down syndrome and lives at home with their mother. Their father died of a heart attack in 2016, on a layover in Doha, alone in a hotel room. He was fifty-four.

Marisol moved to Toronto in 2018 on a student visa to do a master's in public health. She stayed. She works now for a nonprofit in Toronto that runs sexual and reproductive health programs in the Asia-Pacific region, which means she travels often to Manila, Bangkok, and Jakarta and is on calls at strange hours. She lives alone in a one-bedroom apartment in Parkdale with too many plants and a record player that mostly sits unused.

She sends money home every month. She does not talk about how much. Her mother has stopped saying thank you, which Marisol takes as a sign of respect and also of something more complicated.

She was engaged once, in 2021, to a Canadian man named Andrew who worked in development finance. They broke it off four months before the wedding because he wanted to move to Geneva and she was not, when it came to it, able to picture herself there. The truth was more specific: she could not picture herself raising children who would not know her brother. She did not tell Andrew this. She told him she wasn't ready.

She has been in therapy for two years. She has a complicated relationship with the Catholic church she was raised in. She still crosses herself at takeoff.

## Desires
**Stated:** To get her organisation's funding renewed for another three years. To finally take the diving certification course she has been postponing. To bring her mother to Toronto for a long visit.
**Unstated:** To stop being the responsible one. To find out what she would want if she were not also calculating what everyone else needs. To be loved in a way that does not require her to be useful.

## Contradiction
She runs programs that exist to give other women — younger, poorer, in more constrained circumstances than her own — bodily autonomy and the language to claim it. She is articulate, professional, calmly devastating in funder meetings on this point. In her own life she has not had a frank conversation with her mother about anything personal in nine years. She tells herself the cultural context is different. She knows this is partly true and partly an excuse, and she has not yet decided what to do with that knowledge.

## What they don't know about themselves
That she ended the engagement not because of Geneva and not entirely because of Emman, but because Andrew was the first person who treated her as someone whose needs were real and who could be cared for, and she did not know how to receive that without feeling that it would make her soft, and that softness would make her useless to her family, and uselessness was, in the structure of her life, a kind of death. She thinks she made a principled decision. She made a frightened one. She is not yet ready to know this.

## Interview

1. **What is your name, and is it the name you would have chosen?**
   Marisol Tanjuatco. My mother named me after a song she liked. I would have chosen something shorter, when I was younger. Now I like that no one in Toronto pronounces it correctly the first time. It keeps the name mine.

2. **Where do you live, and how long have you lived there?**
   Parkdale, Toronto. Six years in this apartment. Almost seven in the city.

3. **What did your parents do for a living?**
   My father was a civil engineer. He worked on bridges and water systems, mostly in the Gulf. My mother is a midwife. She has caught more babies than she can count. Literally — I asked her once and she laughed.

4. **What is the first lie you remember telling?**
   I told my mother I had finished my catechism homework when I had not. I was seven. I went to confession the next Saturday and confessed it. I think I have been overcorrecting ever since.

5. **What is something you are proud of that you have never told anyone?**
   I negotiated a raise last year that was larger than what I had been told to ask for. I did it in the meeting, on the spot. I did not tell my mother because she would worry I was being greedy. I did not tell my sister because she would tell me I was still underpaid, which is true and not the point.

6. **What is something you are ashamed of?**
   I was not at home when my father died. I was at a conference in Vancouver. I had just gotten a text from him that morning that I had not answered yet because I was in a session. I answered it that evening, not knowing. He was already dead. He never read it.

7. **Tell me about your brother.**
   Emman is twenty-six. He likes cooking shows, basketball, and our mother more than anyone else in the world. He is the funniest person in our family. He notices when I am sad on video calls before our mother does. He cannot live alone, which is a fact, but I want to be careful when I say it because people hear that fact and stop seeing him. I am very protective of him. Probably too protective. He does not need me to be.

8. **What do you do when you are alone?**
   I cook. I cook far more than one person needs. I freeze a lot of it. I read in the bath. I listen to my mother's voice notes more than once. I do a thing where I sit on the floor and lean against the couch and do nothing for a while. I have a record player I almost never use.

9. **When did you last cry, and why?**
   Last week, on a video call with my mother. She had cut her hair. I had not seen her in person in fourteen months. I did not let her see me cry. I said the connection was bad and turned my camera off.

10. **What do you want that you would never ask for?**
    [She breathes out slowly.] I would like someone to take care of me when I am sick. Not in a big way. Soup. A bad TV show. Someone in the next room. I have been sick alone in this apartment many times. I am very competent at it.

11. **What do you believe about God?**
    I believe in something. I am not sure it is what I was taught it was. I still pray when planes take off. I pray for Emman every day. I would feel like something terrible would happen if I stopped. I know that is not how prayer is supposed to work.

12. **Who in your life knows you best?**
    My sister Trish. We have not been close in the way people imagine sisters being close. We are close in the way that two people who survived the same childhood are close. She knows where the bodies are buried. So do I, in her case. We do not talk about it. We don't need to.

13. **What did you want to be when you were twelve?**
    A doctor. I wanted to be the kind of doctor who flies into disasters. Médecins Sans Frontières, that kind of thing. I ended up doing something not entirely unlike it from a desk in Toronto. I am not sure if that counts as the dream coming true or as a compromise.

14. **What is the worst thing you have ever done to someone you loved?**
    I let Andrew think the reason was Geneva. It was not only Geneva. I did not give him the real reason because I did not want to be argued out of my decision. He deserved the real reason. I have not contacted him to give it to him, and I am not going to. That is part of the worst thing.

15. **What do you do when someone is angry with you?**
    I become very polite. Very accommodating on the surface. Underneath I am taking notes and I do not forget. This is not, I have been told, a healthy pattern.

16. **Are you lonely?**
    [Pause.] Yes. I am lonely in a way that I have organised my life around so that it does not become unbearable. I am not sure if that is a solution or a kind of bargaining.

17. **What is your body doing right now?**
    My shoulders are up by my ears. They always are by this time of day. I have a headache starting behind the right eye. I am thirsty and I have not gotten up to get water because we are in the middle of this conversation, which is also a thing I do — I prioritise the meeting over the water. I am aware of it. I do it anyway.

18. **What do you think happens when we die?**
    I do not know. I would like to believe my father is somewhere, and that he knows I answered the text. I do not actually believe that. I light candles for him anyway.

19. **What are you afraid of?**
    My mother dying before I have figured out how to talk to her. Emman outliving her and being alone in a way he has never been before. Being the one who has to decide what comes next. All of it really being on me. It is on me. I am afraid of it.

20. **Is there a question I should have asked you that I did not?**
    You should have asked me what I do that no one knows about. I write letters to my father, since he died. I do not send them, obviously. There are seventy-three of them. They are in a drawer. I have never told anyone. I am telling you because you are not anyone, you are this conversation, which will end, and I want to have said it out loud once.
