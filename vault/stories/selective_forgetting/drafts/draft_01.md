# draft_01

The contract was signed in March, in the conference room on the fourth floor where the carpet was the color of dishwater and the radiator made a sound like someone tapping a wooden spoon against a pot. Margit had recommended it. She had recommended it in writing, with seventeen footnotes, three of them flagging her own reservations, which she had then in person walked the board through with the precision she reserved for things she wanted to be on the record as having said. The reservations were noted. The recommendation was adopted. The vendor representative — a woman named Carla, in a navy blazer, who had flown in from Minneapolis — shook her hand and said, *We're going to do right by you, Margit*, and Margit corrected the pronunciation without smiling.

She walked home along Williamson. The sidewalk was wet from a thaw that would not last. Her hands were cold inside her coat pockets. They were always cold. She thought, briefly, of the apricot jam, and then she thought about what to make for dinner, and the two thoughts were related but she did not look at how.

That was March.

By June the ingestion was running. The first phase was the McCarthy-era papers, which Margit knew the way some people know the inside of their own mouth. There was a misfiled letter from a metallurgist in Racine that had been sitting in the wrong folder since 1956 and that she had moved, twice, in twenty-two years, from drawer to drawer when she was reorganizing — never to its correct home, because its correct home would have placed it next to a name that the metallurgist's widow, who was still alive in a duplex outside Janesville, had asked the archive to forget. The widow's request was not on file. It was on Margit. She had carried it in her hands.

The vendor's system found the letter on the third day of ingestion. It surfaced cleanly in a sample query the engineers ran to verify retrieval. It was a triumph of OCR and proximity matching. Carla emailed Margit a screenshot. *Look what your archive can do now.* Margit looked at the screenshot for a long time. Then she went down to the basement and opened the drawer the letter had been in for six years, and the letter was still there, physically, paper, where she had put it. The system had not moved it. It had only found it.

She put the letter in a different drawer.

She did not call this misfiling. She called it, in her head, *not yet*. She thought she would put it back when she had thought about it more. She did not put it back. By the end of June there were nine letters in drawers that were not theirs, and she had begun to do other things — small things, almost involuntary, a permit slipped into a folder of recipes the regional historical society had donated in 1981, a list of 1953 zoning variances tucked behind the parish bulletins from St. Bernard's. Her hands were ahead of her thinking. She found she did not want to catch up.

What she would not have called it, then, was a practice. A practice has a person doing it. This was something more like a tic, or like the way her mother, in the last year, had begun to fold her napkin into smaller and smaller squares before unfolding it to eat. Not deliberate. Not undeliberate.

In July she stopped touching the paper.

She did not stop all at once. She had been spending more time at the terminal because the system needed her — needed her judgments, in the form of accept-or-reject buttons on flagged ambiguities, six hundred a day on a slow day. *Is this person the same as this person.* *Should this letter be tagged with this name.* The buttons were green and red. She got fast at them. She got fast in the way you get fast at sorting laundry, where the speed itself is a small pleasure that crowds out the question of whether the laundry needed sorting in this particular way.

By the second week of July she was clicking through queries without going downstairs. By the third week she had not been in the basement in five days. On Friday of the fourth week she went down because she wanted to put a thing — a thing that had come in over the transom, an unsolicited donation, a packet of letters from a woman who had taught at the Indian boarding school in Tomah from 1948 to 1953 — somewhere it would not be found until she had read it. She went down with the packet under her arm and stood in front of the drawers and her hand moved toward the drawer for *Education, Federal, 1940-1960* and she stopped it. She tried to think where else the packet could go. She could think of seven possibilities. Six months earlier she would have known, in her hand, before her mind got there, which one. The hand would have lifted and the drawer would have opened and the packet would have been put away and the day would have continued.

She stood there for a long time. Her lower back complained. She put the packet in *Education, Federal, 1940-1960*. She walked back up the stairs. In the elevator she said, out loud, to no one, *I do not know what I just did.* The elevator was empty. She was glad of that and also not glad.

At her desk she logged into the system. She queried the packet. The system surfaced it instantly, with a probability score of 0.94 that the letters were correctly placed in *Education, Federal, 1940-1960*, and adjacencies suggested by the model included three other sets of teacher correspondence and a federal funding ledger from 1951. The adjacencies were correct. The placement was correct. The system had agreed with her.

She thought: *the system has agreed with me.* She thought: *I have agreed with the system.* She could not feel the difference.

She tried, that night, to misfile something. She had brought home — in violation of policy, which she had written — a single document, a 1962 affidavit from a hearing about the disposition of children of unwed mothers in Dane County. It was a document that under the old practice she would have placed in a drawer no one would think to open for forty years, because the names in it were names that still belonged to people. She sat at her kitchen table with the affidavit and a folder marked *Agricultural Bulletins, 1960-1965* that she had also brought home, and she tried to put the affidavit into the folder, and her hands would not do it. They would not refuse, exactly. They simply did not know what to do. She held the affidavit over the folder and the affidavit stayed in her hand and the folder stayed open and nothing happened. She sat there for ten minutes. The cat watched her from the chair.

She put the affidavit back into its correct folder. She put the folder into her bag. She poured a glass of wine and did not drink it.

In August Carla flew in for a quarterly review. The system's metrics were excellent. Retrieval precision was 0.97. User satisfaction among the three pilot researchers was high. *Margit*, Carla said, in her navy blazer, *this is the cleanest ingestion we've ever run. You set us up beautifully.* Margit said *thank you* and meant it the way you mean it when someone compliments a casserole you no longer remember making.

In September a Norwegian girl came to the reading room.

She was seventeen, on a semester exchange, very tall and very quiet and very polite. She wore a sweatshirt that said NYC 1998 and she had a notebook in which she wrote in English. She was researching the path of her grandmother, who had emigrated through Duluth in 1958. Margit, who had been Duluth before she was Madison, asked her grandmother's name. The girl said it. Margit did not recognize it. There was no reason she should have. The girl thanked her and went to the terminal.

Margit watched her, on and off, for an hour. The girl typed slowly. She read what came back slowly. She wrote in the notebook. At one point she took something out from under her sweatshirt — a chain with a small ring on it — and held it for a moment and put it back. She did not know she was being watched. She would not have minded. She was the same when no one was watching, or wanted to be, which was almost the same thing.

Around three the girl came to Margit's desk and asked, in careful English, whether the system could find a record of a hearing. She said the date — March 1962 — and a name. The name was her grandmother's sister, who had come over a year later and whose path through the county Elin had been told, by her father, was unhappy and short and not to be inquired into. Elin was inquiring into it because her father had told her not to, and because her grandmother was dead, and because she had decided something she was not going to tell anyone. She did not say any of this. She said: *I would like to know if there is a record.*

Margit said: *Let me check.* She typed the name into the terminal. The system surfaced the 1962 affidavit. The same affidavit. The one she had tried, in her kitchen, to misfile. It was in *Family Court, Dane County, 1960-1965*, exactly where it belonged, and the cross-reference protocol — which Margit herself had approved in March, with a footnote — automatically generated a notification to the listed descendants of all parties named in the document, informing them that a researcher had accessed the record. The notification was generated at 3:14 p.m. The girl's name was logged as the requesting researcher.

Margit watched the notification compile. She watched the system's progress bar. It was a green bar. It moved smoothly across the screen. There was a checkmark when it finished.

She thought of the metallurgist's widow. She thought: *the widow is dead.* She thought: *no, the widow is not dead, she is in Janesville, she is ninety-three.* She thought: *somewhere in Madison, in Tomah, in three other counties, six people whose surnames I do not know are about to receive an email.*

The girl was looking at her. The girl had a face that was waiting without pressing. Margit said: *There is a record. I can pull the physical document for you tomorrow. You'll need to sign for it.* The girl nodded and said *thank you* and went back to the terminal. Margit went to the bathroom and washed her hands. Her hands were very cold and the water was very cold and she stood there until someone came in and she dried her hands and went back to her desk.

She thought, briefly, of calling Carla. She did not. She thought of telling someone — Ruth, in Vancouver, on paper — and she did not. She sat at her desk until five and clicked through ninety-eight ambiguity queries. She got most of them right.

At home she fed the cat. She sat on the back step. It was the warm part of September and the step was warm against the back of her thighs and she sat there doing nothing for a while, which was a thing she had started doing this past year and which she was suspicious of. The suspicion had been, originally, that the doing-nothing was the beginning of something — depression, she had thought, or just age. She understood now that it was neither. It was the small pocket of her day in which she had not yet been retrieved.

The Hennessey papers were on her desk on Monday. They had been waiting since spring, the second tranche, the one that contained — among five hundred other things — a letter she had cried over in May. The letter had been written in 1981 by a woman named Eileen Hennessey to her sister Mary, apologizing for an abortion in 1939 that Eileen had performed on Mary in a bathroom in St. Paul and that Mary had nearly died from. Mary had not died from it. Mary had died, of a stroke, in 1976. The letter was written five years too late and never sent and Eileen had kept it in a tin behind her wedding photographs. The tin had come to the archive in 2017 with the rest of the Hennessey estate. Margit had opened the tin in May. She had not put the letter back in the tin. She had put it in a folder marked *Recipes, undated* and shelved the folder in the cookbook donation drawer, where no one had any reason to go, and she had cried at her desk and gone home and not told anyone.

The folder was on her desk now because Janet, the new assistant, doing routine pre-ingestion verification, had noticed that *Recipes, undated* contained an item that did not appear to be a recipe. Janet had flagged it and pulled it. The letter was on top of the folder. The folder was on Margit's desk. Janet had left a note. *Margit — found this in the cookbooks. Misfile? Want me to put it where it belongs?*

Margit looked at the note for a long time. She thought of the words *where it belongs.* She thought of a system of belonging that had a place for this letter. She tried to summon the conviction, which she had had in May, that the letter belonged in the cookbook drawer because Eileen had kept it in a tin behind her wedding photographs and because Eileen had not wanted, ever, for it to be read by anyone who was not her sister, who was dead, and who therefore would never read it. She tried to summon the practice. She tried to summon the hand that had put the letter into *Recipes, undated*.

Her hand was on the desk. It did not move.

She picked up the letter. She read the first paragraph. She had read it before. It had not changed. What had changed was that she could now see, through the paper, the green progress bar. She could see the checkmark. She could see the email going out to whatever niece or great-nephew of Mary Hennessey was on the contact list, and she could see them opening it on a Tuesday in November and being told that a researcher from Marquette had accessed their great-aunt's correspondence. She could see this very clearly. It was the clearest thing she had seen all year.

She picked up Janet's note. She wrote underneath it, in her small careful hand: *Thank you, Janet. Please re-file in Hennessey, Box 14, correspondence 1979-1983. Margit.*

She put the note on top of the letter. She put the letter back in the folder. She put the folder in Janet's tray. She walked down the hall to the bathroom and stood at the sink and did not cry. She had not cried since May, which was four months and which was, she understood standing there, going to be the new shape of the rest of her life. She would not cry over the Hennessey letter again, because she had become a person to whom the Hennessey letter was a record. She had become a person who was good at her job in the way her job had been redefined to be good. The badness was in the redefinition and the redefinition was hers.

She walked back to her desk. Janet was at her own desk, two rooms over. Margit could hear her typing.

She thought of her father, in Duluth, ninety-one. She thought of the story she had told him about Peter — the Oregon job, the girlfriend, the not-good-at-calling. She thought of the Tuesdays for thirty-three years on which she had held that story together by hand, by the hand that had also misfiled the metallurgist's letter, by the hand that had put Eileen Hennessey in with the recipes. The story about Peter had been, all along, a kind of misfiling. The truth had been moved into the wrong drawer and the right drawer had been left empty and her father had lived in the empty drawer for thirty-three years and called her every Sunday at four to ask if she was eating enough.

She picked up the phone. It was Tuesday, 4:11 p.m. He would be having his coffee. He would be surprised to hear from her on a Tuesday. He would worry that something was wrong. She put the phone down.

She picked it up again. She put it down again.

She thought: *the hand will not lift.*

She walked, that evening, past her own street and three streets further, to the church on Williamson. It was open. There was no one inside. She had not been in this church before, in twenty-six years in Madison, although she had walked past it most weeks for most of those years. There was a rack of candles in red glass holders to the left of the altar. There was a metal box for offerings. She did not put anything in the box. She did not light a candle. She sat in the second pew from the back and put her hands flat on her thighs and waited for whatever was supposed to happen to happen. Her hands were cold against her thighs. Her thighs were cold against her hands. After a while she understood that what was supposed to happen was that she was supposed to sit there. So she sat.

The church got darker. No one came in. No one turned on a light. At some point she became aware that she was hungry, and that her father was probably already in bed in Duluth, and that the girl Elin would be at her terminal in the morning, and that the email about the 1962 affidavit had been received by six people whose names Margit did not know, and that one of them had probably already opened it, and that whatever the consequence of that opening would be was now in motion and could not be stopped by anything Margit did or did not do at this particular moment in this particular church.

She sat there a while longer. She did not light the candle. She was not sure who the candle would have been for. She thought it might have been for the version of her hand that had known, in May, where the Eileen Hennessey letter belonged. That hand was not coming back. Lighting a candle for a hand felt, even to her, like a thing someone else would do.

She got up. Her lower back complained. She walked home along Williamson. The sidewalk was dry. Her hands were cold inside her coat pockets. They were always cold.

At home she fed the cat. She did not eat. She sat at her kitchen table and opened her laptop and typed a query into the archive system, from her home credentials, which were valid, which she herself had set up. She typed her brother's name. *Peter Halloran.* She added the year, 1991, and the place, Bemidji.

The system returned three results in 0.4 seconds. A police incident report. A coroner's note. A small clipping from the *Bemidji Pioneer*, four lines, no photograph. The clipping had been digitized in the second wave of regional newspaper ingestion in August. She had approved that ingestion in July, with a footnote about access controls she had then not enforced because the footnote had felt, at the time, fussy.

The progress bar had been green. The checkmark had appeared. The clipping was on her screen now, in her kitchen, in Madison, on a Tuesday in September, and her father was asleep in Duluth in a room with a window that faced the lake, and somewhere in a server farm in Iowa a model that had been finetuned on the Halloran surname for the past four months understood, with a confidence of 0.91, that Peter Halloran had died in a motel in Bemidji on the eighth of November, 1991, and would surface this information to anyone who asked, including, eventually, her father, if her father ever learned to ask, which he had been spared learning by her, by her hand, for thirty-three years.

She closed the laptop. The cat was on the chair. The cat was looking at her. She said, out loud: *I do not know what I just did.* The cat did not answer. The kitchen was quiet. The radiator had not yet started for the season. Outside it was getting cold. Inside her hands were cold and her thighs were cold where her hands rested on them, and she sat at the kitchen table with the closed laptop in front of her and waited for whatever was supposed to happen next to happen, and nothing did, and the not-happening was now the shape of the room.
