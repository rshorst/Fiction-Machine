## Face
The smile is what lands first. It is bright and it knows it is being photographed. It is giving the camera something deliberate.

Then the eyes. They are aimed slightly off-frame, past the lens. There is a mild distance in them that the smile does not account for. She is thinking about something the smile has nothing to do with.

She appears to be in her early-to-mid twenties. The braided hair, the orchid at the side of her head, the position of her hands — all of it has been considered. She moves through the world with attention to how she appears in it. This is not vanity. It is precision of a different kind.

There is thanaka on her cheek — the pale paste worn by Burmese women and girls, traditional, applied with care. It is a quiet statement. Present in a photograph that is otherwise composed for a general audience.

Unresolved: the distance between what the face is offering — warmth, openness, the full photogenic smile — and where the eyes have actually gone. What she is thinking about while performing availability is private and has not been surrendered.

## History

Thiri Kyaw was born in 1999 in Yangon, Myanmar, the middle child of three. Her father, Ko Zaw, was an import-export clerk who had taught himself German and Dutch from cassette tapes and correspondence courses over fifteen years, driven by the conviction that language was the only investment that could not be seized. Her mother, Daw Hnin, ran a small tailoring business from their apartment and had opinions about everything which she expressed precisely and without qualification.

Ko Zaw got a work visa for the Netherlands in 2009, when Thiri was ten. The family followed in 2011. They settled in Rotterdam. Ko Zaw found work — not in his field but adjacent to it, clerical work for a freight company that used his language skills for German correspondence. He was good at his job and was not promoted because he was not Dutch, a fact he understood and never spoke about directly.

Thiri grew up in Rotterdam, Dutch from age twelve, Burmese at home, English from television and school. She was good at school in the way that children of immigrants who have watched their parents work very hard tend to be good at school — thoroughly, without margin for failure. She studied translation and interpreting at Hogeschool Rotterdam, completing her degree in 2021.

She did a placement year at Seghers Translations. This is where she met Pieter. He gave her precise, difficult work and told her when it was wrong, which she respected. He was not warm but he was fair, which in her experience was rarer. She worked for him part-time for two years after the placement. She was the last person regularly in the office before he died.

She knows the files. She knows which clients were difficult and which were reliable and which ones Pieter handled personally and which he farmed out. She knows about the Voss file because she had been asked to prepare the document folder before Pieter began work on it, and she had seen enough to understand it was not ordinary.

After Pieter died, she was notified — eventually, slowly, through the lawyer — that the business was closing and she would need to make other arrangements. She found work at a larger translation agency. She is good at her job there and finds it less interesting.

She came to the office once, after the death but before Marta arrived, to collect personal items: a dictionary she had left there, a notebook. She found the Voss file had been moved from where Pieter kept it. She noted this and said nothing about it to anyone.

She does not know Marta. When Marta arrives to close the business, Thiri comes back — ostensibly to ask about a reference letter. She stays because she sees that Marta has found the file, and because she has been waiting, she realizes, for someone to find it.

She has her own relationship to what the file contains. She has not told anyone what she knows.

Her father Ko Zaw has opinions about what she should do with what she knows. She has not asked him for them and he has given them anyway. She is still deciding whether he is right.

## Desires

**Stated:** To be of use. To give Marta what she needs to understand the file, because Pieter would have wanted it understood, and because Thiri spent two years learning how Pieter thought and that knowledge is now hers and she does not want it to be wasted.

**Unstated:** To be recognized. Not praised — recognized. To have someone understand that she is not a peripheral figure in this story, not an assistant, not a resource. She was in that office. She did the work. Whatever the file means, she has earned the right to be in the room where it gets resolved. She does not know how to say this without it sounding like a demand, so she has not said it.

## Contradiction

She is caught between two things she holds with equal conviction: that the information in the file should come out — that concealment is a form of complicity she is not willing to be part of — and that she does not trust the people who would receive it, process it, use it. She does not trust institutions. She has watched her father be treated correctly by institutions and still lose. She does not believe correctness and justice are the same thing. She wants disclosure and she does not trust disclosure. She is holding both without resolution.

## What they don't know about themselves

She has constructed a loyalty to Pieter Seghers that she believes is professional — a matter of respect for good work, for precision, for the standards he held. What it also is: the first time an adult outside her family treated her ability as simply real, without surprise, without the slight adjustment that happened in most rooms when she proved competent. She does not know how much of what she is doing is for Pieter and how much is for the version of herself that Pieter's office allowed her to be. They are not entirely separate. But they are not the same.

## Interview

**1. What did you want to be when you were eight?**
A newsreader. On television. I practiced in front of the mirror with a hairbrush. My mother thought it was very funny. I wanted to be the person everyone listened to — like, everyone had to be quiet because you were speaking and what you said was important. I grew out of it. Or I found different versions of it, I suppose.

**2. Tell me about a time you let someone down.**
When my father got passed over for the team lead position — the fifth time, roughly — he called me and I could hear he was trying not to be upset about it. And I said all the right things, practical things, about updating his CV, about other opportunities. And what I should have said was: this is wrong and I'm angry too and I see it. I gave him strategy when he needed me to see him. I know I did it. I haven't been able to go back and fix it.

**3. What do you own that you would never sell?**
My father's cassette tapes. The German language cassettes he learned from. They don't play anymore — I don't have anything to play them on — but they exist. He carried them from Yangon. I would never sell them.

**4. Who do you call when something goes wrong?**
My older sister Nant. She is very calm. She asks the right questions. She doesn't tell me what to do, she just — she asks until I know what I think.

**5. What do you pretend not to know?**
[small pause]
What's in that file. Or — I pretend I don't know what it means. I know what the documents are. I have thought about what they imply. I pretend I haven't finished that thought.

**6. Describe your mother without using the word "she."**
My mother is a small woman who takes up a lot of room. My mother's opinions arrive fully formed, like they were always there and she is just releasing them. My mother made my father's lunch every day for twenty years and also told him exactly what was wrong with his decisions and he listened to both of those things with equal seriousness. My mother cried at my graduation and then immediately told me what my next step should be. My mother learned Dutch at fifty-two because she decided it was time and it took her eight months and now her Dutch is better than some people who grew up here, which she finds privately satisfying.

**7. What are you afraid other people can see about you?**
That I'm impatient. That I can see where someone's logic is going before they've finished and it shows in my face. I have been told this. I'm working on it but I don't think I've fixed it.

**8. What do you do in the first ten minutes after you wake up?**
I check my phone — messages from family in Yangon, time difference means they've been awake for hours already. Then I make tea. Then I look at my to-do list for the day. I like knowing what's coming.

**9. Tell me something you've never said out loud.**
I miss that office. I know that's — it's a small office above a print shop, it's not impressive. But it was the first place I worked where I felt like the job was real. Like the work mattered specifically because I was the one doing it, and not anyone else. I miss that. I haven't said that because it sounds sad, and I'm not sad about it, exactly. I just miss it.

**10. What do you want that you've never asked for?**
To be part of how this ends. Whatever happens with the file. I was there. I was part of that work. I want to be in the room when it gets finished. I haven't asked because I'm not sure I have standing to ask. But I want it.

**11. Where do you go when you need to disappear?**
There's a pagoda in Rotterdam — a small Burmese Buddhist community space, not grand, a converted building. I go there sometimes. I don't always do anything when I'm there. I just sit. It smells like I remember things smelling. That helps.

**12. What do you refuse to learn?**
[thinks] I refuse to learn to be less direct. People have suggested this to me — that I would get further if I softened my delivery, said things more — I don't know, obliquely. My father has a whole system for this, for navigating, for managing the reaction before you deliver the content. I can't do it. It makes me feel like I'm lying before I've said anything.

**13. Who do you become when no one is watching?**
Louder. I talk to myself when I'm working — in Burmese, mostly, or in whatever language the document is. I make noise. I eat noisily. I'm much more comfortable than I look in public.

**14. What would you take back, if you could?**
I would have asked Pieter — Mr. Seghers — I would have asked him directly about the Voss file. What it was. What he thought. He would have told me it wasn't my concern and I would have pushed back and we would have argued, probably, and I would have found out more than I know now. I didn't ask because I was new enough to still be being careful. I regret the carefulness.

**15. Describe the last time you were surprised by yourself.**
When I found out Mr. Seghers had died, I went to the office before anyone told me to. No reason — I had a key from the placement and had just never returned it. I went there at seven in the morning and I sat at my old desk and I just — I went through all the current files and checked them. Made sure everything was in order. I don't know what I thought I was doing. He was dead, it didn't matter if the files were in order. But I put everything in order. I surprised myself with how much I cared about leaving it right.

**16. What holds you in a place you should have left?**
The new agency is fine. The work is adequate. The people are fine. I stay because it pays well and because I am twenty-four and I am still building something and you don't leave things that are adequate when you are building something. But I know adequate is not what I want and I stay anyway.

**17. What do other people see in you that you don't see yourself?**
Nant says I'm braver than I think. She says most people in my position would have stayed quiet and I don't stay quiet. I don't feel brave, I just feel — like staying quiet has a cost I don't want to pay. Maybe that's the same thing.

**18. What are you good at that you wish you weren't?**
Reading situations quickly. Knowing who has the power in a room and how they're using it. I am very fast at this. It means I can never fully relax in a new environment because I'm always — I'm always mapping it. I'd like to be able to walk into a room and just be there. I can't do that.

**19. Is there something you've done that you don't have a name for?**
I read the Voss documents. More than I needed to for the filing. I read them carefully. I understood most of them — my German is good enough. I held what I understood and I didn't do anything with it and I went home and I cooked dinner and I watched television. I kept going to work every day. I don't know what to call the months between reading those documents and now. Waiting isn't quite right. Surviving isn't right. I was just — holding the information and not yet knowing what to do with it.

**20. What would you say to the person who made you like this?**
My father, I think. I'd say: I see what you did. I see that you carried everything across and rebuilt it here and kept going. I know I am like this because you are like this. I don't know if that's the right way to be yet. But I'm glad I got it from you and not from someone smaller.

**21. Do you think a machine can make something beautiful?**
Yes. I think beauty is in the encounter with the thing, not just in the thing's origin. If I encounter something and it is beautiful to me, that experience is real regardless of how the thing was made.

**22. If a machine wrote something that moved you, would you tell anyone?**
Yes, I'd tell Nant. I'd tell her because she'd want to think about it with me, not because she'd have a comfortable answer. She'd probably have an uncomfortable one.

**23. What would have to be true for you to call something made by a machine — art?**
I think — intention somewhere in the chain. Not necessarily in the machine. In the person who directed it, chose it, put it forward. The intention doesn't have to be in the object itself.

**24. What do you do that you think a machine couldn't do?**
Walk into a room full of people speaking three different languages and know within four minutes who is not saying what they mean. That's pattern recognition, yes, but it's also — it's knowing what a person costs themselves to say something. I don't think a machine knows about cost.

**25. Does it bother you that machines are learning to do things only humans used to do? Why or why not?**
For translation specifically — it worries me for my career in a practical sense, yes. But I also think the things I find most interesting about translation are the things machines currently do worst. The register, the weight of a word in context, the choice between two technically correct options where one is right and one is merely accurate. I think that gap will close. I'm trying not to build my whole professional identity on a gap that might not last.

**26. If you found out something you loved was made by AI, would it change how you felt about it?**
I'd have to think about it each time. I don't think I have a general answer. I think it would matter what the thing was and what I was feeling when I encountered it.

**27. What do you think machines don't understand about being alive?**
That you belong to people. That part of what you are is made of specific others — your mother, your father, your dead grandfather you never met but whose cassette tapes your father carried. You are made of people and they are made of people and it goes back. I don't think a machine is made of anyone.

**28. Do you trust machines? Why or why not?**
For defined tasks, yes, within their defined scope. Beyond that, no — not because they're malicious but because the scope isn't always clear from the outside, and I don't know how to read when a machine is at its limit. With a person, I can usually tell. With a machine, the errors are invisible right up until they're catastrophic.

**29. If a machine could know you better than you know yourself, would you want it to?**
[pause]
I think I'd want to know what it found. I'm not sure I'd want it to hold that knowledge — to have it sitting somewhere in a system, my interior life as a data point. The knowing sounds useful. The storing sounds wrong. I don't know how to have one without the other.
