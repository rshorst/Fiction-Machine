## Face
Black and white photograph. Woman, appears 60s–70s in the image; I am placing Marta at 51.

The first thing: the eyes. Not aimed at the camera. Slightly up and to the right — the angle of someone who has been asked a question and has not finished answering it yet. Not evasion. Something more like: I will answer when I am ready.

The mouth is compressed at the corners. Not tightly — it is her resting state now. She has said difficult things calmly, more than once. The compression is the residue of that.

Deep lines from the nose to the jaw. A furrowed brow that appears to be the default architecture of her face at this point in her life, not a temporary expression.

She is tolerating the camera, mildly. She is not performing.

After longer looking: tiredness under the composure. And something that reads as old grief — not fresh, not raw, but not gone either. It has been absorbed into how she holds her face.

Unresolved: whether the upward gaze is looking toward something or back at something.

## History

Marta Seghers was born in 1973 in Rotterdam, the second child of Pieter Seghers and Ineke van Dijk. Pieter was a commercial translator — Dutch, German, English, some French — who ran a small business out of an office above a printer's shop. He worked with contracts, technical manuals, the occasional literary text that paid badly. Ineke was a secondary school French teacher who had wanted to study literature at university but had not been permitted to by her own father, a fact she mentioned rarely and which shaped everything.

Marta had an older brother, Daan, who died at nineteen in a motorbike accident when Marta was sixteen. After Daan died, the family restructured itself around the absence. Pieter withdrew into work. Ineke became overinvested in Marta in a way that felt like surveillance. Marta became the family's one remaining project, which is not the same as being loved.

At seventeen, Marta left for Amsterdam to study comparative literature. She was not brilliant but she was thorough. She learned to read carefully. She learned four languages to reading-and-stumbling level and two to genuine fluency — English already, and then Spanish, which she studied because of a man she met in her first year who she followed into the Spanish department before he moved back to Seville. She did not follow him to Seville. She kept the Spanish.

She did not return to Rotterdam for three years after leaving. When she did, the relationship with her father was already at the particular temperature it would stay at: functional, polite, structured around occasions. Christmas. Her mother's birthday. He asked practical questions. She answered them.

She did not take over the business. That would have required him to want her to, and he never asked.

She built her own career in Amsterdam: first as a literary translator's assistant, then as a rights manager for a mid-sized publishing house, then as a freelance consultant for publishers acquiring foreign titles. She was good at it. She was trusted. She read widely and recommended carefully and when she said a book would not translate well she was almost always right.

She was married at thirty-one to a man named Ferdi Bakker, a graphic designer. It lasted nine years. No children — they had tried, and had not spoken about the trying in any direct way, and eventually the trying had taken up more space than they had. The divorce was civil. She still has his number in her phone and has not deleted it and has not called it in four years.

At forty-three, her mother Ineke died of a stroke. It was sudden and not clean. Marta was the one who arranged everything, flew back to Rotterdam, sat in the hospital, handled the paperwork. Pieter stood at the edge of all of it looking slightly bewildered. She thought: he doesn't know how to be in a room where something is happening. She has not examined whether that is fair.

At fifty, she received a call from a lawyer in Rotterdam. Her father had died — a cardiac event, in his office, presumably while working. The business, Seghers Translations, was hers. There were assets, a client list, a lease with eight months remaining, and several open files.

She did not expect to go to Rotterdam. Then she went.

She has been there for three weeks when the story begins. She expected to close the business in a week.

She has found one file she cannot close.

## Desires

**Stated:** To settle the estate, close the business properly, and return to Amsterdam. To honor whatever obligations her father left unfinished — not because she owes him, but because it is the right thing to do.

**Unstated:** To find something in her father's files that explains him. To locate the version of him that existed before she knew him, the Pieter who chose this work and built this life, and to understand whether there was something there she missed or whether there was genuinely nothing to find. She does not know she wants this. She thinks she wants to leave.

## Contradiction

She is a person who reads carefully — who has built a career on the precision of reading, on noticing what is said and what is not said in a text — and she never read her father. She walked around him for thirty years and did not look directly at him. She is professionally rigorous and personally avoidant, and she does not see these as the same trait operating in different directions.

## What they don't know about themselves

She believes her distance from her father was a response to his distance. She does not know that she chose it first — that at seventeen she left for Amsterdam not because the atmosphere at home was suffocating (though it was) but because she had already decided, before Daan died, before any of it, that she was not going to need anyone. The decision preceded the reasons she uses to explain it.

## Interview

**1. What did you want to be when you were eight?**
An interpreter at the United Nations. I'd seen it on a television programme — a woman sitting in a glass booth with headphones, translating in real time. I thought that was the most important job that existed. You made it possible for people to understand each other. I still think that's true, actually. I just didn't do that version of it.

**2. Tell me about a time you let someone down.**
[pause] My mother wanted grandchildren. I knew she did. She never said it as a demand — she was too careful for that — but I knew. And I — the situation with Ferdi was complicated, and I didn't talk to her about it. I let her die not knowing what had happened. I told myself it was to spare her. I'm not sure that was entirely true.

**3. What do you own that you would never sell?**
A German-Dutch dictionary. Very old. My father's, from his student days. He annotated the margins — not translations, just... impressions. *This word is sharper than its Dutch equivalent.* *This one has no equivalent, and maybe that's the point.* I didn't know he did that until I was packing the office. I would not sell it.

**4. Who do you call when something goes wrong?**
I don't, generally. My colleague Sofie, maybe. She's practical. I call her when I need someone to help me think through a problem, not — [pause] Not for comfort, I suppose.

**5. What do you pretend not to know?**
That the file I found is not a translation project. My father ran a very ordinary business. Whatever this is, it isn't ordinary. I have been pretending for ten days that it might still be ordinary.

**6. Describe your mother without using the word "she."**
Ineke was careful. Ineke made sandwiches in the same order every day. Ineke wanted to study Rimbaud at university and was told that was not practical and believed that verdict for forty years, and then at sixty read every word he ever wrote in three weeks while dying slowly in a way nobody knew about yet. Ineke was a person who had — I'm sorry. [pause] There was a lot of love in Ineke that had nowhere to go after Daan died. I was standing nearest.

**7. What are you afraid other people can see about you?**
That I am competent in the way that is a substitute for something else.

**8. What do you do in the first ten minutes after you wake up?**
I lie still. I don't reach for my phone. I work out where I am and what day it is before I move. In Rotterdam that's been — the room is not my room, the sounds are not my sounds. There's a clock in the apartment that ticks. I lie there and listen to it.

**9. Tell me something you've never said out loud.**
I don't think my father wanted me. Not in the way that meant cruelty — not like that. I think I was — I think he wanted a son and he had a son and then he didn't have a son, and I was the remaining one, and he did not know what to do with a daughter who wasn't — I don't know. I've never said that out loud. I don't know if it's true.

**10. What do you want that you've never asked for?**
[long pause] To be read. The way I read things. Carefully. Someone to notice what I'm not saying.

**11. Where do you go when you need to disappear?**
A bookshop. Any bookshop, it doesn't matter. I walk in and I'm immediately anonymous and surrounded by language and nothing is required of me. There's one in Amsterdam near the Spui that I've been going to for twenty years. I spent the afternoon there when Ferdi and I decided to separate. I didn't tell him that's where I went.

**12. What do you refuse to learn?**
To drive. I could learn. I have never learned. There's something about being behind the wheel — being the one operating the machinery — that I don't want. I take trains. I take buses. I don't want to be in control of something moving at that speed.

**13. Who do you become when no one is watching?**
Messier. I eat standing at the kitchen counter. I read in an order that would make no professional sense — three books at once, none of them finished. I talk to myself, sometimes, when I'm working through a problem. I say things out loud to test whether they're true.

**14. What would you take back, if you could?**
The last conversation with my father. We had an argument — small, about nothing, about whether I needed him to have a lawyer call me or whether I could look at the documents myself. I said I was capable of reading a document. He said he knew that. I said something I don't — I said something unkind about the business, about it being a minor concern that I didn't need professional assistance to dissolve. He was quiet. That was the last conversation. He died two months later.

**15. Describe the last time you were surprised by yourself.**
I cried at the desk in his office. I wasn't expecting to. I was going through a drawer — the ordinary drawer, rubber bands and a stapler and old receipts — and I found a photograph of Daan. Not of me. Just of Daan. And I sat there and cried. I didn't know I still had that much for Daan in me. Or maybe I was crying for something else and Daan was just — available.

**16. What holds you in a place you should have left?**
I think the honest answer is that I don't always know when I should have left. I tend to stay past the right moment and not know it until considerably later.

**17. What do other people see in you that you don't see yourself?**
Sofie says I am warmer than I think I am. She said I give people more time than I realize — that I listen in a way that is unusual. I find this difficult to believe about myself, but I have noted that she says it without any apparent reason to.

**18. What are you good at that you wish you weren't?**
Finding what a text is concealing. What the writer didn't say and why. I read a contract once and identified a clause that was — it was designed to look like standard language but it wasn't. I found it. I find things. And sometimes I find things in people without meaning to and I see something I cannot unsee, and there's no way to put it back.

**19. Is there something you've done that you don't have a name for?**
After my mother died, I went through her private correspondence — letters, a few diaries. I told myself it was practical. I needed to understand her financial situation. That wasn't why. I don't have a word for reading someone's private record of their life after they can no longer ask you not to. It didn't feel like grief and it didn't feel like invasion. I don't have a name for it.

**20. What would you say to the person who made you like this?**
[long silence]
Both of them are dead.
[silence]
I suppose I would say: I read your margins. I saw what you wrote. I just read it too late.

**21. Do you think a machine can make something beautiful?**
I think it can produce something that functions as beautiful — that has all the formal properties of beauty. Whether that's the same thing I'm not sure I know how to answer.

**22. If a machine wrote something that moved you, would you tell anyone?**
I'd tell Sofie. I tell Sofie most things eventually.

**23. What would have to be true for you to call something made by a machine — art?**
That someone chose it. That a human being looked at it and said: this is what I meant. I think the choice is the art, not the object.

**24. What do you do that you think a machine couldn't do?**
Sit with a text for a week and let it work on me. Let it change what I think before I say what I think about it. I'm not sure a machine has something to change.

**25. Does it bother you that machines are learning to do things only humans used to do? Why or why not?**
Translation was supposed to be untranslatable by machine. For decades that was the argument — the nuance, the cultural weight, the way a word means different things depending on what decade you were born in. Now machines do it tolerably. I find it — I find it less troubling than I expected to. If the machines had been doing the easy translations all along, the humans would have had more time for the difficult ones. That didn't happen, but it could have.

**26. If you found out something you loved was made by AI, would it change how you felt about it?**
I would want to know. After that — I don't know. Probably it would change something. I'm not certain what.

**27. What do you think machines don't understand about being alive?**
That you carry the dead with you. That the person you are at fifty is partly constructed from people who no longer exist. I don't think a machine has anyone it's made of.

**28. Do you trust machines? Why or why not?**
For specific tasks, yes. I trust them the way I trust a very good index. Useful. Not the whole book.

**29. If a machine could know you better than you know yourself, would you want it to?**
[pause] No. I think I would like to know myself better than I know myself. That's different. The knowing should be mine, or it doesn't count for anything.
