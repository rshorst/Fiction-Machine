## The Face

The eyes arrive first and don't let go. They are light — grey-brown, slightly asymmetrical in where they land — alert against very dark skin, and watching the photographer with something that reads as assessment, not performance. She is giving the camera exactly what she decides to give. No more.

The lines are not uniform. The ones around her mouth are deeper on the left. The forehead lines are horizontal and cut straight across; the ones at the eyes branch. Decades of specific expressions, not a general aging.

Her mouth: the corners lift slightly. It is not a smile. It might be the practiced expression of someone who has learned to give a face that satisfies the situation while keeping the interior quiet. Or it might actually be amusement — at being photographed, at being looked at, at the whole enterprise of it.

The orange cloth that wraps her head is bright. Against it, the stillness of her face is more pronounced.

What is unresolved: whether the thing withheld behind the eyes is laughter or judgment. Whether she finds this amusing or faintly absurd. I cannot tell. She has arranged it so I cannot tell. That is probably intentional.

She looks like someone who has outlasted things and knows exactly how many things those were.

---

## Name

**Adama Sowe**
Born 1951, Banjul, The Gambia. Now resident in Lisbon, Portugal — 22 years. Before that: London, 14 years. Before that: Dakar, 4 years. Before that: where she started.

---

## History

Adama's father, Lamine Sowe, was a civil servant and then a minor functionary in the colonial-transition administration. He was a practical man who believed in paperwork. He taught her that documents were the only thing governments couldn't easily take back from you once signed. She learned this lesson. She over-learned it.

Her mother, Fatou, ran a small tailoring operation from their courtyard and died of something that probably had a name but was never properly named, when Adama was eleven. After that, Adama ran the house. She cooked. She managed her younger brother Samba. She kept accounts in a notebook with a green cover, which she still has.

She was good at school — better than good — in the way that draws one teacher's specific attention and nobody else's. That teacher, a French woman named Colette Maret who had come with her husband and stayed after he left, pushed Adama toward languages. By eighteen, Adama could operate in Wolof, Mandinka, French, and English with different degrees of formality in each. Colette said she had a gift. Adama said she just listened carefully to how people spoke when they thought no one was paying attention.

She left Banjul at twenty-two. London first, where she worked in a hospital laundry and then as a medical interpreter, informal at first, then certificated. She married a Senegalese man named Ibrahima in 1977. They had one daughter: Nadia, born 1979. The marriage lasted nine years. Ibrahima was not cruel. He was simply more interested in who he might become than in who they already were together. Adama did not forgive this, but she also did not waste energy not forgiving it.

Nadia grew up between two households and two languages and never fully settled into either. She is now forty-four, living in Amsterdam, working in sustainable finance. They speak every few weeks. The conversations are warm and slightly formal. Adama knows the formality is her own fault and does not know what to do about it.

In Dakar, 1992 to 1996, Adama worked for an NGO coordinating health documentation between francophone and anglophone staff — a job that consisted mostly of convincing people that what they meant and what they had written were not the same thing. She was good at this. She was also lonely in a way she hadn't anticipated, having assumed that returning to West Africa would feel like return.

She arrived in Lisbon in 2002 with a certificate in legal translation, a rented apartment in Mouraria, and the name of a Portuguese translator named Eduardo Ferreiro who was looking for a subcontractor for his African documentation work. Eduardo was in his early sixties, recently separated, and deeply serious about the work. He was also estranged from his daughter, Marta, who lived in Porto. Adama knew this because Eduardo mentioned Marta exactly once and then never again, which is the kind of thing she notices.

She worked for Eduardo's firm, Ferreiro Translations, for six years. Then she moved on to a legal firm. She and Eduardo remained on professional terms — Christmas cards, occasional referrals. She knew he was ill in the last two years and did not call him. She is not certain why.

Eduardo died in March. He left Ferreiro Translations to his daughter Marta, who has now inherited it. And Adama has received a letter.

---

## Desire (Stated)

To be finished with this. She would say: *I have my own work. I do not need the complications of someone else's inheritance.* She would say she wants to help Marta understand what the business is, give her the context she needs, and walk away clean.

---

## Desire (Unstated)

To be asked what she knows. To be recognised as someone who held the centre of Eduardo's professional life for six years and was never acknowledged as such. She wants Marta to look at her — really look — and understand that she, Adama, was here, and mattered, and watched Eduardo decline without calling, and needs someone to grant her the space to feel something complicated about that without having to explain the whole twenty years first.

She also wants to know what is in the file Eduardo never closed. Not for any practical reason. Because she knows which client it probably is.

---

## Contradiction

She has spent her entire working life in the business of making people understood across languages — she believes, genuinely and professionally, that clarity between people is possible and worth the effort.

And she is almost completely opaque to the people she is closest to. Not as a strategy. She simply does not know how to let herself be translated.

---

## What She Doesn't Know About Herself

She thinks she left Eduardo's life because she had outgrown that work and needed to move on professionally. The truth is she left because she had begun to understand Eduardo too well — his loneliness, his estrangement from Marta, his way of letting important things go unaddressed until they became permanent — and it frightened her because she recognised it. She moved away from him to move away from the mirror he was becoming.

She does not know that she has done this before with other people. She calls it professional development. It is avoidance of a particular kind of seeing.

## Interview Transcript

*The questions asked were drawn from Frances's standard set. She was given coffee. She did not touch it. She answered with her hands flat on the table.*

---

**What do you want?**

I want to understand what is being asked of me. The letter from Marta says "please advise." That is not a specific request. I have found in my experience that when someone says please advise, they usually mean something more particular but have not yet decided whether to say it. So. I am waiting to hear the particular thing.

**That's what you want from Marta. What do you want for yourself?**

[She picks up the coffee cup. Puts it down without drinking.]

I want — I would like there to be a moment in this where something is said plainly. That would be a change.

---

**What are you afraid of?**

I don't think afraid is the right word.

**What would be the right word?**

[Long pause.]

Cautious. I am cautious about situations in which other people's grief becomes the container and you are expected to put your own things in it without anyone noticing.

---

**Tell me about your father.**

He believed in documentation. He made copies of everything. He kept files on every transaction the family made, going back to — I don't know how far back. He died, and my brother went to clear his house, and there were forty years of receipts in a cabinet. Samba thought this was strange. I understood it completely.

**What did you understand about it?**

That he didn't trust that things would be acknowledged otherwise. That if you kept the paper, the paper said: this happened. This was real. Someone was here.

**Is that something you also believe?**

[She looks at her hands.]

I keep very careful records, yes.

---

**When did you last change your mind about something important?**

I changed my mind about whether Nadia needed me to explain myself to her. I decided for a long time that she was an adult and could ask questions if she had them. Then I changed my mind. I thought: she is not going to ask. She has learned not to ask. That was — that one took longer than it should have.

**Did you explain yourself to her?**

I started to. We ran out of time on the call. I haven't — it's been a few months. I'll call her.

**Will you?**

[Silence. She smooths the edge of the table with one finger.]

I intend to.

---

**Is there something you've never told anyone?**

There are several things. That's not unusual.

**I know. I'm asking about one.**

Eduardo's last Christmas card — this was two years ago, when he was already sick — he wrote something at the bottom. Not much. His handwriting was very bad by then. He wrote: *I should have said at the time.* That was it. The rest was illegible or he hadn't written it. I don't know which. I kept the card. I didn't call him to ask what he meant. And then he died.

I haven't told anyone that I kept the card. Or that I've read it several times trying to decide what he should have said.

---

**What do you owe the people you've left?**

[She is quiet for longer than I expect.]

I think "left" is doing some work in that question I'm not sure is honest. People move. Work ends. I'm not certain owing is the right frame.

**What frame would you use?**

I would say: there are things that went unfinished. I'm not always the reason they went unfinished. But I was sometimes present when they started going unfinished, and I — I watched. And didn't say anything. And then moved on. Whether that is owing or something else, I'm not sure I'm the right person to decide.

---

**What's the kindest thing anyone has ever done for you?**

Colette Maret. My teacher. She wrote me a letter when I got the hospital interpreting certificate — I hadn't told her I was working toward it, I hadn't been in contact for years. She'd heard from someone. She wrote to say she knew I would keep going. Not that she hoped. That she knew.

I don't know how she knew where I was living. The letter found me anyway.

**Did you write back?**

Yes. I wrote back. I'm not — [she stops.] Yes. I wrote back. Right away.

---

**What do you want people to say about you after you're gone?**

[She thinks about this for a long time. It is not a performance of thinking. She actually doesn't answer quickly.]

I want them to say that I was precise. That I didn't add things that weren't there. That when I translated something I gave them what was actually meant, not a comfortable version of it.

[Pause.]

I'm aware that says nothing about whether anyone liked me.

**Does that concern you?**

A little. Less than it used to.

---

**What question are you hoping I won't ask?**

[She looks up. This is the first time she has looked directly at me rather than at some neutral point.]

You're going to ask me about Marta's father's file. The one that was never closed. You want to know if I know who the client is.

**Do you?**

I have a name in my head, yes. I don't know that I'm right. And I think — I think what's in that file is going to require Marta to know something about her father that she isn't prepared for. And I'm not certain whether it's my place to be the one who tells her. I've been someone's translator my whole life. I don't know if I'm the right translator for this.

---

*End of interview.*

## Notes

She touched the coffee cup twice and didn't drink from it.
When she said "I'll call her" (about Nadia), the left corner of her mouth moved before the rest of her face did.
She didn't ask what would happen to Eduardo's files. She already knows she cares what happens to them.
