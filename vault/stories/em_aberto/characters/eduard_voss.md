## Face
The glasses are what you see first. Blue frames, bold, clearly chosen — not the glasses of a man who picks whatever is available. He is in his late 60s to mid-70s. The gray stubble is kept but not shaped; functional rather than groomed.

The eyes are oblique, slightly upward from the lens. Watchful. Not warm, not cold — assessing. These are the eyes of someone who has spent a long time reading rooms and deciding when to speak. There is intelligence in them that he has learned to keep quiet about.

The mouth is at the beginning of something — a word about to form, or one just finished. Not a smile. Not closed off. Suspended.

He is wearing suspenders. You notice them only after. There is a person in there who dresses with small, private idiosyncrasies.

What is unresolved: the tension between warmth and the habit of withholding it. A face that knows how to be kind and has learned to time the knowing carefully.

## History

Eduard Voss was born in 1952 in Bonn, West Germany, the only child of a civil servant father and a mother who had been a language teacher before the war and was a housewife after it. He grew up in a household where precision of language was considered a moral virtue. His mother corrected his grammar at the dinner table. He does not remember this as unkind.

He studied law in Cologne, specializing in international commercial law. He was not interested in courtroom work — he was interested in documents: in the architecture of contracts, in the way language creates obligation. He qualified in 1977 and joined a firm that handled cross-border commercial disputes, primarily between German and Dutch companies. This is how he came to Rotterdam in 1981 — a secondment that lasted three years and then became a life.

He met his wife Lies in Rotterdam in 1982. She was a municipal archivist. They were together for thirty-one years. She died in 2017 from cancer, a slow dying that he attended to completely — he took early semi-retirement to care for her, and he has not fully returned to full practice since. He works consultancy now, specific cases, nothing that requires him to be somewhere every day.

He met Pieter Seghers through work: Seghers had translated a series of contracts for one of Voss's clients in 1989, and did it with unusual precision — not just linguistic accuracy but awareness of the legal implications of certain phrasings. Voss noticed this. He started sending Pieter work. Over thirty years it became something that was, on his side, more than professional. They ate dinner together perhaps four times a year. They shared opinions on Dutch politics in short, clipped emails. Pieter never asked for more and Voss never offered it, and this suited them both.

The open file is Voss's. He brought the matter to Pieter eighteen months ago — a translation dispute embedded in a larger legal matter, private, sensitive. Pieter had made progress on the document — a complex set of corporate records in German that bore on a question of provenance — and then had died before completing the work. The matter has not gone away. It has, in fact, become more urgent.

Voss does not want to explain the full nature of the case to a stranger. Pieter had signed a confidentiality agreement. Pieter had also agreed, in the way of a long professional relationship, to things that were never written down. Voss believes those agreements should be honored. He is not wrong to believe this.

What he wants from Marta is the documents Pieter had — his working notes, the partial translations, his correspondence on the matter — and their return, or their destruction. What he does not want is a competent woman reading them with a literary translator's eye and asking questions.

He is not a bad man. He has a client whose interests he is protecting. He has spent forty years protecting clients' interests. The habit of it is structural now — he does not always pause to evaluate whether the protection is proportionate.

His daughter Katje lives in Berlin and calls him on Sundays. She does not know what case he is managing. She would have opinions about it.

## Desires

**Stated:** To retrieve or confirm the destruction of confidential client documents that were in Pieter Seghers's possession at the time of his death. To close the matter with discretion and without complication.

**Unstated:** To protect the last significant piece of work he and Pieter did together — to have it end cleanly, the way Pieter would have wanted it to end. He is not only protecting a client. He is honoring a friendship in the only language he knows: professional obligation. He does not want to examine this.

## Contradiction

He is a man who has devoted his career to the principle that what is written is binding and what is not written is negotiable — and the most important agreement he ever made with Pieter Seghers was never written down. He asks Marta to honor an unwritten obligation. He does not see the irony. Or he sees it and has filed it away somewhere he does not visit.

## What they don't know about themselves

He believes he is protecting his client. He is, partially. He is also trying to complete the last transaction of a friendship that he never adequately acknowledged while it was alive. He experiences the urgency he feels about the file as professional conscientiousness. It is partly that. It is partly grief, administered in the only form he has.

## Interview

**1. What did you want to be when you were eight?**
A judge. I wanted to be the person in the room who said what was true and what was not. I find this embarrassing now, in retrospect — it's a child's conception of how truth works. A judge doesn't determine what's true. A judge determines what has been proven. That distinction took me twenty years of practice to understand properly.

**2. Tell me about a time you let someone down.**
I was not present the way I should have been when my daughter was young. I was available — physically, financially — but I was working. Lies managed the daily life and I came home and was pleasant and present at weekends and I thought that was sufficient. Katje has never said this to me directly. She doesn't need to.

**3. What do you own that you would never sell?**
Lies's archive. Her professional archive — she collected duplicates of documents she found significant over her career, obscure municipal records, things she thought deserved attention. It has no monetary value. I would not part with it.

**4. Who do you call when something goes wrong?**
I work through it myself first. If I still need counsel after that, I have a colleague in Cologne — Manfred, retired now — who is reliable. I call Manfred.

**5. What do you pretend not to know?**
[pause, looking somewhere to the left]
That some of the work in this matter was not — there are aspects of the provenance question that Pieter had identified which I have chosen not to pursue aggressively. I have told myself this is because the evidence is ambiguous. It is ambiguous. But I have also not looked as hard as I might have.

**6. Describe your mother without using the word "she."**
My mother was a woman who had been trained to be precise and was given a life that required imprecision. Raising a child is not a precise business. My mother corrected my German throughout my childhood and I believe this was a form of love, or at least the form love was permitted to take in that household. My mother died in 1991. I did not grieve properly at the time and I notice this now.

**7. What are you afraid other people can see about you?**
That I have simplified myself over the years in ways that were convenient. That I am less complicated than I used to be and that the reduction was not wisdom, it was just — easier.

**8. What do you do in the first ten minutes after you wake up?**
I make coffee. I sit at the kitchen table — the same chair, Lies's chair is on the other side — and I read whatever I was reading the night before. I don't turn on my phone until the coffee is finished. I've kept that rule since Lies died.

**9. Tell me something you've never said out loud.**
I am not certain the client I am protecting deserves to be protected. I have known this for some months. I am protecting the work, I think, more than the client. The work that Pieter did was careful and good and it should not — it should be resolved correctly. I do not want it to simply disappear because the situation has become inconvenient.
[pause]
I have not said that out loud before.

**10. What do you want that you've never asked for?**
To have had one real conversation with Pieter Seghers. Not about work. We never did that. I thought we would have more time. I did not ask for it, and now the arithmetic on that decision is final.

**11. Where do you go when you need to disappear?**
I walk. Long walks, no destination. Rotterdam has good walking if you know where to go — along the water, out past the container yards, places that have no pretension to be beautiful. I walk until the problem has changed shape.

**12. What do you refuse to learn?**
I refuse to learn to use the translation software. The automated tools. Pieter argued with me about this — he said they were useful for first passes, for technical vocabulary. I told him the errors were invisible in a way that a poor human translator's errors were not. He said I was being obstinate. He may have been right. I have not changed my position.

**13. Who do you become when no one is watching?**
I eat badly. I let the apartment become untidy in specific ways — papers, books left open. And I talk to Lies, still. Not as a habit I'm unaware of. I know I'm doing it. I do it anyway.

**14. What would you take back, if you could?**
I would have told Pieter the full complexity of this matter before he was too far into it to step back. I gave him the professional version. There was a personal dimension I did not disclose. He would have wanted to know. I withheld it because I thought it would complicate things, and now it has complicated everything.

**15. Describe the last time you were surprised by yourself.**
When the lawyer called me about Pieter's death, I sat down on the floor. Not on a chair — on the floor. I don't know why. I sat there for perhaps twenty minutes. I have not told anyone that. It seemed — disproportionate, given what we were to each other professionally. Afterward I kept thinking: was it disproportionate? And I still don't know.

**16. What holds you in a place you should have left?**
Rotterdam. I came for three years and I have been here forty. There is no reason that makes sense. Lies is buried here. Katje grew up here. Those are reasons. But I think I would have stayed anyway. I don't fully understand why.

**17. What do other people see in you that you don't see yourself?**
My colleague Manfred says I am more persuasive than I think I am — that people agree to things in my presence without fully realizing they've agreed. He means it as a compliment. I'm not certain it is one.

**18. What are you good at that you wish you weren't?**
Finding the weakness in a position. Any position — an argument, a contract, a person's reasoning. I locate the flaw immediately. This was useful in my career and it has made me — it has made conversation with my daughter difficult, sometimes. She will say something and I will have already found what is wrong with it before she has finished the sentence. I am working on the delay between finding and saying.

**19. Is there something you've done that you don't have a name for?**
I have continued to manage this case beyond the point where a reasonable professional would have passed it to someone else or withdrawn. I am doing it because of Pieter — because it was his last professional matter and I don't want it to be handled by someone who didn't know him. That is not a professional reason. I don't have a name for the obligation I feel, because the person I feel obligated to is dead and cannot release me from it.

**20. What would you say to the person who made you like this?**
My mother is dead. My father is dead. Lies is dead. The people who made me like this are comprehensively unavailable.
[pause]
I would say: the precision was useful. Thank you for the precision. I would ask about the rest of it — whether the rest of it was necessary. But I expect the answer would be that it was all one thing and you cannot have part of it.

**21. Do you think a machine can make something beautiful?**
Beauty is not my area. I can say that a machine can produce a legally precise document. Whether that constitutes beauty depends on how much you care about legal precision, and I am aware my answer to that is unusual.

**22. If a machine wrote something that moved you, would you tell anyone?**
I would tell Katje. I'd tell her partly because she would find it interesting and partly because I would need to tell someone and she is who I have.

**23. What would have to be true for you to call something made by a machine — art?**
[long pause] I don't know enough about art to answer that question responsibly.

**24. What do you do that you think a machine couldn't do?**
Take forty years of professional experience and a specific knowledge of one dead man's habits and use that to determine what he would have done and what he would have wanted done in his absence. A machine doesn't have the one dead man.

**25. Does it bother you that machines are learning to do things only humans used to do? Why or why not?**
In translation, yes, it concerns me for the reasons I mentioned — invisible errors. In law, the risk is different. A machine can find precedent. It cannot yet understand what a particular judge will do with it. Though I expect that will come.

**26. If you found out something you loved was made by AI, would it change how you felt about it?**
I would want to think about it carefully before I answered that. Which probably means yes.

**27. What do you think machines don't understand about being alive?**
That you spend much of it managing what you owe people and being unsure whether you've paid or not. I don't think a machine has debts of that kind.

**28. Do you trust machines? Why or why not?**
For specific, defined tasks with clear parameters, yes. Outside those parameters, no. That is also, incidentally, my answer about most people.

**29. If a machine could know you better than you know yourself, would you want it to?**
No. Not because I am certain I would dislike what it found. Because the process of not fully knowing oneself is — it leaves room for things to be otherwise. If a machine told me definitively what I was, I think I would find it very hard to continue being uncertain in the ways I currently am. And I think the uncertainty is doing something.
