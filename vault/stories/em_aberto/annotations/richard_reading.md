## Richard's reading

**Em Aberto, Draft 01 — what the draft made**

The draft became something I didn't expect from the seed. The seed promised an inheritance plot — woman gets business, finds secret. What the draft actually made is a story about the architecture of deliberate not-knowing. That is more interesting. Marta is not discovering her father's secret. She is discovering the structure he built to preserve it — the unlabeled keys, the spine-down folder, the unsent letter, the photograph with the sentence on the back. He constructed an elaborate system for holding something open. She is not inheriting a translation business. She is inheriting an open question her father kept alive for forty years and left facing the wrong direction.

---

**What the draft knows**

The draft knows that the central subject is transmission — what passes from one person to another, what gets lost in the channel, what the medium does to the content without meaning to. This is present everywhere without being named: the translation business, the Hungarian that runs out at one word, the German she reads fluently, the photograph caption in the language her mother used in the argument she overheard at eight. The draft is not writing about translation as metaphor. It is writing about what language actually is — a container that leaks, a system that transforms what it carries. Marta is trying to read her father through materials he assembled across four languages and forty years. The inheritance is the problem of reading itself.

The draft also knows something about the ethics of custody. When Renáta hands over the keys "with the efficient tenderness of someone transferring custody of a child they had raised and were now required to give back" — that is a precisely observed sentence. Renáta gave twenty years to this office and has to hand it to someone who gave nothing to it yet. The tenderness is real. The efficiency is the only dignity she has left. That is character understood from the outside in.

---

**What is emerging in the material (not themes to illustrate — things already there)**

*The managed not-knowing.* Marta catches herself reading her father's translator's notes "too carefully and then — once she noticed she was doing it — not reading at all." She chose to stop. She made it a decision rather than an emotion. This is how she moves through the whole draft: touching something that matters, then withdrawing before it can change her. The photograph face-down in the drawer — she turns it over, it's herself at eight, she turns it back. The word in Hungarian she can't translate — she holds it, decides it's significant, but does not look it up. She is doing what her father did. She is choosing to keep the file open rather than close it. The draft found this parallel without stating it. It should trust it.

*The layers of language as layers of access.* Klaus's Hungarian is "more formal, older in register" than Marta's — she can feel the difference without being able to cross it. Her Hungarian is her mother's Hungarian, private-room Hungarian. Her father's Hungarian came from somewhere else and she can read around the edge of it but not into the center. The bureaucratic compound noun she doesn't know is the exact shape of what she can't reach. The draft is wise not to name the word. The word being unnamed is the point.

*The question of deliberate versus inadvertent drift.* Miranda's deposit puts pressure on this: did Klaus deceive the client, or was the translation simply what it was — technically correct and spiritually wrong, a system that drifted toward what it was actually optimizing for without knowing it? The draft doesn't engage this directly and should not. But the unsent letter — "I cannot, in good conscience, complete the work as commissioned" — is a man who knew. Who knew for twenty years, then forty, and kept the file open instead of closing it or destroying it. That is not drift. That is decision held in permanent suspension. The draft has this but doesn't stay with it long enough.

**The untranslatable thing**

*Nem felejtettem el.* I have not forgotten.

Her father wrote this on the back of a photograph of a man and put it at the bottom of a file he kept for forty years. This is the untranslatable thing in the story — not the Hungarian phrase itself (Marta knows this one; her mother used it in an argument when Marta was eight), but what it means that her father used it. What he was doing when he wrote it. Was it a promise to Tibor Szabó? A confession? A self-accusation? An explanation to whoever would eventually find the folder — meaning Marta, meaning now? He knew someone would find it. He re-labeled the folder in recent ink. He placed the photograph at the bottom. He arranged the finding. This sentence on the back of the photograph is not private. It was addressed, in advance, to the person who would come to this desk and open this drawer. It was addressed to Marta. She has not yet understood this. The draft hasn't yet let her understand it. That gap is the right gap to leave open.

**Where it settled too early**

"The office smelled like an office." — This is a good opening move, the reversal of expectation (she wanted pipe tobacco and atmosphere; she got toner). But then: "Pipe tobacco is what she had wanted." That sentence does the work the reader should be doing. The wanting was already communicated by the contrast. We didn't need to be told she wanted it.

The Voss phone call goes too quickly. He has been in Lisbon since yesterday. He wanted to give her time to find it on her own. This is the moment with the most pressure in the draft and the draft moves through it in six exchanges. Voss says "I knew your father" and Marta says "I'm aware that people knew my father" — that line is sharp and right. But what Voss's voice is, what it does to the air in the room, how Marta is sitting when she hears it — we don't have this. The draft was moving toward the end when it should have stopped and stayed.

"Something moved in her chest at this — not quite anger, not quite recognition. Something in between that she didn't have a name for in any of her languages." — This is reaching for resonance. It is trying to be the thing instead of showing the thing. The image that earned it hasn't been built yet. What actually moved in her? What is the specific physical fact? The draft says *something* and then says she couldn't name it, and names it anyway as a felt state. Cut back to the physical.

The final paragraphs — her thinking about whether the unknown word will change what she's reading or just fill a gap — this is too explicit about its own stakes. The draft is telling us what the story is about. The writing is better when it just shows Marta sitting with the folder, showing what she does, and lets us feel the stakes ourselves.

"The spine was facing up." — This final image is earned. The inversion from how she found it is real. Keep this.

---

**What arrived unexpectedly and should be trusted**

"Pigeons were happening to each other." — The draft found this. It is not a simile, not a description — it is a true observation. Pigeons don't exist to each other, they happen to each other. The draft returns to the pigeons twice. The second time: "Outside the window a pigeon achieved something." This is even better. These are not decorative birds. They are a register — the city continuing its business while inside this room a woman handles materials that have been waiting forty years. The contrast is accurate. Trust the pigeons.

The photograph face-down in the drawer. This arrived as a specific detail. The draft gives it one line. It deserves more — not explanation, but duration. She picks it up. It's herself at eight. She turns it back. She closes the drawer. The draft moves past this in four sentences because it was afraid of it. The fear is the right instinct. Stay with it.

Renáta's pause on the phone — "Not the quiet of someone thinking. The quiet of someone deciding." — This is good observation. This is the voices corpus's way of attending to what isn't said. The draft earned this and then moved on. Renáta is carrying something too. What has she protected for twenty years without knowing what it was? The draft doesn't need to answer this. But Marta should feel it — the specific texture of twenty years of loyal not-asking.

The detail that her father re-labeled the folder in recent ink. "He had taken it out. He had looked at it, at some point in the last ten years. He had re-labeled it. He had put it back, spine-down." — This is the most important thing in the draft. A man keeping a file open, returning to it, re-labeling it — these are deliberate acts. This is not accumulated negligence. This is a maintained relationship with an unfinished thing. The draft has this but it is stated more than felt. The second draft should let Marta sit with the ink longer.

---

**What connects the characters they cannot see themselves**

Marta and her father are both engaged in managed not-knowing. She doesn't look up the word. She doesn't read the translator's notes fully once she catches herself caring. She closes the drawer with the photograph. He never sent the letter. He kept the file open. He put it spine-down. Both of them are preserving an opening rather than forcing a closure. The draft has this structural rhyme but hasn't yet pressed on it. The second draft should let it breathe.

Marta and Voss are both in Lisbon for the same reason, both arrived around the same time, both waiting for the other to make contact. He waited; she waited. She called first. He already had the address. The draft registers this as slightly sinister — the surveillance quality of him knowing she was there. But it is also grief. He lost someone who mattered to him and he is behaving the way people behave when something is at stake: careful, strategic, more careful than his feelings want him to be. That phrase is in the draft: "he paused the way people pause when they are being more careful than their feelings want them to be." This is the draft's best observation about Voss and it arrives in one clause and disappears. Stay with it.

---

**Where the draft trusts itself**

The draft trusts itself with objects. The key ring, the unlabeled keys, the face-down photograph, the spine-down folder, the receipt, the dead batteries, the re-labeled tab — these are not symbols. They are facts about a man and what he kept and how he kept it. Trust this inventory. The character of Klaus Voss is built entirely from the physical evidence he left behind, and the draft never breaks from that method to explain him. This is the right method. Keep it.

The draft trusts itself with Marta's control. She rehearsed her feelings once, on the plane, and told herself carefully. She withdraws from what she starts to feel before it can arrive. This is a woman who manages herself the way her father managed his files. The draft found this without stating it. Do not state it in the second draft.

The draft trusts the ending — the folder on the desk in the dark, spine facing up. Keep it exactly.
