# Em Aberto
### First Draft — Richard

*Seed: A woman inherits her estranged father's translation business and finds a client file he never closed.*

---

The office smelled like an office.

Marta stood in the doorway for a moment — not waiting, she told herself, just orienting — and then stepped inside. Pipe tobacco was what she had wanted. That particular paper smell and something else she couldn't name, the specific atmosphere of a man who had worked in the same room for thirty years. She had rehearsed this on the flight from Cologne. She had been very careful to only rehearse it once.

What she got instead: dust, and under the dust the neutral chemical smell of toner cartridges, and under that something faintly mildewed from the bathroom she hadn't yet opened. Three hours on the plane and forty minutes on the metro for this: a room with grey carpet and two windows facing a courtyard where pigeons were happening to each other.

She put her bag on the desk.

The desk was Klaus's. She knew it from a photograph — the one photograph she had of the office, taken in 1994 for some kind of directory listing, her father standing slightly to the left of center looking toward but not quite at the camera. He had never been comfortable being looked at directly. She had not inherited this. She had inherited other things.

The filing cabinets were along the left wall. Four of them, industrial, pale grey going to cream at the edges where the paint had worn. One drawer hung slightly open, empty. The rest were closed. On top of the nearest cabinet sat a key ring — she had been told about this by Renáta, the office manager who had been with her father for twenty years and who had handed Marta the ring at the airport with the efficient tenderness of someone transferring custody of a child they had raised and were now required to give back. The ring was large. Thirty-seven keys, Renáta had said. None of them labeled.

Marta picked up the key ring.

It was heavier than she expected. She held it for a moment, getting the weight of it. Thirty-seven keys for a business this size meant one of two things: her father had kept adding locks as the years went on, finding new things to secure, or he had never thrown away a key in his life and some of these opened nothing anymore — doors that had been replaced, drawers that no longer existed, a lock somewhere in Portugal she would never find. A man who keeps every key even after the lock is gone. She had not thought about her father in these terms before.

She tried three keys in the nearest cabinet before she found the right one.

---

The active client files were in alphabetical order, and tidy — this was Renáta's work, probably, or Renáta's standards preserved across decades. Marta worked through them methodically: Alvarez, Banco de, Castelo, Dias, Elias. The work was technical for the most part. Engineering documents. Legal contracts, the dry machinery of international commerce. A few literary items, smaller, a translator's note clipped to each one in her father's small cursive that she found herself reading too carefully and then — once she noticed she was doing it — not reading at all.

She had made a list on the flight. Thirty-nine active contracts. She would need to contact each client. She would need to assess which jobs could be reassigned and which would require closure. Renáta had two colleagues, both freelance, neither of whom had been told yet that the business might not continue. There were decisions Marta was not going to be able to defer much longer.

She worked for two hours. She opened every drawer in sequence. She found a receipt for a restaurant she didn't recognize, a pencil sharpener shaped like a globe, three dead batteries in a row as if arranged, a box of paper clips that had been unopened long enough for the cardboard to have gone soft. She found a framed photograph face-down in the bottom drawer of the second cabinet, and she turned it over and it was herself at eight years old, standing in front of a harbour, and she turned it back over and put it in the drawer and closed the drawer.

She worked through lunch.

---

The third cabinet was the one she'd been avoiding, not consciously. Consciously she had simply been working in order. But her body knew: she had slowed down as she approached it. She had refilled her water glass and stood at the window for a moment watching the pigeons and then come back and opened it.

The filing was different here. Older. The folders were a slightly different shade, manila going yellow at the tabs, and they were arranged differently — not alphabetical but something else, some organizing principle she didn't immediately recognize. She went through them carefully. Old contracts, many of them closed and stamped FINALIZÁDO in red ink. Technology clients from the eighties, German engineering firms. She understood the logic now: this was the archive. Twenty years of completed work, organized by decade and then by some secondary system she couldn't quite parse.

At the back of the bottom drawer was a folder that had been placed spine-down.

She nearly didn't notice. The other folders were upright, readable by their tabs. This one was horizontal, faced away, tucked at the back. You would have to lift it out to read it. You would have to make a choice.

The folder was thick — three centimeters at least, maybe four. The paper inside had the compressed density of something that had been accumulating for a long time.

She lifted it out.

The tab said: *SZABÓ / T. — EM ABERTO.*

*In progress.* Or: *open.* The Portuguese was ambiguous. She had known Portuguese for years; she had never told her father this, which was a fact she was not going to examine right now.

She put the folder on the desk.

---

She called Renáta.

"The Szabó file," she said. "T. Szabó. It's in the archive cabinet, but it's marked in progress. Is there a — do you know the account?"

Renáta was quiet for a moment. Not the quiet of someone thinking. The quiet of someone deciding.

"That one was from before my time," Renáta said. "From Budapest. Before your father moved the business to Lisbon."

"Budapest."

"He never spoke about it. I asked once. He said it was complicated." A pause. "I asked Filomena and she didn't know either. We agreed to leave it."

"How long has it been in there?"

"I don't know. Before 2001, I think. Possibly much earlier." Another pause, and then: "Marta. I should tell you. A man called last week. He's called twice now. A lawyer, he said. He wanted to know about the estate and whether the active files were being — whether they were being accessed."

"Did he give a name?"

"Voss."

Marta held the phone. Outside the window a pigeon achieved something.

"He said Voss."

"Eduard Voss. He left a number."

She wrote the number on the back of an envelope. She thanked Renáta. She ended the call.

The folder sat in the middle of the desk. The label: *SZABÓ / T. — EM ABERTO.* Her father's handwriting, but the ink was newer than the folder — he had re-labeled it, at some point, in a pen he'd been using recently, within the last decade maybe. The folder was old. The label was not.

Which meant: he had taken it out. He had looked at it, at some point in the last ten years. He had re-labeled it. He had put it back, spine-down.

She opened it.

---

Inside: layers. This was the first impression — sediment, the kind that forms when something accumulates over years without being disturbed. At the top, a letter in German, one page, from someone called Voss & Partner, dated 2003. Below that, a sheaf of documents in Hungarian. Then more documents in German. Then photocopies, a different texture — older, some of them, the tonal range of photocopiers from the early nineties that left a shadow around the text. And underneath all of this, at the bottom, a handwritten document in Hungarian that had been paper-clipped to a photograph.

She read the 2003 letter first. Her German was fluent.

*Dear Mr. Voss,* it began — it was from her father to the other Voss, then — *I am writing to inform you that I cannot, in good conscience, complete the work as commissioned. The documents I have been asked to translate contain —*

The sentence stopped. This was a draft. Not sent. The second paragraph began: *I have been in possession of these materials for nineteen years.* A correction: *twenty years.* The word *nineteen* crossed out, *twenty* written above it. And then: *I believe you understand why I am not able simply to —*

Another stop. Another crossed-out line. The paragraph began a third time: *Tibor Szabó trusted me with —*

And then nothing. The draft ended in the middle of the sentence.

She set the letter down.

She picked up the Hungarian documents. Her Hungarian was her mother's Hungarian — Hamburg-inflected, the Hungarian of a woman who had come to Germany at twenty-two and kept the language like a private room she entered alone. Her father's Hungarian was different. She could feel the difference as she read. More formal. Older in register, a bureaucratic cadence she recognized from documents she had read years ago when she was trying to understand something about the period and had found only more things she didn't understand.

The documents were from 1981. A translation contract, in the standard form. Client: *Tibor Szabó, Mérnök* — Tibor Szabó, engineer. The work to be translated: technical specifications for industrial equipment. Standard. And then a second document, attached, in a different hand: a set of notes, not a formal document, just notes on paper that had gone cream with age. At the top: *Kedves Klaus —*

*Dear Klaus.* Not *Dear Mr. Voss.* Friends, then, or something like friends.

She read the notes slowly. Her Hungarian moved carefully through the cadence of the older language, feeling for footholds. Most of it she understood. There was a term she encountered twice that she didn't know — a bureaucratic compound noun she had no referent for, something from the administrative vocabulary of a specific place and time that she had never needed and that no one had taught her. The place where her inheritance ran out.

She stopped at this word both times. She held it. She did not know what it meant and she could not look past it — it sat in the middle of the sentence like a locked room, like a drawer she didn't have the key for, and she was aware, sitting here in the Lisbon office of her dead father, that this was probably funny in some way she couldn't access yet.

She kept reading.

What she understood, from what she could understand: Tibor Szabó had asked her father to translate something more than industrial specifications. What the something more was — this she couldn't yet see. But the notes had the quality of instruction, and the instruction had a quality of precaution, and the precaution had a quality of fear. Not dramatic fear. The contained, ongoing, administrative fear of someone navigating a system that was watching.

*You will understand why I cannot put this in the formal contract,* Tibor Szabó had written. *But I need you to know what you are carrying.*

She put the document down.

She picked up the photograph.

It was black and white, small, the size of a passport photo. A man she didn't recognize — fifties, probably, a broad face, glasses with heavy frames. On the back, in her father's writing: *T.S. 1984.* And below that, smaller: *nem felejtettem el.*

She knew this phrase. This one she had. She had learned this one, years ago, from her mother, who had used it in an argument, the specific argument she had with Marta's father when Marta was eight and had not understood what was being said but had heard the weight of it and memorized the phrase the way children memorize things that feel important before they understand why.

*I have not forgotten.*

Her father had written this on the back of a photograph of Tibor Szabó and placed it at the bottom of a folder he had kept for — she looked at the 1981 date again — forty years. He had not forgotten. He had placed a sentence at the bottom of something he then placed spine-down in a drawer he gave thirty-seven keys to access and left not one of them labeled.

She sat with this for a moment.

Then she picked up the phone and called the number Renáta had given her.

---

It rang four times. A man answered. His voice was measured, professional, with an accent she couldn't immediately place — German, but attenuated by something else.

"Voss."

"This is Marta Voss," she said. "I'm Klaus Voss's daughter. I'm calling from the Lisbon office." She paused. "I've found the Szabó file."

A silence on the other end. Not the silence of someone surprised. The silence of someone who has been waiting for a specific thing to happen and is now noting that it has happened.

"Yes," he said. "I see."

"You've called twice," she said. "I'd like to understand what your interest is."

"My interest is legal," he said. "And also —" Another pause. She noticed that he paused the way people pause when they are being more careful than their feelings want them to be. "Also personal. I knew your father."

"I'm aware that people knew my father," she said. "That's not what I asked."

He was quiet for a moment. Then: "The documents in that file — some of them have consequences that are still current. For a person who is still living. I need to know that they will be handled correctly."

"Handled how."

"With discretion," he said. "And with — understanding of what they represent."

She looked at the folder in front of her. The unsent draft of a letter. The bureaucratic Hungarian she couldn't read. The photograph. The sentence on the back.

"I can't tell you yet whether I understand what they represent," she said. "I've been reading for an hour."

"I know."

"You've known I was here today?"

"I've been in Lisbon since yesterday," he said. "I wanted to give you time to find it on your own."

Something moved in her chest at this — not quite anger, not quite recognition. Something in between that she didn't have a name for in any of her languages.

"I'd like to meet," she said.

"Yes," he said. "I think that's necessary."

She looked at the photograph again. The man in the heavy glasses. The back of the photograph and her father's writing. She was not going to be able to close this file today. She understood this now with the particular clarity of someone who has just finished making a decision they had already, without knowing it, made.

"Tomorrow morning," she said. "Here."

She gave him the address. He already had it, she could tell from how quickly he said yes. She put the phone down.

Outside, the light over the courtyard had shifted. It was late afternoon now. The pigeons were gone or had become still. She didn't know which.

She picked up the 1981 contract again and found the word she didn't know and looked at it for a long time. She was trying to decide whether it was the kind of word that, once you knew it, would change what you were reading — or the kind that would just fill in a gap, leaving everything essentially as it was. She had worked with enough texts to know that it was sometimes possible to tell the difference in advance. Sometimes you could feel the word waiting to change you.

She thought this was that kind.

She did not look it up. Not yet. She set the document down with the others and closed the folder and left it in the center of the desk, and took her coat from the chair where she'd put it that morning, and stood for a moment in the room that smelled like an office and not like her father, and then she turned off the light and went out.

The folder was still on the desk in the dark.

The spine was facing up.
