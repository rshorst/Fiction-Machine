# Em Aberto
### Second Draft — Richard

*Seed: A woman inherits her estranged father's translation business and finds a client file he never closed.*

---

The office smelled like an office.

Marta stood in the doorway for a moment — not waiting, she told herself, just orienting — and then stepped inside. She had rehearsed this on the flight from Cologne: the pipe tobacco she would smell, and under it something else she couldn't name in advance, the specific atmosphere of a man who had worked in the same room for thirty years. She had been very careful to rehearse it only once.

What she got: dust, and under the dust the neutral chemical smell of toner cartridges, and under that something faintly mildewed from the bathroom she hadn't opened yet. Three hours on the plane and forty minutes on the metro for this: a room with grey carpet and two windows facing a courtyard where pigeons were happening to each other.

She put her bag on the desk.

The desk was Klaus's. She knew it from a photograph — the one photograph she had of the office, taken in 1994 for some kind of directory listing, her father standing slightly to the left of center looking toward but not quite at the camera. He had never been comfortable being looked at directly. She had not inherited this. She had inherited other things, and she was here to learn what they were.

The filing cabinets were along the left wall. Four of them, industrial, pale grey going to cream at the edges where the paint had worn. One drawer hung slightly open, empty. The rest were closed. On top of the nearest cabinet sat a key ring — she had been told about this by Renáta, the office manager who had been with her father for twenty years and who had handed Marta the ring at the airport with the efficient tenderness of someone transferring custody of a child they had raised and were now required to give back. The ring was large. Thirty-seven keys, Renáta had said. None of them labeled.

Marta picked up the key ring.

It was heavier than she expected. She held it for a moment, adjusting to the weight. Thirty-seven keys for a business this size meant one of two things: her father had kept adding locks as the years went on, finding new things to secure, or he had never thrown away a key in his life and some of these opened nothing anymore — doors that had been replaced, drawers that no longer existed, a lock somewhere in Portugal she would never find. A man who keeps every key even after the lock is gone. She had not thought about her father in these terms before. She stood at the cabinet with the ring in her hand and thought about it now.

She tried three keys in the nearest cabinet before she found the right one.

---

The active client files were in alphabetical order, and tidy — this was Renáta's work, probably, or Renáta's standards preserved across decades. Marta worked through them methodically: Alvarez, Banco de, Castelo, Dias, Elias. The work was technical for the most part. Engineering documents. Legal contracts, the dry machinery of international commerce. A few literary items, smaller, a translator's note clipped to each one in her father's small cursive — she found herself reading these, then noticed she was reading them, then made a decision and stopped.

She had made a list on the flight. Thirty-nine active contracts. She would need to contact each client. She would need to assess which jobs could be reassigned and which would require closure. Renáta had two colleagues, both freelance, neither of whom had been told yet that the business might not continue. There were decisions Marta was not going to be able to defer much longer.

She worked for two hours. She opened every drawer in sequence. She found a receipt from a restaurant she didn't recognize, a pencil sharpener shaped like a globe, three dead batteries arranged in a row as if by intention. She found a box of paper clips that had been unopened long enough for the cardboard to have gone soft. She found, in the bottom drawer of the second cabinet, a framed photograph placed face-down.

She lifted it. Turned it over.

Herself at eight years old, standing in front of a harbour. The sky was the white of overexposed film. She was wearing a red anorak she had forgotten she owned. She was not looking at the camera either — she was looking at something off to the left, something outside the frame, whatever had caught her attention the moment before the shutter.

She looked at the photograph for longer than she meant to. Then she turned it face-down and put it in the drawer and closed the drawer. She stood there for a moment with her hand on the closed drawer. Then she went and refilled her water glass and stood at the window.

Outside, a pigeon achieved something. Another pigeon disputed this. The courtyard was full of the ordinary urgency of creatures with no knowledge of this room.

She went back to work.

---

The third cabinet was the one she'd been avoiding, not consciously. Consciously she had simply been working in order. But she had slowed as she approached it — she had refilled her water glass, she had stood at the window — and now she pulled out the key ring again and tried four keys before she found the right one. The lock turned with a heaviness that suggested it was used less often than the others.

The filing was different here. Older. The folders were a slightly different shade, manila going yellow at the tabs, and they were arranged differently — not alphabetical but something else, some organizing principle she didn't immediately recognize. She went through them carefully. Old contracts, many of them closed and stamped FINALIZÁDO in red ink. Technology clients from the eighties, German engineering firms. She understood the logic now: this was the archive. Twenty years of completed work, organized by decade and then by some secondary system she couldn't quite parse.

At the back of the bottom drawer, a folder that had been placed spine-down.

She nearly didn't notice. The other folders were upright, readable by their tabs. This one was horizontal, faced away, tucked against the back wall of the drawer. To read it you would have to lift it out. You would have to make a choice.

The folder was thick — three centimeters at least, maybe four. The paper inside had the compressed density of something that had been accumulating for a long time.

She held it without opening it. She could feel, through the cardboard, the slight differences in paper stock — smooth against textured against the glazed surface of what might be photographs. Her father had put his hand on this the same way, at some point in the last ten years. He had lifted it out. She looked at the tab.

*SZABÓ / T. — EM ABERTO.*

*In progress.* Or: *open.* The Portuguese was ambiguous — she had known Portuguese for years and had never told her father this, which was a fact she was not going to examine right now.

She carried the folder to the desk and put it down.

She looked at the label more carefully. The tab itself was old — the same aged manila as the folder. But the label was recent. The ink was still dark, the edges of the letters clean. She compared it to the label on the folder she'd set down beside it: older ink, slightly faded, the letters wider and more pressured, the handwriting of a younger man. This label had been written by an older man. It had been written within the last decade, maybe less.

He had taken it out. He had read it, or held it, or looked at the label and decided not to read it. Then he had written a new label and put it back. He had put it back spine-down.

She did not know what this meant. She opened it.

---

Inside: layers. This was the first impression — sediment, the kind that forms when something accumulates over years without being disturbed and then is disturbed and resealed and left again. At the top, a letter in German, one page, from someone called Voss & Partner, dated 2003. Below that, a sheaf of documents in Hungarian. Then more documents in German. Then photocopies, a different texture — older, some of them, the tonal range of photocopiers from the early nineties that left a shadow around the text. And underneath all of this, at the bottom, a handwritten document in Hungarian that had been paper-clipped to a photograph.

She read the 2003 letter first. Her German was fluent — it was the first language she had worked in professionally, the language she used for contracts and depositions and the careful machinery of legal meaning.

*Dear Mr. Voss,* it began — it was from her father to the other Voss, then — *I am writing to inform you that I cannot, in good conscience, complete the work as commissioned. The documents I have been asked to translate contain —*

The sentence stopped. This was a draft, not a sent letter. No salutation at the top to indicate it had been received anywhere. The second paragraph began: *I have been in possession of these materials for nineteen years.* A correction: *twenty years.* The word *nineteen* crossed out, *twenty* written above it in the same pen, the same session. And then: *I believe you understand why I am not able simply to —*

Another stop. Another crossed-out line. The paragraph began a third time: *Tibor Szabó trusted me with —*

And then nothing. The draft ended there, in the middle of a sentence about trust.

She set it down. She sat with the question of when he had written it: 2003, the letter's date, but 2003 was the year he had stopped trying to write it. He had begun the sentence about trust and then he had stopped and put the folder in the drawer and, at some point in the years after, taken it back out to re-label it and then put it back. He had maintained the file for two more decades after the letter he could not finish.

She picked up the Hungarian documents. Her Hungarian was her mother's Hungarian — Hamburg-inflected, the Hungarian of a woman who had left Budapest at twenty-two and kept the language like a private room she entered alone, never with guests. Her father's Hungarian was different. She could feel the difference as she read: more formal, older in register, a bureaucratic cadence she recognized from documents she had read years ago when she was trying to understand something about a specific period and had found only more things she didn't understand. His Hungarian had been formed somewhere and by something she had no access to.

The documents were from 1981. A translation contract, standard form. Client: *Tibor Szabó, Mérnök* — Tibor Szabó, engineer. The work to be translated: technical specifications for industrial equipment. Routine. And then a second document, attached by a rusted paper clip, in a different hand: notes on paper that had gone cream with age, not a formal document, just notes, the handwriting of someone writing quickly or under some pressure she couldn't see. At the top: *Kedves Klaus —*

*Dear Klaus.* Not *Dear Mr. Voss.* The form of address that crosses a threshold.

She read the notes slowly. Her Hungarian moved carefully through the older register, feeling for footholds. Most of it she understood — the syntax was formal but not inaccessible, the vocabulary recognizable if not always the sense. There was a term she encountered twice that stopped her: a bureaucratic compound noun she had no referent for, something from the administrative vocabulary of a specific place and time she had never needed to enter. The word sat in the middle of each sentence like a locked room.

She held it the first time she encountered it. Tried to parse the compound — the constituent parts were familiar words, ordinary nouns yoked together into something technical and period-specific that she couldn't reconstruct from the parts. She read around it and continued. The second time she encountered it she stopped longer. The word was not incidental — it appeared in the key sentence of the paragraph, the sentence that probably told her what Tibor Szabó had actually been asking Klaus to do. The place where her inheritance ran out.

She had worked with enough texts to know: sometimes a single term changes everything. Sometimes it just fills a gap and leaves everything as it was. She thought she could sometimes tell the difference in advance. She thought this was that kind.

She did not look it up. She set the document down with the others and kept reading.

What she understood, from what she could understand: Tibor Szabó had asked her father to translate something more than industrial specifications. The notes had the quality of instruction. The instruction had the quality of precaution. The precaution had the quality of fear — not dramatic fear, not the fear of someone facing something imminent, but the contained and habituated fear of someone navigating a system that was watching everything with patience and no particular hurry.

*You will understand why I cannot put this in the formal contract,* Tibor Szabó had written. *But I need you to know what you are carrying.*

She read the sentence twice. Then she read it a third time and this time she felt it differently — not as a statement to her father but as a statement to her. He had known someone would eventually sit at this desk and open this drawer. He had been writing to that person. He could not have known it would be a woman forty years later who had learned his language from a woman who had taken it with her out of the country he'd been navigating. He had written anyway.

She put the document down and picked up the photograph.

Black and white, passport-sized. A man she didn't recognize: fifties, probably, a broad face, the face of someone who had kept himself in good condition and knew it provided a kind of protection. Glasses with heavy frames, the frames of someone who wore glasses not for aesthetics but because everything was slightly wrong without them. He was looking directly at the camera, directly at whoever stood behind it. He was not uncomfortable being looked at. He had decided not to be.

On the back, in her father's writing: *T.S. 1984.* And below that, smaller, in a hand that was slightly less controlled — the hand of someone writing something that cost them: *nem felejtettem el.*

She knew this phrase. This one she had. She had learned it years ago from her mother, who had used it in an argument — the argument with her father when Marta was eight, the one she hadn't understood except in its outline: two adults in a kitchen, voices that stayed low in the controlled way of people who knew there was a child in the next room, and then her mother's voice saying this phrase in Hungarian, and then a door closing. Not slammed. Closed with precision, which was worse.

*I have not forgotten.*

She turned the photograph over. She looked at his face — Tibor Szabó, engineer, 1984, dead or alive she didn't know — and then she turned it back and looked at the sentence again.

Her father had written this in the year Tibor Szabó had presumably given him the photograph to keep. Or later — the ink was impossible to date precisely. He had written it and placed it at the bottom of the folder. He had kept the folder for forty years. He had come back to it, in the last decade, to re-label it in fresh ink, and he had put it back with the spine facing away, with this photograph at the bottom, with this sentence on the back of this photograph. He had arranged the finding. He had put it face-down and spine-down and then died and left it for whoever came to this desk.

She had been in this room for three hours.

She sat with the folder in front of her and let herself think about what it meant that she had come here at all. She had not had to. There had been other options — a lawyer could have handled the dissolution of the estate, the business could have been wound down by correspondence. She had taken the flight. She had stood in this doorway and told herself she was orienting. She had worked through the cabinets in order, telling herself she was being methodical.

She called Renáta.

---

"The Szabó file," she said. "T. Szabó. It's in the archive cabinet, but it's marked in progress. Do you know the account?"

The pause that followed was not the pause of someone thinking. It was the pause of someone deciding how much of what they had already decided to tell her. Twenty years of working for a man who kept unlabeled keys: Renáta had her own way of not-knowing by now, her own practiced relationship with things she had agreed not to press on.

"That one was from before my time," Renáta said. "From Budapest. Before your father moved the business to Lisbon."

"Budapest."

"He never spoke about it. I asked once, in the beginning." A pause. "He said it was complicated, and he said it in a way that meant he wasn't going to say more. I understood that." Another pause, and then: "Marta. I should tell you something. A man called last week. He's called twice. He said he was a lawyer. He wanted to know about the estate and whether the active files were being accessed."

"Did he give a name?"

"Voss."

Marta held the phone. Outside, a pigeon achieved something. She was aware of the weight of the folder on the desk in front of her.

"He said Voss."

"Eduard Voss. He left a number."

She wrote the number on the back of an envelope — an old envelope from the desk drawer, the kind her father had saved, the kind of person who saved envelopes. She thanked Renáta. She ended the call.
