# The Notebook

The first one came back at four-forty-seven in the morning. He read it standing in the kitchen, in a t-shirt, with the fridge humming, because his bedroom felt like a place where someone might be watching him read it. The acceptance was a paragraph long. The editor said the story had a quality of waiting in it that she had not seen done that way before. She used the word *patience* and then crossed it out — he could see the strikethrough, she had left it in — and replaced it with *refusal*. She said: *you have written a refusal that sounds like patience, and I think that is the story*.

He put the phone face down on the counter and then face up and then face down again. His mother's tin was on the shelf above the kettle. He could hear her in the next room, not yet awake, the small irregular breathing of a woman who would be up in twenty minutes to start her shift at the airport. He did not wake her. He did not wake Tahmina, who was in Brampton anyway and would not have been there to wake. He stood there until the kettle, which he had not turned on, made a sound he eventually understood was the fridge cycling.

The thing he could not say to himself, then, in the kitchen: he had recognized the sentences in the story. Not as his — as ones he had been about to write. There were three in particular. One about a man on a bus watching another man eat a samosa with one hand while holding a phone with the other; one about the way his mother folded a fitted sheet (she folded it badly, and the badness was the love); one about Anika, although the story was not about Anika and did not name her. He had drafted these sentences in his head, on the 25 Don Mills, between Eglinton and Lawrence, on a Tuesday in February. He had not written them down. He had thought: I will write them down when I get home. He had not written them down.

He had uploaded the notebook six weeks earlier. Tahmina had told him about the platform — not because she thought he was a writer, because she did not know he was a writer, but because she had read an article about it and forwarded the article to him with the message *this looks like the kind of thing you would have an opinion about*. He had read the article. The platform offered something it called "preparatory authorship." You uploaded what you had — drafts, fragments, anything — and the system worked against the corpus, overnight, on cheap compute, building out what it called "candidate continuations." When you logged in, your continuations were waiting. The article quoted a co-founder saying *we want to meet you where you would have gone*. Rashed had read this sentence twice and then signed up.

The free tier let you generate two stories from a small archive. He had uploaded the notebook — the first one, the one he kept because if he lost it he would lose proof that he started — and the eleven-month-finished story, the one he had not sent. He had uploaded them at one in the morning, with the door shut, with his headphones on for no reason, because there was no audio. The progress bar took eight minutes. At the end the system said: *Preparing. Check back tomorrow.*

He had checked back the next morning and there had been one story. He had read it on the bus. He had missed his stop. He had read it again at his desk, with his coffee going cold beside the second monitor where the credit memos lived, and at the end of it he had done the thing he had not been able to do since he was seventeen, which was to want, very specifically, to be read by a person he did not know. He had sent the story to a magazine he had read for three years and had never submitted to. He had not told anyone. Not Tahmina. Not his mother. Not the version of himself that had been waiting eleven months.

That was the first story. There was now a second.

He paid the upgrade fee on a Thursday. The fee was eighteen dollars. The platform thanked him in a small green banner that disappeared after four seconds. The next story arrived on Saturday morning, and the small literary press wrote to him on Tuesday, and by the end of the month he had said yes to a contract for a collection — eight stories, ten if he could — and he had told his sister he had been "working on something" and she had said *good for you, finally*, and he had not corrected her.

He told himself, in the kitchen, in the room he had not left in three weeks except to go to the bank, that this was what it was supposed to feel like. That the waiting had been a way of not noticing the work was already done.

---

The pricing was on the third tab of the dashboard, under *Account*, in a section called *Compute Allocation*. He had not looked at it. He had paid the upgrade and another upgrade after that — one of them was forty dollars, one of them was a hundred and twenty — and he had paid them on his debit card without examining the line items, because the platform's invoicing was clean: *Generation: Story 04. $42.50.* He could read a credit memo. He could not read this.

The bill that broke the surface was a hundred and ninety dollars for a story he had not generated. He logged in. The line item said *Discarded preparation: candidate continuations, retained 14 days.* He clicked the question mark beside it.

*Sleep-time compute is the work performed in anticipation of a request you have not yet made. We prepare a range of candidate continuations against your archive each night. When you generate, we serve the closest match. The remaining candidates are retained for a short period and then released. You are billed for the preparation of all candidates within your active tier, not only for the candidate served.*

He sat with this for a long time. The window in his bedroom faced the parking lot, and a man in a green jacket was loading boxes into the back of a Sienna, and he watched the man finish loading and close the door and stand for a moment with his hand on the door before getting into the driver's seat. Then he opened a new tab and read the question mark again.

He understood, then, that he had been paying for Rasheds. Not the one that wrote. All of them. The one that would have gone in another direction on Tuesday if he had eaten a different breakfast. The one that would have written the bus sentence and not written the sheet sentence. The one that would have written about Anika directly. The one that would have written about his father and abandoned the draft. Each night, while he was asleep, the platform was running through these Rasheds, choosing the ones it thought he would buy, holding them in case he wanted a different one, and dissolving them at the end of the fourteen-day window without his ever having met them.

He thought: I am paying for the lives I did not live.

He thought: that is dramatic. I am paying for sentences I did not write.

He could not tell which thought was his.

He paid the bill.

---

The collection's title story was not yet written. The contract had named it provisionally — *The Notebook* — and the editor had said, on the phone, *we love what we've seen, we want the title piece to be the one that holds it together*. He had said *of course* and *I'm working on it* and *give me three weeks*. He had two weeks left.

He sat down at the desk that had been Tahmina's, in the room that had been Tahmina's, with the window that faced the parking lot. He opened a blank document. He waited for a sentence.

A sentence came. It was about a notebook. It was a good first sentence — the kind he had been told, by people who had read the published stories, was a thing he was good at. It said: *The first one I filled was the one I kept; the rest I lost on purpose.*

He stopped. He read it again.

He had written something close to this in the notebook, when he was nineteen — not this sentence, but its parent. He had also seen this sentence, or one very near it, in the second story the platform had generated for him. He had also, possibly, drafted this sentence on the bus on the morning he had uploaded the notebook, although he could not now be sure if the drafting had happened before the upload or after — if it was a sentence he had carried in and not written, or one the platform had given him, or one he had thought of in response to one the platform had given him, or one the platform had given him after he had thought of it, or one that had only existed once in his head and had been retrieved by the system from the corpus of his typing on his own laptop, which the platform had asked permission to index and which he had given permission to index in a checkbox on the third page of the onboarding.

He did not know whose sentence it was.

He sat with the sentence for forty minutes. He tried to write the next one. He could not write the next one without knowing whose the first one was. He typed something, deleted it, typed something else, deleted it. The cursor blinked.

He opened the platform. He generated a candidate continuation from the first sentence, paying eighty dollars for a "deep" generation. He read the result. The result was very good. It was better than what he had been about to write, possibly — possibly — because what he had been about to write had also been the platform's, which is to say it was the same sentence twice with different friction. He could not tell.

He thought of his sister. He thought of the article she had sent him. He thought of the first acceptance email and the strikethrough on *patience*. He thought of what the editor would say if he sent her a story whose first sentence he could not locate.

He pasted the platform's continuation into the document. He kept reading. The continuation knew about the bus, and about his mother folding the sheet, and about Anika without naming her, and it knew them all in a register he had spent fifteen years not being able to find when he tried — a register that had only ever come to him at one in the morning, in his old bed, with the headphones on for no reason.

He wrote the rest of the story across two nights. Some of the sentences were typed by his hands and were arguably his. Some of them were the platform's, lightly altered. Some of them were the platform's unaltered. He could not tell, by the end, which were which, because each time he changed a word he was not sure if he was changing the platform's word into his or his into the platform's. The seam was not in the document. The seam was in him, and it had moved.

He sent the file to the editor at three in the morning. The editor wrote back at nine and said it was the best one yet.

---

The collection came out in September. He bought five copies at the launch and gave one to his mother, who put it on top of the microwave, where it stayed; one to his father, who said *acha* and put it on the shelf above the television and did not mention it again; one to his manager, who said *I had no idea*, which was the kindest thing she had ever said to him; one to himself; and one, in person, to Tahmina.

He gave Tahmina hers in her kitchen in Brampton. Her husband was in the other room watching cricket. She made tea without asking him if he wanted any. She took the book and turned it over and read the back and read the first page of the first story standing up, with one hand on the counter, the way she read everything. She read for a long time.

She closed the book. She put it on the counter beside the kettle. She looked at him.

"Rashed," she said.

He waited. He was good at waiting. He had been told.

"Did you write this."

He had rehearsed many things in the car on the way over. He had rehearsed: *yes, of course, who else.* He had rehearsed: *I worked with a tool.* He had rehearsed: *it's complicated*. He had rehearsed silence. He had rehearsed laughing.

What he said was: "Yes."

She looked at him for a second longer than she usually looked at him. Then she nodded — once, small — and picked up the book again and put it in the bag she had brought home from the grocery, the one she would take upstairs later. She did not ask anything else. She poured the tea. She asked about their mother's knee. They talked about their mother's knee for nine minutes.

When he left she walked him to the door and hugged him the way she always hugged him, which was briefly and from the side, with one arm. She said *I'm proud of you, bhaiya*, and the *bhaiya* — which she only used when she was teasing him, or when she meant it, and he could not always tell which — sat in the doorway after she closed the door and stayed there as he walked to the car.

He sat in the driver's seat in her driveway for some time before he started the engine. The streetlight at the end of the cul-de-sac was the orange kind, the old kind, the kind they had not replaced yet on this street. He watched it. He thought about the sentence he had said. The *yes*. He could not take it back. He could write to her tomorrow. He could write to her in an hour. He could go back to the door right now and tell her about the platform, about the discarded preparations, about the seam he could not find. He could do any of these things and none of them would unsay the *yes*.

She had not believed him. He had seen the second of her looking. She had not pressed. She would never press. She would carry the not-believing with her for the rest of her life, the way he had carried his father's face, the way their mother carried whatever she carried in the bathroom with the tap running. Tahmina would put the book on a shelf and she would mention it sometimes to her husband in the kind voice she used for things she had not made up her mind about, and the not-having-made-up-her-mind would be the form their relationship now took, because he had given her a thing he could not vouch for and had said, when she asked, that it was his.

He started the engine. He did not turn on the radio. He drove.

---

He did not stop using the platform. He had thought, in the driveway, that he might. He thought it again at home, sitting at the desk that had been Tahmina's. He thought it the next morning, drinking the coffee his mother had made and left for him on the counter under a small plate, and he thought it on the bus on the way to the bank, and he thought it at his desk where the credit memos still lived. By the evening he had paid for another deep generation, because the editor had asked, gently, whether there was a second collection in him, and he had said *probably*, and the *probably* had been the only honest thing he had said in a week.

The new generation cost two hundred and ten dollars. He paid it. He sat at the kitchen table while his mother heated dal in the microwave behind him, and he opened the file the platform had prepared, and he read the first sentence, and the first sentence was a sentence about a sister in a kitchen.

His mother said something to him in Bangla about the dal. He did not hear her the first time. He heard her the second time and said *yes, yes, in a minute*. The minute did not come. The platform's sentence sat on the screen. Below it, the cursor blinked, waiting for him to begin choosing — to keep the sentence, to alter it, to delete it and write his own, although his own was no longer a category he could verify. He read the sentence three times. He could feel, in the back of his throat, the pressure of a sentence forming, his own, perhaps, or in response, perhaps, or having been the source, perhaps, of the platform's.

He could not tell.

He kept reading.
