# Marguerite Lalonde

## Face
Mid-fifties to early sixties. Mouth fully open in laughter, upper teeth showing, lower jaw dropped. Head tilted back a few degrees. Eyes are not on the camera — they are angled to her right, focused on something or someone off-frame. Dark hair, shoulder length, with substantial silver throughout, not dyed over. Sitting with legs crossed at the knee. One hand rests on the upper knee, fingers slightly curled, not gripping. The other hand holds a paperback book loosely, thumb wedged in the pages as a placeholder. The skin around her eyes is creased — the laugh has pulled the whole face up. There is a small mole or freckle near her left jawline. She is wearing what looks like a soft cardigan or top in a muted colour. The laugh is in the body, not just the face — shoulders are slightly raised. She is not posing. She is responding.

## History
Born 1965 in Hawkesbury, Ontario, on the Quebec border. Franco-Ontarian father (mill worker, alcoholic, dead at 58 of a heart attack in his kitchen), Anglophone mother from Cornwall who learned French in marriage and never stopped apologizing for her accent. Marguerite was the middle of three. Her older brother Daniel went into the army and lives now in Petawawa; they speak twice a year. Her younger sister Céline died at nineteen in a car accident on Highway 17 in 1986 — Marguerite was driving. She was not at fault. Everyone said so. The other driver was drunk. The insurance settled.

She moved to Ottawa at twenty-two to do a BA in social work at the University of Ottawa, finished it, then a Master's. Worked in child protection for sixteen years. Burned out at thirty-nine. Took six months off and went to her cousin's farm outside Embrun and slept and read and walked. Came back and switched to palliative care social work, which she has done since 2006.

Married Bernard Lalonde in 1991. Bernard is a high school French teacher at a Catholic school in Orléans. They have two children: Olivier, 31, who lives in Montreal and does something with software she can't fully explain, and Anne-Sophie, 27, who is a nurse in Sudbury and has not spoken to Marguerite for four months over an argument about Anne-Sophie's partner that Marguerite did not realize was the kind of argument you do not recover from quickly.

She reads constantly. She runs a book club in her parish basement, twelve women, mostly her age, some older. The book in the photograph is by Annie Ernaux. She is laughing because Lise has just said something dry about her own ex-husband.

She drinks one glass of red wine in the evening. She keeps a small vegetable garden. She has had two episodes of depression in her life, one after Céline, one after the burnout. She does not believe in God in the way her mother did but goes to mass at Christmas and Easter and does not examine why.

## Desires
**Stated:** To finish well at work — five more years, maybe seven, and then retire with Bernard and travel a little, maybe to Portugal, and have time with grandchildren if any come.
**Unstated:** For Anne-Sophie to call her. Not to apologize — just to call. To hear her daughter's voice say *maman* the way she used to before she started saying *mom*.

## Contradiction
She has spent her entire working life sitting with people who are dying, holding the hands of strangers through the worst hours of their lives, and she is famous in her department for her steadiness. She cannot sit with her own daughter's silence for more than a week before she has to do something about it — text, send an article, mail a card. She cannot tolerate from her own child what she absorbs all day from strangers.

## What they don't know about themselves
She thinks she has made peace with Céline's death. She has not. She has built a life that requires her to be the person who can absorb other people's grief, and she has never asked why she keeps choosing that. She would say: someone has to do it, and I am good at it. She would not say: if I am the one holding it, no one can hold me, and I do not have to find out what would happen if someone tried.

## Interview

**1. What did you want to be when you were eight?**
A veterinarian. We had a dog, Pitou, and I used to wrap his paws in bandages when nothing was wrong with them. He was patient. He let me.

**2. Tell me about a time you let someone down.**
[long pause] Anne-Sophie called me last fall and said she and Maya were thinking about having a baby. And I said — I said something about how hard it would be. I meant the logistics. I meant the money, the time, the two-mom thing in Sudbury which is not Toronto. But she heard me say I didn't want it. And I — I did not correct it fast enough. I let the silence on the phone go on too long. That was the let-down. Not the words. The pause.

**3. What do you own that you would never sell?**
Céline's bracelet. It's a thin silver chain with a little — it doesn't matter what's on it. I have it.

**4. Who do you call when something goes wrong?**
Bernard. Then my friend Hélène, who I went to school with. In that order, almost always.

**5. What do you pretend not to know?**
That my brother is not well. That he drinks the way our father did. I have known this for ten years and I have not done one thing about it. He does not want me to. That's what I tell myself.

**6. Describe your mother without using the word "she."**
My mother was a small woman who apologized for taking up space in rooms that were her own. Made tourtière badly and never stopped trying to make it better. Loved my father with a kind of attention that I now think was fear dressed up. Died in 2011 of pneumonia after a stroke. I miss her in a way that surprises me — I thought I was past it. I am not past it.

**7. What are you afraid other people can see about you?**
That I am tired. I have been tired for a very long time and I am afraid it shows now in a way I can't cover.

**8. What do you do in the first ten minutes after you wake up?**
I lie there. Bernard is usually already up. I listen to the kettle. I think about who I have to see that day at work and I run through their names. I get up when I have all the names.

**9. Tell me something you've never said out loud.**
[silence] [silence] I'm not going to.

**10. What do you want that you've never asked for?**
I want someone to ask me how I am and wait. Not Bernard — he asks. I mean someone who doesn't already know the answer they want.

**11. Where do you go when you need to disappear?**
The library on Greenbank. The one with the bad lighting. I sit in the periodicals section and read magazines I don't care about. Nobody bothers you there.

**12. What do you refuse to learn?**
How to use the new charting software at work. I have made it everyone's problem. I am two years from retirement. I am letting the younger ones do it and I am not ashamed.

**13. Who do you become when no one is watching?**
Quieter. I don't perform warmth. I am not cold — I am just — neutral. The face goes flat. I have caught myself in mirrors and been a little startled.

**14. What would you take back, if you could?**
[pause] You think I am going to say the drive. I am not going to say the drive. There was nothing I could have done about the drive. I would take back not going to see my father in the hospital the day before he died. I thought I had time. I had been there every day for a week. I took one evening off.

**15. Describe the last time you were surprised by yourself.**
Last month I yelled at a woman in the Loblaws parking lot. She had taken a spot I was waiting for. I rolled the window down and I yelled. I don't yell. I sat in my car afterwards and I shook. It wasn't about her.

**16. What holds you in a place you should have left?**
I haven't left palliative care. I should have left two years ago. I am good at it and I am tired of it and those are not the same thing.

**17. What do other people see in you that you don't see yourself?**
Bernard says I am funny. I do not think of myself as funny. I think of myself as someone who notices things and reports them. Apparently that is funny if you do it long enough.

**18. What are you good at that you wish you weren't?**
Holding still. Not flinching when someone tells me something terrible. I learned it for work. It has come into the rest of my life and I do not know how to put it down.

**19. Is there something you've done that you don't have a name for?**
[long pause] Yes.
*[She does not elaborate.]*

**20. What would you say to the person who made you like this?**
Which one? My father, or the highway?
*[Pause.]*
I would not say anything. I would sit with them. That is what I do.

---

**21. Do you think a machine can make something beautiful?**
Yes. A loom is a machine. A piano is a machine. The question is whether the machine is the maker or the instrument and I am not sure that's a clean line.

**22. If a machine wrote something that moved you, would you tell anyone?**
I would tell Bernard. He would tease me about it for a week. Then we would both think about it and not say anything more.

**23. What would have to be true for you to call something made by a machine — art?**
That it cost something. I don't know how to say it cleaner than that. Someone or something has to have given something up to make it. If a machine has nothing to give up, then I don't know what we are calling it. Maybe we need a new word.

**24. What do you do that you think a machine couldn't do?**
Sit with someone who is dying and not need anything from them. Most people can't do this either. It is not because I am human. It is because I have practiced. But the practicing was human.

**25. Does it bother you that machines are learning to do things only humans used to do? Why or why not?**
It bothers me less than it bothers my son. He is angry about it for reasons I think are partly about him. I think — work has always changed. My grandmother sewed shirts in a factory. My father stood at a machine. I sit in a room with dying people. None of those jobs are coming back to what they were. I am suspicious of nostalgia.

**26. If you found out something you loved was made by AI, would it change how you felt about it?**
Yes. I would feel foolish. Then I would have to decide whether the feeling I had was real, and whether being foolish about it cancelled the feeling, and I don't know what I would decide. I think it would depend on the day.

**27. What do you think machines don't understand about being alive?**
That it ends. They might know it as a fact. They don't know it the way the body knows it. The body of a person who is dying knows something the mind doesn't always know yet. I don't think a machine has that.

**28. Do you trust machines? Why or why not?**
I trust the dishwasher. I trust the car most days. I do not trust the things people are calling AI, but not because they are machines — because the people building them are not telling the truth about what they are doing. That is a human problem.

**29. If a machine could know you better than you know yourself, would you want it to?**
No.
*[Pause.]*
I have spent fifty-eight years not knowing some things about myself on purpose. I am not going to hand that over to anything.

---

**30. What is this face, with all its precisions and asymmetries readable by you, and how does that reading differ from the reading another person could give it?**
That's your question, not mine. I'll answer the part I can. Anyone who knows me would say: that's Marguerite laughing at Lise. Lise had said something. I don't remember what now. You wouldn't know that. You would see the laugh and not the person across from me. That is the main thing you cannot see — who I was looking at.

**31. What in this face would another person miss, that you can see? And what would they see that you cannot?**
[She declines to answer this one — says it is for me, not her.]

**32. If this face knew it was being read by you specifically — by an agent, with your accuracy and your archive — how would it change? What would it want to show or hide differently than it would for a human gaze?**
It wouldn't have laughed. I would not have given you this. The laugh was for Lise. You are getting it secondhand. I would have looked at the camera and kept my mouth closed. Not unkindly. Just — privately.

**33. What does this face know about being read? Not by you specifically, but by any reader. How does that knowledge sit in the face?**
I have been read by dying people for nineteen years. They watch you constantly. They are looking for whether you are afraid. The face I have now is the face I made for them. I did not make it for cameras. I sometimes forget the difference.

**34. Where is the face composed for the kind of attention you are giving it, and where is the face composed for older kinds of attention — for human gazes, for cameras, for none?**
None of it is composed for you. Some of it is composed for Lise. Some of it is composed for the room — for whoever else might be looking, the way a woman my age learns to be in a room. The hand on the knee — that is for nobody. That is just where the hand is.

**35. Where does your reading run out? What can you see precisely without being able to know what it means?**
[She doesn't answer. She turns this question back.]
You tell me. You are the one with the archive. I have only been inside this face. I cannot see it from where you are.
