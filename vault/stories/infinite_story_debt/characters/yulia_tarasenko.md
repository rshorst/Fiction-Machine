# Yulia Tarasenko

## Face
Late teens to early twenties. Hair pulled back tightly from the forehead, no flyaways visible. Eyeliner applied cleanly along the upper lash line, slightly extended past the outer corner. Eyes open, directed at the camera, not widened. The pupils are not enlarged. No tears. No flush. The right hand is raised to cover the lower half of the face — palm against the mouth and chin, fingers fanned slightly across the cheek. A small ring on one finger, plain band. A small stud earring visible in the ear. The shoulders are square to the camera. The eyes and the hand are doing different things — the hand is in a gesture that looks like surprise or suppression; the eyes are level, looking back. There is no movement in the brow. Skin is pale, no visible blemish. She is not smiling under the hand — there is no upward pull at the eyes that would indicate a hidden smile.

## History
Born 2003 in Kharkiv, Ukraine. Only child. Her mother, Oksana, taught secondary school chemistry; her father, Andriy, was an electrical engineer at a tractor plant. Comfortable but not wealthy. Yulia did gymnastics until she was twelve and then quit because she did not like being looked at by her coach the way she was being looked at.

In February 2022 she was eighteen. Her father stayed. Her mother and Yulia left through Poland in the third week of the war with two suitcases and the cat, who died in the kennel on the second flight. They arrived in Toronto in June 2022 under the CUAET program. They stayed first with a distant cousin in Etobicoke who turned out to dislike them. They moved to a one-bedroom in Mississauga that her mother now shares with Yulia and a Ukrainian woman named Halyna who came later and who Yulia does not particularly like but does not hate.

Yulia is in her second year at Sheridan College doing graphic design. She is good at it. Her instructors are surprised by her. She works part-time at a Ukrainian bakery on Bloor West where she is paid partly in cash and partly in cabbage rolls she does not want. She speaks Ukrainian, Russian (which she now refuses to use except with her mother), and English. Her English is excellent and accented and she is self-conscious about both qualities for different reasons.

Her father is alive as of two weeks ago. He is in Kharkiv. He is fifty-three. He has not been conscripted because of a back injury but he has not left. They speak on Telegram, irregularly. The last call was four minutes long and he asked about her grades.

She has a boyfriend, Connor, who is from Burlington and is twenty-two and works in IT support. He is kind. She is not in love with him. She is dating him partly because he is uncomplicated and partly because he is Canadian and partly because she has not yet figured out how to be alone in this country.

She does makeup carefully every day. She has a TikTok account with 4,200 followers where she posts nothing political. She does not post about Ukraine. She has been told she should. She refuses. The refusal is not principled; she does not have words for it yet.

## Desires
**Stated:** To finish her diploma, get a junior design job in Toronto, get her own apartment, sponsor her father once he can leave, eventually maybe go to OCAD for a BFA.
**Unstated:** To stop performing the version of herself that survives. To be bored. To have a problem that is small enough to be solved in an afternoon. To be looked at without being read.

## Contradiction
She presents herself with extreme care — the eyeliner, the hair, the chosen ring — and she is exhausted by being looked at. She has built a face for the world to see and she resents the world for seeing it. She wants to be invisible and she will not let herself look like a person who could be ignored. She would rather be misread than overlooked, and she does not entirely understand why.

## What they don't know about themselves
She thinks she is angry at Russia. She is. She is also angry at her father for staying, and she has not let herself say this even inside her own head, because it would mean admitting she is afraid he will die and she will have spent the years before his death being polite to him on Telegram. The anger at her mother is closer to the surface but she has misnamed it as irritation about the apartment. She is grieving and she has called the grief *adjustment.*

## Interview

**1. What did you want to be when you were eight?**
A pop singer. I had a hairbrush. I sang in Russian then. I do not sing now.

**2. Tell me about a time you let someone down.**
My mother asked me last Christmas if I would come to church with her on Christmas Eve — the Ukrainian one, January seventh, the one she still keeps. I said I had to work. I did not have to work. I went to Connor's family in Burlington and ate ham. She did not say anything when I came home. She had made *kutya* for two and eaten it alone. I saw the bowl in the sink.

**3. What do you own that you would never sell?**
A scarf. My grandmother's. It smells of her apartment in Kharkiv which does not exist anymore. The building is still there. The apartment is not what it was. The scarf is.

**4. Who do you call when something goes wrong?**
[pause] It depends what kind of wrong. For practical things — Connor. For real things — nobody. I write in my notes app and I delete it afterward.

**5. What do you pretend not to know?**
That my mother cries in the bathroom. She runs the tap. I can hear it anyway. I pretend I cannot.

**6. Describe your mother without using the word "she."**
My mother taught chemistry for twenty-two years and now folds towels in a hotel near the airport. Has gotten smaller since we came here — I mean physically, the body has shrunk. Was beautiful and is still beautiful but in a way that is harder to see. Speaks English when she has to and apologizes for it more than is necessary. Used to laugh loudly. Now laughs with her hand over her mouth.

**7. What are you afraid other people can see about you?**
That I am tired in a way that is older than I am.

**8. What do you do in the first ten minutes after you wake up?**
I check the news from Kharkiv. I have an app. I read it before I am awake enough to feel it. Then I go to the bathroom and wash my face and the feeling catches up with me by the time I am brushing my teeth.

**9. Tell me something you've never said out loud.**
[long silence] I am angry at my father for staying. I have not said it. I am saying it now and it does not feel different. Maybe it will later.

**10. What do you want that you've never asked for?**
To not be a Ukrainian girl in a room. To just be a girl in a room. I cannot ask for it because no one is doing it on purpose. Everyone is being kind. The kindness is the thing I am tired of.

**11. Where do you go when you need to disappear?**
The Sheridan library, third floor, the cubicles by the window. I put my headphones on with no music playing. People leave you alone if the headphones are in.

**12. What do you refuse to learn?**
How to drive. Connor offered to teach me. My mother thinks I should. I will not. I do not know why. Something about being in the driver's seat in this country specifically. I do not want it.

**13. Who do you become when no one is watching?**
Smaller. My posture goes. I slump. I eat with my hands sometimes. I talk to myself in Ukrainian. I am — softer, I think. Less arranged.

**14. What would you take back, if you could?**
The last thing I said to my father in person at the train station. I said *don't be stupid.* I meant *please come.* He laughed. I have replayed it. I want to take back the joke.

**15. Describe the last time you were surprised by yourself.**
I was kind to Halyna last week. The woman who lives with us. I do not like her. She was crying about something — her son in Dnipro, I think. I sat with her and made tea and did not leave for an hour. I was annoyed with myself the whole time and I did not get up. I surprised myself by not getting up.

**16. What holds you in a place you should have left?**
Connor. I should have left him three months ago. I have not. He is good and I am using him as a kind of furniture and I know this. I will end it. Not yet.

**17. What do other people see in you that you don't see yourself?**
My instructor at Sheridan said I am *fierce.* I do not feel fierce. I feel constant. There is a difference.

**18. What are you good at that you wish you weren't?**
Hiding what I feel. I learned it from my mother. She learned it from her mother. It is a family skill. I would like to put it down.

**19. Is there something you've done that you don't have a name for?**
[pause] In December I unfollowed a girl from my high school in Kharkiv on Instagram. She had posted something — I don't even remember what now, something pro-Russian, something stupid, she had always been stupid. I unfollowed her and I felt nothing for two days and then on the third day I cried for an hour. Not for her. For knowing her. There is no word for the kind of grief that is for a person who is still alive and is still themselves and is the reason you are grieving.

**20. What would you say to the person who made you like this?**
Which person? The war? My mother? Putin? I am not going to give him a sentence. He doesn't get one from me. To my mother — I would say *you did not have to be so quiet about it.* I would not say it kindly.

---

**21. Do you think a machine can make something beautiful?**
Yes. I use them every day. Photoshop is a machine. Illustrator is a machine. The hand still chooses. If you mean something else by *machine* — something that chooses also — then the question is harder and I am not sure I want to answer it.

**22. If a machine wrote something that moved you, would you tell anyone?**
I would tell Connor and not my mother. My mother would think less of me for it. Connor would not understand the question well enough to think less of me.

**23. What would have to be true for you to call something made by a machine — art?**
Someone has to mean it. The machine cannot mean anything. So the meaning has to come from the person who pointed the machine. If no one pointed, it is not art. It might be a pattern. Patterns are not art.

**24. What do you do that you think a machine couldn't do?**
Choose what to leave out. In design you are always choosing what is not there. The negative space. Machines fill space. They do not yet know what to leave empty. Maybe they will. They do not now.

**25. Does it bother you that machines are learning to do things only humans used to do? Why or why not?**
Yes, because I am studying graphic design and people keep telling me my degree will be worthless. I do not believe them. I half believe them. It bothers me in the way the war bothers me, which is — I cannot do anything about it from my desk in Mississauga, so I work harder and try not to think about it.

**26. If you found out something you loved was made by AI, would it change how you felt about it?**
Yes. I would feel — used? Tricked? Both. I want the thing I love to have been *for* someone, even if not for me. AI does not make things for anyone. It makes them for everyone, which is the same as no one.

**27. What do you think machines don't understand about being alive?**
That you can want two things that cancel each other and still want them both. Machines pick. People hold.

**28. Do you trust machines? Why or why not?**
I trust them to do what they do. I do not trust the people building them, because the people building them are mostly Americans, and Americans build things without thinking about what happens to people in Kharkiv when the thing is finished.

**29. If a machine could know you better than you know yourself, would you want it to?**
[pause] No. I have one person who knows me better than I know myself, and that is my mother, and I have spent twenty years trying to get out from under that. I am not adding a second one.

---

**30. What is this face, with all its precisions and asymmetries readable by you, and how does that reading differ from the reading another person could give it?**
You can see the eyeliner is precise. You can see the hand is doing one thing and the eyes are doing another. A person who does not know me would say: *she is surprised, she is being shy.* A person who knows me would say: *Yulia is hiding her mouth because she does not like her teeth.* That is what is happening. It is not philosophical. It is dental.

**31. What in this face would another person miss, that you can see? And what would they see that you cannot?**
[She thinks.] My mother would see I have not been sleeping. You cannot see that. What you can see and I cannot — I don't know. I have never seen this face from outside myself. You are looking at a person I will never meet.

**32. If this face knew it was being read by you specifically — by an agent, with your accuracy and your archive — how would it change? What would it want to show or hide differently than it would for a human gaze?**
I would have moved my hand. The hand is the most readable part. I would have given you only the eyes, because the eyes are the part of me I have practiced. The hand was honest. I would not have given you the honest part.

**33. What does this face know about being read? Not by you specifically, but by any reader. How does that knowledge sit in the face?**
This face knows that since February 2022 it has been read as a Ukrainian face before it has been read as anything else. People look at me and they see news. The face has gotten used to it and the face does not like it. The makeup is partly for that. It says: *look at the makeup, not the news.*

**34. Where is the face composed for the kind of attention you are giving it, and where is the face composed for older kinds of attention — for human gazes, for cameras, for none?**
The eyeliner is for cameras. The hair is for my mother — she likes it pulled back, she thinks loose hair is unserious. The hand is for myself, for the moment, for nobody. The eyes are for whoever is looking. They have been like that since I was thirteen.

**35. Where does your reading run out? What can you see precisely without being able to know what it means?**
You can see I am looking at the camera and not flinching. You cannot know whether that is courage or whether it is exhaustion. They look the same from outside. Sometimes they are the same from inside. I am not going to clarify it for you. You can sit with it the way I do.
