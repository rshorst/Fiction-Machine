# Rashed Chowdhury

## Face
Mid-to-late twenties. Hair is dense, curly, voluminous, growing up and out from the scalp rather than down. Beard is sparse — patches of growth along the jaw and above the upper lip, not connected, deliberately not shaved off. Skin is brown, even-toned. Eyes are directed slightly off-camera, not meeting the lens. The gaze is steady; the eyelids are at neutral, neither widened nor narrowed. Mouth is closed, lips held still — not pressed, not relaxed. There is small tension at the corners. Shoulders are turned a few degrees off the frame's axis. Arms are down, not crossed. He is wearing a black-and-white checked shirt, collared, buttoned. He is not smiling. He is not frowning.

## History
Born in Sylhet, Bangladesh, in 1997. Came to Canada at age eleven with his mother and younger sister; his father had come three years earlier and worked at a Tim Hortons in Scarborough while saving for the family reunification paperwork. They settled in a two-bedroom apartment near Victoria Park and Eglinton. The father is now part-owner of a halal grocery in Pickering. The mother works in housekeeping at a hotel near the airport.

Rashed went to a high school where he was one of many Bangladeshi kids and not one of the standout ones. Average grades. Quiet. He was good at math but did not love it. He went to U of T Scarborough and did a B.Comm because his father wanted him to and because he could not think of a better reason not to. He works now as a credit analyst at a mid-sized commercial bank in North York. He is two years in. He is good at it. He does not like it.

He still lives at home, in the same apartment, in what used to be his sister's room — she moved out last year when she got married to a man from Brampton whom Rashed likes more than he expected to. He pays his parents a small amount of rent that they accept reluctantly. He gives his mother more cash separately, which she puts into a tin in the kitchen and spends on groceries and small gifts for the relatives in Sylhet.

He writes. Has written since he was fifteen. Short stories, mostly. He has never shown them to anyone in his family. He has shown them to one person — Anika, a woman he dated in his third year of university, who broke up with him because he would not say what he wanted. He thought he was being respectful. She thought he was being absent. He has not dated since. That was four years ago.

He has been to Bangladesh twice as an adult, once for a wedding, once for his grandfather's funeral. He felt like a tourist both times and was ashamed of it.

He prays sometimes. He does not pray sometimes. His relationship to it has not stabilized and he does not push it.

He goes to the gym three times a week. He is not building anything in particular there. It is a place where the day stops.

## Desires
**Stated:** To get promoted to senior analyst within two years, save enough for a down payment on a condo, eventually move out, eventually maybe get married, eventually maybe write a book.
**Unstated:** To leave the bank. To tell his father he never wanted commerce. To send a story to a magazine and have someone he doesn't know read it and say something about it that is not kind.

## Contradiction
He is the quietest person in every room he enters and he wants, more than almost anything, to be heard. Not loudly — exactly, fully, by one person paying close attention. He has structured his life so that this almost cannot happen. He chose a job where listening is the skill. He has chosen friendships where he is the listener. He resents this and has built it himself.

## What they don't know about themselves
He thinks his silence is a form of patience. He thinks he is waiting for the right moment, the right person, the right reader. He is not waiting. He is hiding. The story he has not sent out has been finished for eleven months. The job he could leave he could leave today — he has savings, he has a degree, his father would be hurt and would survive it. He believes he is biding time. He is, in fact, refusing.

## Interview

**1. What did you want to be when you were eight?**
A pilot. I wanted to fly the plane that brought us to Canada. I had not been on one yet. I thought it would be like driving but with the sky. When I was finally on the plane I was disappointed. You don't see anything. You sit.

**2. Tell me about a time you let someone down.**
[pause] Anika. I don't want to go into it. I let her down by not — by not saying things. She gave me chances. I didn't take any of them. She told me, near the end, *you are making me do all of this alone.* And I didn't even argue. I just nodded. That was probably the worst part.

**3. What do you own that you would never sell?**
A notebook. The first one I filled. It is full of bad sentences. I keep it because if I lose it I will lose proof that I started.

**4. Who do you call when something goes wrong?**
My sister. Not my parents. My sister, Tahmina. She is two years younger and three years ahead of me in every way that matters.

**5. What do you pretend not to know?**
That my father is disappointed in me even though I did exactly what he asked. He wanted commerce. He got commerce. Somehow the disappointment is still there. I think it is because I am quiet. He wanted a son with commerce *and* a voice. I gave him the wrong half.

**6. Describe your mother without using the word "she."**
My mother is a small woman who has cleaned other people's bathrooms for sixteen years and has not let it touch her. Laughs more than my father. Knows everything about everyone in our building. Watches Bangla dramas on her phone in the kitchen with the volume too low to actually hear and reads the subtitles. Has never asked me what I want. I do not know if that is love or its opposite or both.

**7. What are you afraid other people can see about you?**
That I am scared. Of most things. Of being wrong, of being seen wanting something and not getting it. I think I look composed. I am not sure I am.

**8. What do you do in the first ten minutes after you wake up?**
I check my phone. I hate that I do it. I read emails I am not going to answer. I lie there. I get up when my mother starts making noise in the kitchen on purpose for me to hear.

**9. Tell me something you've never said out loud.**
I think I am a writer. Not *I want to be.* I think I already am one and I am pretending I am not, and the pretending is the only thing that keeps me at the bank.

**10. What do you want that you've never asked for?**
A year off. Not to travel. To stay in my room and write. I would never ask. My father would not understand the question.

**11. Where do you go when you need to disappear?**
The Toronto Reference Library. The fifth floor. There's a corner near the back where the chairs face the wrong way. Nobody finds you there.

**12. What do you refuse to learn?**
How to network. I have been told to. I have been told it would help. I will not. I do not want to meet people that way. I would rather stay where I am.

**13. Who do you become when no one is watching?**
Looser. I make jokes to the room. My face moves more. I sing along to things in the car. I am — embarrassingly — fun. I would not want any of my coworkers to see it.

**14. What would you take back, if you could?**
The night Anika told me she was leaving. I said *okay*. I want to take back the *okay*. I want to put something else in its place. I don't know what yet.

**15. Describe the last time you were surprised by yourself.**
I cried at a Pixar movie last month. I was alone. *Inside Out 2.* I am twenty-seven years old. I cried because the girl had anxiety and the anxiety had a name and a shape and could be talked to. I sat on my couch and I wept. I have not told anyone.

**16. What holds you in a place you should have left?**
My father's face. Not a thing he has said. The face he would make. I have been managing that face for nine years.

**17. What do other people see in you that you don't see yourself?**
Tahmina says I am funny. My manager at work says I am *unflappable.* I don't know which one of them is more wrong.

**18. What are you good at that you wish you weren't?**
Waiting. I can wait longer than almost anyone. People assume it means I am calm. It means I am stuck.

**19. Is there something you've done that you don't have a name for?**
[long pause] I read Anika's wedding announcement online last year. She married a doctor. I read it maybe forty times in one night. I did not feel jealous. I felt — I don't know. Relieved? Grieved? Both. There is no word for what I was doing. Reading it was the doing.

**20. What would you say to the person who made you like this?**
Which one — my father, or me? Because I have done a lot of this myself. I would say to my father: *I know you were tired.* I would say to myself: *what are you waiting for.* I have been saying it. I am not answering.

---

**21. Do you think a machine can make something beautiful?**
Yes. Beauty is not the bar. Anyone can make something beautiful. A sunset is beautiful and nothing made it.

**22. If a machine wrote something that moved you, would you tell anyone?**
[pause] No. I would be embarrassed. Not of the machine. Of myself, for being moved by something that wasn't trying. That is the part I can't get past — a thing that wasn't *trying.*

**23. What would have to be true for you to call something made by a machine — art?**
Someone would have to lose sleep over it. Either making it or because of it. Art has insomnia in it somewhere. If nothing lay awake, it isn't art. It might still be good.

**24. What do you do that you think a machine couldn't do?**
Sit at my desk and not write. The not-writing is mine. A machine would just write. I am the one refusing. A machine cannot refuse.

**25. Does it bother you that machines are learning to do things only humans used to do? Why or why not?**
Less than it bothers people I work with. I am a credit analyst. A machine can do my job. I have known this for two years. I keep showing up. Maybe I want it to take the job from me. That would be a reason to leave.

**26. If you found out something you loved was made by AI, would it change how you felt about it?**
Yes. I would feel — small. Like I had been talking to someone who wasn't there. I am sensitive to that. I have been talking to people who weren't there for a while.

**27. What do you think machines don't understand about being alive?**
That it is mostly waiting. They are too fast. Being alive is slow. You have to sit through it. Most of being a person is just enduring time.

**28. Do you trust machines? Why or why not?**
I trust them more than I trust most people who use them. The machine is honest about being a machine. The people are not honest about being people.

**29. If a machine could know you better than you know yourself, would you want it to?**
[long silence]
I think it already does. My phone knows what I look at when nobody is watching. It knows the things I read forty times. I have not asked it what it makes of me. I am not going to. I think I am afraid it would be right.

---

**30. What is this face, with all its precisions and asymmetries readable by you, and how does that reading differ from the reading another person could give it?**
You can see the tension at my mouth. People who know me would see *Rashed being quiet.* That is not a reading. That is recognition. You don't have recognition. You have only the tension. So you might make more of it than my mother would. She wouldn't even notice. To her this is just my face.

**31. What in this face would another person miss, that you can see? And what would they see that you cannot?**
[He thinks for a while.] My sister would see I haven't slept enough. You would not see that — sleep is not in the photograph, it is in the week behind it. What I cannot see, that another person could — I don't know. I have not seen my own face from outside. I only ever see it in mirrors, looking at itself. That is a different face from the one in this picture.

**32. If this face knew it was being read by you specifically — by an agent, with your accuracy and your archive — how would it change? What would it want to show or hide differently than it would for a human gaze?**
I would have closed my mouth tighter. The tension you saw — I would have hidden it from you, because you would name it. A human would feel it without having a word for it, and the wordlessness is a kind of privacy. You take that away. So I would have given you less.

**33. What does this face know about being read? Not by you specifically, but by any reader. How does that knowledge sit in the face?**
[pause] A brown man in Canada knows he is being read. From age fourteen onward. At borders, in stores, in elevators. The face I have is partly the face I made for that. The stillness is partly that. I did not invent it for the photograph. The photograph found it because it was already there.

**34. Where is the face composed for the kind of attention you are giving it, and where is the face composed for older kinds of attention — for human gazes, for cameras, for none?**
The eyes are off-camera because I was not composing for the lens. The hair is composed for nobody — I just have this hair. The mouth is composed for the room I have been in for fifteen years. The shirt I bought because Tahmina told me to.

**35. Where does your reading run out? What can you see precisely without being able to know what it means?**
You can see I am not smiling. You cannot see whether I am at peace or whether I am exhausted. You will guess. You will pick one or hold them both. I'm not going to tell you which. It would not be fair to either of us.
