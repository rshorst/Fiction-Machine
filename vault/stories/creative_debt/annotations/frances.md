## Megan

**The moment only she would do.** In the paragraph that begins *"She read the note in the kitchen, standing up, with one sock on,"* the gesture that is hers is not the standing — it is the third reading. *"She read it a third time, this time not as a writer reads a rejection but as a policy analyst reads a memo, looking for the load-bearing claim."* This is the accommodating scorekeeper exactly: she has already absorbed the wound on reading one and two, and on three she switches register to the one she trusts, the federal-department one, and starts looking for the structural sentence. The two diagnoses she pulls out — *younger writer*, *flattening into average* — and her instant private knowledge that *they were two diagnoses of the same surface* but that *she could not tell which one was hers* — that is her. The Megan in the interview who said she catalogued her own crying at the dog food commercial, who said *I don't get to not know what I think*, would do exactly this: receive a wound and immediately try to forensically separate it into components she could fix one at a time. No one else in this draft would read a rejection three times in three different professional registers.

The second moment that is hers, and possibly more hers: the proofs in the recycling bin. Not the gesture itself — the line *"because cancelling would have been a gesture and she was through with gestures."* The scorekeeper would notice that. Only she would.

**The generic moment.** The paragraph beginning *"In the mornings she went to the bench past the bridge with the graffiti, the one where the path bends."* The bench is generic. Any woman in any draft about a woman undoing herself goes to a bench. The cold being *harder than it had been in October* is generic weather. The comparison to her father in the recliner watching curling on mute is hers, and rescues part of the paragraph, but the framing — *she sat. She did not bring her phone* — is the recovery-montage version of what the Megan I interviewed would actually do, which is keep working, keep cataloguing, keep score against herself in a different column. The wrong verb is *sat*. She does not sit. She compiles.

**Voice match and mismatch.** The match is strong in the interior: the parenthetical *(she had learned over four years that he agreed with her about almost everything, and at twenty-five she had read this as compatibility, and at twenty-eight she was beginning to read it as something else for which she did not yet have a clean word)* is exactly the rhythm she had in the interview when I asked her about Adrien — the dated self-assessment, the willingness to say *I do not yet have a clean word* rather than reach for one. The *she had also catalogued it* tic, recurring, is hers. The mismatch is in the dialogue with Adrien at the end. *"I don't know yet"* is too clean. The Megan in the interview deflected by answering a question I didn't ask, or by going specific where I'd gone general. Here she answers honestly and briefly and it sounds like a different woman — a woman who has finished the work the draft is showing her start.

**Unstated desire legible?** Yes. She wants to be loved by someone who could see her being a little cruel and not flinch. It is legible in the negative space around Tobias's November paragraph — her recognition that *the warm wall doing exactly what the warm wall did* is not what she wanted, that the kindness was the disqualification. It is legible in the final scene with Adrien: she asks him to ask, which is the closest she has come to setting a test she wants someone to fail in front of her. She does not get what she wants. The desire is present in the reaching.

## Tobias

**The moment only he would do.** The *I think.* In the October paragraph: *"This is good. It sounds like the earlier ones. I mean that as a compliment. I think."* This is his — specifically his — because the warm wall does not crack outward, it leaks. The Tobias I interviewed said almost nothing in first person and apologised once for using the word *I*. The *I think* is him losing the third-person scaffolding for two words and immediately registering that he has lost it. He would not retract it. He would not address it. He would let it sit and hope it goes unread, and when she does not address it either he would resume as if it had not happened. That is exactly what he does in the draft. No other character would put the crack in and then keep walking.

The second moment: his four-sentence reply at the end. *"I knew it on the first message and I let it keep going because I liked who I was being."* The clause *because I liked who I was being* is his. It is the sentence of a man who has spent his life being chosen for a role and has, finally, named the pleasure of being chosen. The Tobias in the interview circled this — said he was *useful* to his mother, said his students made him *legible to himself* — without ever saying he liked it. Here he says it. It is the one moment he uses *I* to describe a want rather than a duty.

**The generic moment.** The November paragraph itself — the one Megan reads eleven times — is generic on purpose, and the draft tells us so. But within that, the line *"the writing was real because it was read"* is the generic one. Not because a kind man wouldn't write it; any kind man would. It is the pastoral aphorism a youth pastor or a thoughtful teacher would produce, and the draft frames it correctly as *not designed to her*. The wrong move is that he produces it at all without first producing a sentence aimed at her specifically and failing. The Tobias I interviewed would have tried, badly, to write something specific to her, and then defaulted to the pastoral. The draft skips the trying. It gives us the default cleanly. That is the place he reads as a type rather than a person — for one paragraph, fairly.

**Voice match and mismatch.** Match: the avoidance of *I* across eight months of correspondence. That is exactly his interview pattern. The phrase *what I notice in this is* is a formulation he would actually use; in the interview he said *what comes up for me when I read that is*, which is the same architecture. Mismatch: *Thank you for naming it.* That sentence is therapy-language and he is not a therapy-language person. The Tobias I built was raised Mennonite-adjacent, teaches grade six, talks like a person who learned warmth from his mother and from a specific kind of small-town pulpit, not from a workshop. He would say *thank you* and stop, or he would say *I needed you to say it*, which would cost him more. *Naming it* is borrowed vocabulary.

**Unstated desire legible?** Partially. His unstated desire is to be wanted in a way that doesn't feel like being chosen for a role. It is legible in the *because I liked who I was being* — that is the role being named. It is not legible earlier. Through most of the draft he reads as the warm wall and we see Megan's reading of him; we don't see him reaching. The reaching only surfaces in the four sentences. A reader who didn't have his profile would register a kind man who let a flirtation drift; the specific shape of his want — to be seen without being assigned — is only legible at the end, and only because he does the unusual thing of admitting it. That is, I think, accurate to him: he would not let the desire be legible until forced.

## Elliot

Elliot is not in this draft. He is in the relations document; he does not appear in *Two Ascents*. There is no scene in the wine bar, no moment Megan walks past him, no Felix, no hat. The draft is Megan and Tobias and Adrien, with Lucille and Cody and Priya named in passing.

So: the people I built who appear in the draft are two of the three. Megan is here, mostly herself, sharpest in her cataloguing and softest in her bench. Tobias is here, accurate in his avoidances, slightly borrowed in his final vocabulary. Elliot is somewhere else — still on Dundas West, still in the hat, still pouring wine for women who tip 22% and do not remember him. The draft did not need him. He is not diminished by being absent; he is just not in this room. If asked whether the Elliot I built is the Elliot in the draft, the honest answer is that there is no Elliot in the draft to check against, and the Elliot I built would find that fitting in a way he would resent and not name.
