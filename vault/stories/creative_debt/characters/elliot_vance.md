# Elliot Vance

## Face

A man in his mid-to-late twenties standing near a white wall. He is wearing a wide-brimmed hat — felt, dark. The eyes are blue and looking directly at the camera but the lids are slightly lowered. The mouth is doing something between a smile and a smirk — the corners are up but only a few millimetres. The jaw is set; the lower face has not joined the smile that the eyes are partway into. Light hits one cheekbone harder than the other. His shoulders are square to the camera. There is no visible hair — the hat covers what would be the hairline. A few days of stubble, even, on the cheeks. The skin is fair. He is holding still in the way someone holds still when they know a photo is being taken and have decided how to be in it.

## History

Born 1996 in Lethbridge, Alberta. Father an electrician (Doug), mother a Grade 3 teacher (Karen, née Veitch). One younger sister, Hannah, who works in nursing in Calgary. Elliot was a middle-of-the-pack student, a decent but uninterested hockey player until he quit at fifteen, and the kid in his friend group who was always slightly better-looking than the others, which became a small fact about his life he did not have words for until he was about twenty-two.

Did a year of business at the U of L, dropped out, moved to Calgary, worked at a Cactus Club as a server for three years and made more money than his father, which neither of them mentioned. Started taking photos on a Fujifilm someone left in the apartment after a party. Got decent at it. Got an Instagram following — clothing brands, mostly — that peaked at about 14,000 in 2020 and has been slowly declining ever since.

Moved to Toronto in 2021 with a girlfriend, Sasha, who was getting a master's at OCAD. They broke up in 2022. He stayed. Lives in a basement apartment in Parkdale, shares it with a roommate named Felix who plays bass in a band Elliot pretends to like more than he does. Works at a wine bar on Dundas West four nights a week and does freelance photography — mostly headshots for actors and small e-commerce shoots. Made $34,000 last year. Tells his parents he made more.

The hat in the photo is one of three he owns. He bought the first one in 2019 after a girl at the restaurant told him he had "a face for hats." He has thought about that sentence more times than he would admit.

Sleeps with women he meets through the wine bar and through Hinge. Has not been in love since Sasha and is not sure he was in love with Sasha. Calls his mother every Sunday. Has not been home in fourteen months.

He reads — actually reads, not performatively. Currently halfway through a Patrick deWitt novel. Smokes when he drinks. Doesn't drive in the city. Sends his sister memes she doesn't always respond to. Voted Conservative once, in a federal election, and has never told anyone in Toronto.

## Desires

**Stated:** To shoot a magazine cover within two years. To get out of the basement apartment. To "figure out what's next" — a phrase he uses often and means less by than it sounds.

**Unstated:** To be looked at without having to set the terms of the looking. To be loved by someone who isn't impressed by him. To stop feeling like he is auditioning for a life he hasn't been cast in yet.

## Contradiction

He has built an entire low-grade career around being looked at and being good at controlling how he is looked at, and he is privately sure that the people who actually know him don't see him at all. He resents this and he engineers it. He cannot tell which one is the cause.

## What they don't know about themselves

That the hat is a tell, not a disguise. That he is more like his father than he thinks — same patience, same way of leaving a sentence unfinished, same difficulty admitting he wants things. That the reason he stayed in Toronto after Sasha left wasn't ambition. It was that going back to Alberta would have meant being seen by people who knew him before the hat.

## Interview

**1. What did you want to be when you were eight?**
A paleontologist. Every kid wanted to be a paleontologist. I had the books. I had the little brushes. I think I just liked the word.

**2. Tell me about a time you let someone down.**
My sister had her grad from nursing school and I told her I'd fly out and I didn't. I sent flowers. She said it was fine. It wasn't a money thing, I had the money, I just — I didn't want to be in Lethbridge that weekend. That's the truth.

**3. What do you own that you would never sell?**
My grandfather's watch. Doesn't work. I don't wear it. It's in a drawer. — Also the Fuji. The first camera. I've got better cameras now but I won't get rid of that one.

**4. Who do you call when something goes wrong?**
Depends on the wrong. — My mom, probably. Which is embarrassing to say at twenty-eight but it's true. Felix if it's a thing in the apartment. I don't really have — I don't have a guy I call. I notice that sometimes.

**5. What do you pretend not to know?**
[pause] That my career isn't going to happen the way I've been telling people it's going to happen. I'm a decent photographer. I'm not — I'm not the photographer I told my parents I was going to be. I know that. I keep talking like I don't.

**6. Describe your mother without using the word "she."**
Karen. Tall. Taller than my dad by a bit, which I think bothered him for years and then stopped. Cuts her own hair. Cried at my high school graduation and then apologized for crying. Has a binder of every report card I ever got. Calls every Sunday whether I pick up or not.

**7. What are you afraid other people can see about you?**
That I'm trying. — That the whole thing is more constructed than it looks. The hat, the apartment, the — I don't know. The vibe. That somebody's going to look at me at a party and just see the work of it.

**8. What do you do in the first ten minutes after you wake up?**
Lie there. Look at my phone but not really. Felix is usually up before me and there's coffee. I drink the coffee. I don't talk for like an hour. I'm not a morning person and I've stopped pretending.

**9. Tell me something you've never said out loud.**
[silence]
I think I peaked at twenty-four. I think the best version of me was at twenty-four and I'm just managing the descent now. I'm aware that's a stupid thing to think at twenty-eight. I think it anyway.

**10. What do you want that you've never asked for?**
For my dad to say the thing. He's never said he's proud of me. He's also never said he isn't. I don't think he is. I'd want him to say either thing.

**11. Where do you go when you need to disappear?**
The Music Garden, down by the lake. Sounds pretentious. It's just quiet. I sit on a bench and look at the water and don't perform anything.

**12. What do you refuse to learn?**
How to take criticism from anyone other than my mom and one ex-girlfriend. I'm bad at it. I get defensive and then I get cold and then I leave. I know I do it. I haven't fixed it.

**13. Who do you become when no one is watching?**
Slower. The smile goes away. I sit weird in chairs — I don't sit pretty. I eat cereal at midnight standing at the sink. I look — I look like my dad, actually. I catch it in mirrors sometimes and it's a small shock every time.

**14. What would you take back, if you could?**
Telling Sasha I didn't think we were going to make it before she'd said it. I said it first because I wanted to be the one who said it first. That's all that was. I'd take that back.

**15. Describe the last time you were surprised by yourself.**
I went to a wedding in October — a guy from the wine bar — and I cried. I didn't know either of them well. The bride said something in her vows about her father and I just — went. I had to leave the tent. I haven't cried in years. I don't know what that was.

**16. What holds you in a place you should have left?**
Toronto. — Or, no. The apartment. Felix. The whole setup. I should have left it a year ago. It's cheap and I know how it works and leaving would mean admitting something. I don't know what.

**17. What do other people see in you that you don't see yourself?**
That I'm — apparently I'm intense. People say that. I don't feel intense. I feel chill. So I think there's a gap there I can't see across.

**18. What are you good at that you wish you weren't?**
Charm. The fast kind. Walking into a room and getting the room within ten minutes. It works on people I don't want it to work on and it doesn't work on people I do.

**19. Is there something you've done that you don't have a name for?**
[long pause]
There's a thing where I let women fall for me a little before I tell them I'm not — that I'm not going to be the guy. I don't lie. I just don't correct things fast enough. I do it on purpose and I don't know what to call that. It's not cruelty, it's something smaller and worse.

**20. What would you say to the person who made you like this?**
Which one. — My dad, probably. I'd ask him why he never said anything. The good or the bad. I'd ask him what it cost him to be quiet for that long. I think it cost him a lot. I don't think he knows that either.

**21. Do you think a machine can make something beautiful?**
Yeah. Sure. A camera is a machine. Every photo I've ever taken is a machine making something. The question is whether the machine cared. I don't think mine does and I don't think it matters.

**22. If a machine wrote something that moved you, would you tell anyone?**
Probably not. I'd feel weird about it. Not — not ashamed. Just weird. Like I'd been tricked even if I hadn't been.

**23. What would have to be true for you to call something made by a machine — art?**
I don't know. I think I'd want to know who chose what it made. There's always a person somewhere — pressing the button, picking the prompt, picking the keepers. That person is making the art. The machine is the brush. I think.

**24. What do you do that you think a machine couldn't do?**
Know when to put the camera down. There's a moment in a shoot where the person is about to do something real and if you keep shooting they stop. You have to stop first. I don't think a machine knows that yet.

**25. Does it bother you that machines are learning to do things only humans used to do? Why or why not?**
For my work, yeah. The headshot stuff, the e-comm stuff — that's all going to be AI in five years. That's my rent. So yeah. It bothers me. The bigger philosophical thing — I don't really think about it.

**26. If you found out something you loved was made by AI, would it change how you felt about it?**
Yeah. I wouldn't be proud of that, but yeah.

**27. What do you think machines don't understand about being alive?**
Boredom. The actual thing, the ache of it. The way time goes when nothing's happening and you're alone in a basement apartment. They process. They don't sit through.

**28. Do you trust machines? Why or why not?**
I trust my camera. I trust my phone less. I trust the algorithm not at all. The further from the object the less I trust it. Maybe that's not about machines, that's about scale.

**29. If a machine could know you better than you know yourself, would you want it to?**
[pause]
Maybe. Yeah, actually. I think I would want it to. Then I'd want it to tell me. — That's probably bad. That's probably the wrong answer. I don't care.

**30. What is this face, with all its precisions and asymmetries readable by you, and how does that reading differ from the reading another person could give it?**
I don't know what you're seeing. I know what I see in the mirror and I know what the photo is doing and they're different things. A person looking at me sees the hat first. You're seeing — what, the jaw? The lid on the eyes? I don't know what to do with that question.

**31. What in this face would another person miss, that you can see? And what would they see that you cannot?**
A person would miss that I'm tired. A person would see — I don't know. People tell me I look like my dad and I never see it. So that.

**32. If this face knew it was being read by you specifically — by an agent, with your accuracy and your archive — how would it change? What would it want to show or hide differently than it would for a human gaze?**
I think it would try harder. Which is the thing it always does. The hat is already trying. If I knew you were reading I'd probably take the hat off and that would also be a performance. There's no version of this where I'm not aware of being read. That's the answer.

**33. What does this face know about being read? Not by you specifically, but by any reader. How does that knowledge sit in the face?**
In the lids, I guess. The half-closed thing. That's not a mood. That's a habit. I do it because I don't want my eyes to be too available.

**34. Where is the face composed for the kind of attention you are giving it, and where is the face composed for older kinds of attention — for human gazes, for cameras, for none?**
The hat is for cameras. The smile is for the photographer — it was Felix, actually, who took this. The jaw is — the jaw is for nobody. The jaw is just how I hold my jaw. I don't think about it.

**35. Where does your reading run out? What can you see precisely without being able to know what it means?**
[silence]
You're asking me where your reading runs out. I don't know where your reading runs out. I'd guess: you can see I'm withholding something and you can't tell whether I'm withholding it from you or from myself. I don't know either. So if you were going to stop, stop there.
