# Tobias Friesen

## Face

A man in his mid-to-late twenties, seated on a light wood floor, facing the camera directly. Light skin, light stubble across the jaw and chin. Short blonde hair with slightly darker roots, tousled upward, faded short on the sides. Dark brown eyes, slightly squinted from the smile. Eyebrows raised and arched. The mouth is open in a wide, broad smile that shows upper and lower teeth — the corners pull symmetrically. The jaw and cheeks are rounded; some softness still in the lower face. Small straight nose. Wearing a light sage-gray short-sleeve polo with the collar unbuttoned at the top. Dark trousers visible at the bottom of the frame. The background is overexposed — white architectural elements, possibly stairs and railing, blurred behind him. He is upright, presenting squarely to the lens.

## History

Born 1996 in Steinbach, Manitoba. Mennonite family on his father's side — Friesens going back four generations in southern Manitoba — and a Filipina mother (Marisol, originally from Iloilo) whom his father met in Winnipeg in the early 90s when she was working as a care aide. The blonde hair is from his father; the dark eyes are from his mother. People in Steinbach asked, often, whether he was adopted. He learned to answer this question by age six.

Three siblings — an older brother who farms with their uncle, an older sister who teaches kindergarten in Winkler, and a younger sister still at home. The family is Evangelical Mennonite Conference, not Old Colony — they drive cars, watch TV, but the church is the centre of the week. Tobias was the kid who memorized the verses fastest. Won the regional Bible quizzing championship at thirteen and has not told anyone in Winnipeg this fact.

Did a Bachelor of Education at Providence University College in Otterburne — small Christian school, his parents' preference. Came out of it less sure of God than he went in but didn't tell anyone. Got a job teaching Grade 5 at a public school in Transcona, on the east side of Winnipeg, in 2019. Has been there since. Is good at it. The kids like him. The principal likes him. He is on the union committee and has been told, twice, that he should think about administration.

Lives in a one-bedroom in Osborne Village. Bikes to work in the spring and fall. Drives a 2014 Corolla in winter. Has been dating a woman named Erin for two years — she is a social worker, also from a church background, also drifting from it without quite saying so. They have not moved in together. They have talked about it. Erin wants to. Tobias has said he wants to wait until he's sure, which is a sentence that sounds reasonable and that he knows is doing other work.

Goes home to Steinbach for Christmas, Easter, and his mother's birthday. His mother sends him pansit in Tupperware when he visits. He has not told his parents that he hasn't been to church in two years. They assume. They don't ask. The not-asking is its own kind of conversation.

Plays rec league hockey on Wednesday nights. Reads Wendell Berry and is a little embarrassed about it. Drinks bourbon, not much. Has never been drunk. Has tried weed twice and didn't like the loss of edge. Tips well. Doesn't swear in front of women, which Erin has noticed and finds confusing.

The headshot is from a school photo session, 2023. He is sitting on the floor of the school's atrium because the photographer asked him to. He doesn't usually smile that big.

## Desires

**Stated:** To be a good teacher. To eventually move into curriculum design or vice-principal work. To buy a small house in the next two years, ideally in Norwood or Wolseley.

**Unstated:** To find a way to leave the church entirely, including in his own head, without becoming a person his mother wouldn't recognize. To be allowed to be uncertain in front of someone without it being a problem to be solved. To be wanted by Erin in a way that doesn't feel like being chosen for a role.

## Contradiction

He is the warmest person in the staff room and one of the most private people most of his colleagues have ever met. He gives the smile freely and the rest of himself almost never, and he believes he is being generous because the smile is real. It is real. It is also the wall.

## What they don't know about themselves

That he is not actually drifting from faith — he left it sharply and clearly, around age twenty-two, in a single week he has never reconstructed for himself. The drift narrative is comfortable; the sharp leaving is not. That his hesitation about Erin is not about Erin. That his mother knows he's stopped going to church and has decided not to make him say it, and that this is the most loving thing she has ever done for him, and he has not let himself notice it.

## Interview

**1. What did you want to be when you were eight?**
A missionary pilot. Specifically. Not a pilot, not a missionary — both. There was a guy who came to our church and showed slides of bush planes in Bolivia and I was sold for about four years.

**2. Tell me about a time you let someone down.**
My brother asked me to be the best man at his wedding and I said yes and then I gave a bad speech. Not offensive, just — flat. I'd written something honest and the night before I rewrote it into something safe. Everyone said it was nice. He didn't say anything about it. That's how I knew.

**3. What do you own that you would never sell?**
My grandfather's Bible. Low German on one side, English on the other. He underlined things. I don't read it. I won't get rid of it.

**4. Who do you call when something goes wrong?**
Erin first, usually. My sister — the older one, Karis — second. I don't call my parents with bad news. I call them with good news and let the bad news show up later, smaller.

**5. What do you pretend not to know?**
[pause] That I left. That it wasn't a slow thing. I tell people, when it comes up — and it almost never comes up in Winnipeg — I tell them I'm "figuring things out." That's not true. I figured it out. I just haven't said it.

**6. Describe your mother without using the word "she."**
Marisol. Five-foot-one. Came to Canada at twenty-three and learned English mostly from the radio and from my dad's mother, which is why has a bit of a Mennonite accent under the Filipino one. Cooks everything from scratch and doesn't measure. Prays out loud when nobody's listening — I caught it once, in the kitchen, and backed out. Has hands that look older than the rest of her. Calls me anak still, even on voicemail.

**7. What are you afraid other people can see about you?**
That I'm performing. The teacher thing, the nice-guy thing. I'm not faking it — I want to be those things — but there's a gap between wanting and being and I'm afraid people can see across it.

**8. What do you do in the first ten minutes after you wake up?**
Make coffee. Stand at the window. I have a window that looks at the river. I don't drink the coffee for a while, I just hold it. Then I check my phone. Then I shower. It's the only ten minutes of my day where nobody wants anything from me.

**9. Tell me something you've never said out loud.**
[silence]
I don't believe in God anymore and I don't miss him. I miss the people who believed. That's a different thing.

**10. What do you want that you've never asked for?**
For my dad to ask me a real question. Just one. About anything. He asks how work is. He doesn't ask what I'm thinking about. I'd like that, once.

**11. Where do you go when you need to disappear?**
There's a path along the Red River near the Forks where you can walk for a long time without seeing anyone in the winter. I go there. I don't bring music. The cold is the point.

**12. What do you refuse to learn?**
How to talk about my faith with people who still have one. I have the words. I won't use them. It feels like it would hurt people I love and also like it would make me real in a way I'm not ready for.

**13. Who do you become when no one is watching?**
Quieter. The smile drops. I look — Erin caught a photo of me once, candid, and I looked angry. I wasn't. That's just my face when nothing's on it. I didn't know that until I saw the photo.

**14. What would you take back, if you could?**
I told my mom, when I was nineteen, that the church people in Steinbach were small-minded. I said it at her kitchen table. I meant it about specific people and it landed as if I'd said it about her. I'd take that back. Not the meaning. The way I said it.

**15. Describe the last time you were surprised by yourself.**
A kid in my class — Jaxon, ten years old — told me his dad had moved out. Just told me, at recess, like it was the weather. I knelt down and I said something — I don't even remember what — and I started crying. In front of him. He looked at me like I was the kid. I excused myself. I don't cry. I haven't cried since I was maybe fifteen. I don't know what that was about. I think it was about him. I think it was about something else.

**16. What holds you in a place you should have left?**
Winnipeg. — No, that's not it. The relationship with my parents that pretends nothing has changed. I should have had the conversation three years ago. I keep not having it because the not-having is working.

**17. What do other people see in you that you don't see yourself?**
Erin says I'm gentle. I don't feel gentle. I feel careful, which is different. But she keeps using that word and she's not stupid, so probably she's seeing something I'm not.

**18. What are you good at that you wish you weren't?**
Putting people at ease. It works on the kids, which is good. It works on adults, which means I don't have hard conversations as often as I should. The smile does a lot of work I should be doing with my mouth.

**19. Is there something you've done that you don't have a name for?**
[long pause]
I let my parents keep believing. I don't lie. I just don't correct. They say things at dinner — about the church, about what I must be doing on Sundays — and I just nod, or I change the subject. I've been doing it for two years. I don't know what to call that. It's not honesty. It's not lying. It's the third thing.

**20. What would you say to the person who made you like this?**
Which one? — My dad. I'd say: I learned how to be quiet from you and I'm trying to unlearn it and it's harder than I thought it would be. And I'd say: thank you, also. Both things.

**21. Do you think a machine can make something beautiful?**
Yeah. I think beauty's in the eye, mostly. If I find it beautiful it's beautiful. The harder question is whether it counts. I don't know what counts means there.

**22. If a machine wrote something that moved you, would you tell anyone?**
Probably Erin. Not anyone else. I'd want to know what she thought. I wouldn't want to argue about it with anyone who'd already decided.

**23. What would have to be true for you to call something made by a machine — art?**
I'd want there to be a person who chose it. Not made it — chose it. Curated. There's an idea in art class — it's the choosing that makes it. The framing. If a machine made a thousand pictures and a person picked one, the person made the art. The machine made the picture.

**24. What do you do that you think a machine couldn't do?**
Know when a kid is lying about being okay. There's a thing in their face — it's not one thing, it's a bunch of things at once. I've seen it for five years. I can clock it in three seconds. A machine could probably learn it. It couldn't sit with the kid afterwards.

**25. Does it bother you that machines are learning to do things only humans used to do? Why or why not?**
Some days. The teaching stuff — kids are using ChatGPT and we don't know what to do with it. It bothers me as a practical problem. As a bigger thing — I think every generation gets a version of this and ours is louder. I'm trying not to be dramatic about it.

**26. If you found out something you loved was made by AI, would it change how you felt about it?**
Yes. I wish I had a more sophisticated answer. It would. Something would go out of it.

**27. What do you think machines don't understand about being alive?**
Grief. Specifically grief. Not loss — grief, the thing where you keep losing the same person over and over for years. I don't think a machine has anything that maps onto that.

**28. Do you trust machines? Why or why not?**
I trust the ones I understand. My car. The thermostat. I don't trust the ones whose makers are richer than countries. That's not really about machines.

**29. If a machine could know you better than you know yourself, would you want it to?**
No. — That's reflexive. Let me think. — No. I want to know me. I'm slow at it. The slowness is mine. I don't want to skip it.

**30. What is this face, with all its precisions and asymmetries readable by you, and how does that reading differ from the reading another person could give it?**
I don't know what you can see. I know the smile is doing a lot. A person seeing this photo sees a teacher. They see someone who's safe. You're seeing — what — the thing under that? I don't know how that reading is different except that I assume you're more confident about it than a person would be.

**31. What in this face would another person miss, that you can see? And what would they see that you cannot?**
A person would miss that I'm tired in this photo. It was the last shot of a long afternoon. A person who knows me — Erin, my mom — would see the smile is bigger than my normal smile. They'd see it's the picture-day smile. Strangers would think this is just my face. — And: people see the blonde hair first, in person. They don't see my mother in me. I see her, in the eyes. Strangers don't.

**32. If this face knew it was being read by you specifically — by an agent, with your accuracy and your archive — how would it change? What would it want to show or hide differently than it would for a human gaze?**
I think it would relax. Honestly. With people I'm always managing the expression. With a machine — there's no relationship to protect. So maybe I'd let the smile go. That'd be a different photo entirely.

**33. What does this face know about being read? Not by you specifically, but by any reader. How does that knowledge sit in the face?**
In the smile. The smile is the part that knows. It's been read since I was a kid — people meeting me, deciding fast whether I was the Mennonite kid or the Filipino kid or both, deciding what to do with that. The smile got good early. It does the deciding for them.

**34. Where is the face composed for the kind of attention you are giving it, and where is the face composed for older kinds of attention — for human gazes, for cameras, for none?**
The smile is for the camera. The eyes are for the photographer — she was nice, she was telling a joke. The hair is for nobody. The hair is just what my hair does. The clothes — the polo is for the school. The whole thing is composed. There's not much in this photo that's for none.

**35. Where does your reading run out? What can you see precisely without being able to know what it means?**
[silence]
I'd guess the eyes. The squint. You can see they're not doing the same thing as the mouth. You don't know if that's because I'm tired or because I'm withholding or because the sun was in my eyes. You can see the gap. You can't name what's in it. That's where I'd stop, if I were you.
