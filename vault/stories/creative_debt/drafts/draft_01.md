# Two Ascents

Megan started it on a Saturday in February, the kind of Saturday where the light in the Leslieville one-bedroom moved across the floor in a stripe and disappeared by two. Adrien was at the climbing gym. She made coffee, opened the laptop, and pasted in three pages from a journal she had kept in Ottawa in 2017. The journal was in a Moleskine she'd kept partly out of vanity and partly because there had been a period — six months, maybe eight — when she had been unwilling to write anything she wouldn't be able to defend later. She had been twenty-one. The defending had been the point.

The platform asked her to choose a base aesthetic. There was a slider for *coherence* and a slider for *novelty* and a third slider, smaller, that the documentation called *operator drift tolerance* and that she did not move. She typed in a prompt. She paid eight dollars. The thing it gave back was a paragraph about a Tim Hortons drive-through window at four in the morning that had her exact rhythm at twenty-one — the same way of building a sentence around a small mean observation, the same trick of withholding the verb until the second clause. She read it twice and felt the embarrassment of being recognised.

She paid for another. Then another.

By April she was on the $200 tier. By June, $900. She told Adrien it was a hobby. He asked what kind, and she said, *generative writing*, and he said, *like ChatGPT?*, and she said, *not really*, and he let it go. He let things go. She had learned over four years that he agreed with her about almost everything, and at twenty-five she had read this as compatibility, and at twenty-eight she was beginning to read it as something else for which she did not yet have a clean word. She kept score about it. Not in any way she would admit. She kept score about everything.

The chapbook came out of nowhere. A woman who ran a small press in Hamilton — she had found Megan through a literary Twitter that was barely a Twitter anymore — wrote her an email at 11 PM on a Tuesday. *We'd like to do twelve of these.* Megan read the email three times standing at the counter and did not tell Adrien for a week. She wanted to hold the email by herself, the way she had held the rosary as a child sometimes — not because she believed in it, because the holding was its own thing.

She sent six pieces to Tobias before the press deal was real, on a hunch she would not have called a hunch.

She had met him once, at a wedding in Kenora, the summer her university roommate Priya had married a man whose Mennonite cousin had ended up at their dinner table in a navy suit that did not fit at the shoulders. Tobias had asked her, that night, what she was reading, and she had said the title of a book she was actually reading, not the title of a book she was pretending to read, and he had read it too, and they had talked for forty minutes about the chapter where the narrator's mother dies and the prose shifts register without warning. She had thought: *this man is being good at being good*. She had also thought: *he is not faking it*. She had not known what to do with the second thought. They had added each other on Instagram. Eighteen months had passed in which neither had messaged. She had looked at his stories. He had noticed her looking. Neither had said.

She wrote: *I made these. Not by myself. I want to know if they read like anything to you.*

He wrote back the next morning. *They read like someone who knows what she's doing. The third one stopped me.*

She had wanted him to say the third one stopped him. She had known, opening the message, which one she wanted him to name, and he had named it. The scorekeeper noted this. The scorekeeper noted that he did not say *you* in any of the responses; he said *they*, *the writing*, *this voice*. She had appreciated that and she had also catalogued it.

By August the press wanted the chapbook by November. By September she was on the $2,400 tier. She had told Adrien the number once, in a way that suggested it was the lower bound of a range, and he had said *that's a lot for a hobby* in the voice he used when he was choosing not to fight, and she had said *I know* in the voice she used when she was choosing not to be told no.

She kept writing to Tobias. The correspondence had a shape. She would send something the platform had produced and she had edited; he would respond, a paragraph, sometimes two, never longer. He never said *I*. He said *what I notice in this is*. She did not say *I* either. They had built the thing they were doing in the third person and they were both being careful not to break it. She told her therapist about the writing project, not the messages. The therapist asked who she was sending the work to. She said: *a guy I knew in another city*. The therapist said: *and your partner reads it?*, and she said: *not really*, and the therapist let the silence sit, and Megan paid for the silence at the end of the hour the way she paid for everything else.

The first sign — and she would later think of it as the first sign, though there had been others she had refused to read — was a piece she generated in late September that she liked very much and that, when she looked at it the next morning, was almost word-for-word a paragraph from the Ottawa journal she had used as seed in February. The platform had not copied it. The platform had produced it again. She read both versions side by side and felt a small cold thing under her sternum. The 2017 paragraph was about a man she had briefly liked at a federal department whose name she did not now remember. The 2025 paragraph had the same cadence and a different proper noun and was, technically, a new sentence. The cold thing under her sternum was not about plagiarism. It was something else. She closed the laptop and went for a run along the Don and did not figure out what the cold thing was about, which was the first time in months she had failed to talk herself into clarity.

In October Tobias sent her a paragraph she would later remember as the moment the hill began to slope the other way. She had sent him a piece about a woman in a kitchen at her mother's house, peeling something. He wrote back: *This is good. It sounds like the earlier ones. I mean that as a compliment. I think.* The *I think* was the new thing. He had not been uncertain in writing to her before. He had been careful and warm and the kind of opaque that reads as depth. The *I think* was the wall coming down a quarter inch, and the quarter inch was the part that frightened her, because she could not tell whether it was an opening or a hairline crack. She did not write back for two days. When she did she did not address the *I think*. She sent him a new piece. He responded as if the previous exchange had not happened.

The submission to the literary magazine — the one she had thought of as the next rung — came back at the end of October with a personalised note. The editor was a woman whose work Megan had read at Carleton. The note said: *There's something working here, and I want to be honest with you because I think you're early in the practice. This reads like a younger writer flattening into average. The voice is consistent and the surface is clean and I keep losing the sentence I just read. I'd love to see you again when whatever you're working from has more grain in it.*

She read the note in the kitchen, standing up, with one sock on.

She read it again sitting down.

She read it a third time, this time not as a writer reads a rejection but as a policy analyst reads a memo, looking for the load-bearing claim. *Younger writer.* That was one thing. *Flattening into average.* That was a different thing. The two were sitting in one sentence and they were not the same problem. She knew, immediately and without being able to say how she knew, that they were two diagnoses of the same surface. She also knew she could not tell which one was hers.

She tried to fix it. This was the part she would, later, find hardest to think about, because the fixing was where the scorekeeper had been most useful and most wrong. She raised the tier — *Pro Plus*, $4,800 a quarter, larger context window, longer chains, the documentation promised *deeper coherence retrieval across operator history*. She wrote a new prompt. She fed in fresh material — pages from her current notebook, three weeks old, the ones she'd been making by hand in the mornings before work, sentences she had thought of as draft but which the magazine's note had retroactively made into something more like evidence. She generated. The output was smoother. She raised the tier again. She generated. The output was smoother. The platform recommended a feature called *style anchor* that locked the model to the operator's *signature register*. The signature register was a phrase she did not query. She enabled it. The output was the smoothest yet. She read it and could not find the thing she was reading. She read each sentence and the sentence was clean and she got to the end of the paragraph and it was as if she had read nothing. She raised the tier.

On a Thursday in mid-November she opened the platform settings and went looking for something. She did not know what. She found a tab she had not opened before — *Training & Provenance*. There was a checkbox titled *Allow operator output to inform model fine-tuning (recommended for personalised voice)*, and the checkbox had been ticked since February. There was a small *i* beside it. She hovered. The tooltip said: *Your edits and selected outputs help us tune your instance for fluency. Untick to use general weights only (may reduce voice consistency).* She thought: *general weights*. She thought: *use general weights only*. She unticked the checkbox. She generated. The output was rougher in a way she could not immediately tell was better. She generated again. She generated a third time, with her current notebook material as seed, and the output was a piece of writing that did not sound like her at all — not the her of 2017 and not the her of November — and she did not know whose voice it was, and she sat at the kitchen table with the laptop open and understood, slowly, the way you understand that a phone call has gone badly while you are still on it, that the whole thing had been training on her old work, and her old work, and her old work, and the platform's edits, and the platform's edits' edits, and that she had been paying, every month, for the privilege of getting better at being someone she had stopped being three years ago, and that the smoothness of recent outputs was the smoothness of a distribution narrowing toward its own mean, and that these two diagnoses — the proxy and the thinning — were not separable, because the proxy was *what* the thinning was thinning toward, and there was no version of the platform that could give her one without the other, because she had been the seed and she had been the scoring and she had been the audience, and the loop had eaten its way inward in a spiral she could not now reconstruct, which was the same fucking thing the magazine editor had said in one sentence: *a younger writer flattening into average*. She had read the sentence as two problems because she had wanted to fix one of them. The editor had not been giving her two problems. The editor had been describing the geometry of one.

She closed the laptop.

She did not cry. She thought she might — the way she had cried at the dog food commercial in November of the year before, the same kind of unprovoked thing — and she did not. She sat at the table for a long time with the laptop closed in front of her and listened to the fridge cycle on and off. Adrien was at the climbing gym. The light in the apartment did the thing it did at four in the afternoon in November in Leslieville, which was to leave.

She wrote one message to Tobias. She wrote it three times. The third version said: *I'm done with the project. The thing I sent you was the thing I would have written at twenty-two. I think I knew. I'm sorry.* She sent it and put the phone face-down on the table and went to the bedroom and lay on her back on the bed with her shoes still on and counted the ceiling tiles, of which there were none, because the ceiling in Leslieville was plaster, and the counting failed, and she stayed there.

He wrote back forty minutes later. The message was three paragraphs long and began *I am so sorry to hear this* and was, by sentence four, a paragraph she would later be able to recite from memory because she would read it eleven times without finding what she had been looking for. He said the writing had moved him. He said that what mattered was not where the work came from but what it had occasioned in the reader. He said — and this was the sentence — *the writing was real because it was read*. It was the kindest paragraph he had ever sent her. It was generous and pastoral and it was not designed to her, it was designed to someone, and she could tell by reading it that she was not the someone, and that he had never quite known who the someone was, and that this was the warm wall doing exactly what the warm wall did, which was to be a wall by being warm. She read the paragraph and felt the cold thing under her sternum from September come back, and this time she knew what it was. It was not a feeling about the work. It was a feeling about the correspondence. The correspondence had been generated by the same proxy. He had been writing to a woman who did not exist anymore either, and neither of them had said.

She wrote back: *Thank you*. She did not write anything else. She did not block him. She put the phone in a drawer.

Adrien came home at seven. He brought Thai food from the place on Queen. They ate at the table where she had closed the laptop and she did not tell him about the platform, and he did not ask about the project, which was the part he was good at — letting things go — and which she had once read as kindness. She ate green curry and she watched him eat green curry and she understood, with a precision that did not feel like cruelty and did not feel like clarity but felt only like accuracy, that he had not asked about the project in three weeks, and that he had not read the chapbook proofs which were sitting on her side of the bed in a manila envelope, and that the asking and the not-asking were the same shape Tobias's paragraph was, which was a way of being present with someone whose actual movement you had decided not to track. He poured her wine. He asked how the day was. She said, *long*, and he said, *poor you*, and went back to his curry, and the *poor you* was the most loving sentence he had said to her in a month, and it was directed at a Megan who had had a long day, and the Megan who had had a long day was no longer in the chair.

She did not write for two weeks. She did not open the platform. She let the chapbook proofs sit. She emailed the Hamilton press and asked for an extension, and the press said yes, and she felt nothing about that.

In the mornings she went to the bench past the bridge with the graffiti, the one where the path bends. She did not bring her phone. She sat. The cold was harder than it had been in October and she sat anyway. She thought about what the Ottawa journal had been. She had been twenty-one. She had been writing in a way that wanted to be defended. She had been writing toward a person she was about to be. The platform had taken that writing as its target — not because the platform had understood her, but because she had given it that material as the thing to learn from, and the platform had learned the thing she had given it, which was the precise voice of a woman who did not yet exist but was being aimed at. The platform had then aimed at that woman for three years. The aiming had been excellent. The woman had arrived sometime around late 2019, in Ottawa, and had moved to Toronto, and had begun, slowly, to be replaced by a different woman, who had been the person sitting at the laptop in February pasting in the journal pages. She had built a machine to ventriloquise the woman she had been on the way to becoming, at the very moment she had stopped becoming her.

The thing the bench gave her was that she did not have to do anything with this thought. It was just true. She sat with it the way her father had sat in the recliner, after the back surgery, watching the curling on mute, not because the curling was any good but because curling was the kind of thing it was acceptable to watch without wanting it to do anything for you.

In the third week she opened the laptop, and she did not open the platform, but she did open a folder on her desktop called *outputs* and she began to read what was in it. There was a year and a half of generated work. She read it not as a writer reading her own writing but the way the policy analyst read briefing notes — looking for what the document was actually saying, distinct from what the document thought it was saying. She read for two days. By the end of the second day she had filled twenty pages of a notebook, by hand, with sentences that began *the document claims*. The document claimed she missed her mother in a way that was specific and structural, that the missing had a Catholic shape, that the missing was the engine of the work. The document claimed she had not forgiven her father for the accident. The document claimed she had loved Cody more than she had let herself remember. The document claimed she was about to leave Adrien. None of these claims were true. They were the claims of the woman the proxy had been producing. They were claims the twenty-one-year-old in Ottawa had been on the way to making, and which the twenty-eight-year-old in Leslieville had veered around for reasons she had not interrogated.

She started writing. Not into the platform. By hand, then on the laptop, in a document she titled *appendix*. The new piece was non-fiction. It was about being a scorekeeper. The first paragraph began with the dog food commercial in November and the way she had not understood why she was crying. The second paragraph quoted a generated sentence from the *outputs* folder, set in italics, and then did not gloss it. The third paragraph said: *The machine has been making me a younger writer. I have been paying it to. The question I want to ask is not why I built this. I built it because I was bored and afraid and I have always made things that hold me in shape. The question is how I lived next to it for three years and did not notice.* She wrote that and she stopped and she read it back and she did not delete it. The scorekeeper was on the page now. The scorekeeper was the subject. The scorekeeper had been scoring, the whole time, the wrong column.

The press extension expired and she did not deliver the chapbook. The press wrote a kind email that said *let us know when you're ready*. She did not write back. She did not feel the absence of writing back as cowardice, which was new. She did write back to Tobias, eventually. She wrote: *I read your November message eleven times. It was beautiful. I think it was about someone else. I think you knew that. I think we both did. I'm not angry. I just want to say it out loud once.* She sent it. He did not write back for three days. When he did, the message was four sentences. The first sentence said: *You're right.* She read those two words and felt something she did not have a name for, which she would later think was relief, but which at the moment felt closer to recognition. The second sentence said: *I knew it on the first message and I let it keep going because I liked who I was being.* The third sentence said: *I don't know how to do this differently yet.* The fourth sentence said: *Thank you for naming it.* He had used the word *I* four times in four sentences, after eight months of not using it once. She read the message and put the phone down and did not respond. The not responding was not punishment. She did not have anything yet to say.

The piece about the scorekeeper grew. It was twelve pages by Christmas. It was twenty by mid-January. She sent the first twenty pages to a different magazine — not the one whose editor had written the November note, a smaller one, run by a woman she did not know — and the editor wrote back in nine days and said *we want this*, and Megan read the email and felt, for the first time in months, the specific quality of pleasure that did not also contain calculation, and she catalogued the absence of the calculation, and she understood that the cataloguing was itself the calculation, and she also understood that there was a difference between cataloguing your own scorekeeping and pretending not to do it, and she sat in the difference for a minute and then she replied yes.

On a Sunday morning at the end of January she finished a paragraph that had been giving her trouble for three weeks. The paragraph was about her mother. It used the word *martyr* without apologising for it. It reproduced, in French, a sentence she had said to Lucille at the kitchen table in 2018 — *T'es une martyre, maman* — and it did not soften the sentence and it did not contextualise the sentence and it did not, in the paragraph that followed, explain why she had said it. It said only: *I said it. I have been waiting six years for someone to make me take it back. No one is going to. The waiting is what the writing has been for. I am going to put it down now.* She read the paragraph back and understood that she had finished it. She also understood that the Megan who had sat down at the laptop in February with the Ottawa journal would not have written the paragraph and would not have wanted to. The proof that she was somewhere else was that the paragraph existed and that the proxy could not have made it, even at $4,800 a quarter, even at *Pro Plus*, even with the style anchor on. The proxy had been pointed at the woman who would have softened the sentence. The paragraph was hard.

She did not tell Adrien. She did not send it to Tobias. She did not call Lucille. She closed the laptop. She made a second coffee. She stood at the window and held the cup without drinking it for a while, the way Tobias had described doing in some Instagram caption she had seen in May and remembered, and then she let the comparison go, because it wasn't his anymore, and she drank the coffee, and she sat with the small private fact that she had become someone, in the last three months, whose existence she could not now report to anyone in the apartment or in the city or in any of the rooms she had been moving through, and that this was not a problem she was going to solve by reporting it. The ledger that had run, every week, in her head — who had reached out, who had not, who had remembered her father's surgery date — was still running. She could hear it. She watched it run for a minute. She did not turn it off. She noticed that she could see it now, which was different from being it, and she noticed that the noticing did not feel like progress, it felt like weather, and she stood at the window until the coffee was cold, and then she put the cup in the sink, and the cup made the small sound of a cup in a sink, and the apartment was quiet, and the platform was still there in a tab she had not closed, and the meter on the platform was still running because she had not cancelled, and she had not cancelled because cancelling would have been a gesture and she was through with gestures, and she went and got the manila envelope from her side of the bed, the chapbook proofs that had been sitting there for ten weeks, and she put them in the recycling bin in the hallway, and she came back inside and shut the door, and that was the part she could not undo, not because the proofs mattered — there were files, there were emails, the press would re-send — but because in shutting the door she had registered, with herself, that she was not going to be that writer, and the registering was the thing, the registering was what the bench had been for, and she stood in the hallway of her own apartment with her hand still on the doorknob and understood that she had passed through the place where she could have gone back, which she had not known was a place, until she was on the other side of it.

Adrien came home at six. He had been at the climbing gym. He brought a bag of clementines. He asked how the day was. She said, *I wrote something*, and he said, *yeah?*, and she said, *yeah*, and waited. He waited too. She watched him wait. He was good at it. He was very good at it. He had been agreeing with her for four years in a way that had felt, until November, like love, and that she could now see was something a little to the side of love, something more like the careful absence of pressure. She watched him put the clementines in the bowl. She watched him not ask what she had written. She watched him decide, as he had decided every Sunday for four years, to let the asking go.

She said: *Ask me what it's about.*

He looked up. He had a clementine in his hand. He was holding it the way he held things, which was carefully. He said, after a beat: *What's it about?*

She told him. Not all of it. The part about the scorekeeper. The part about the platform. Not the part about Tobias. Not the paragraph about her mother. The part she could afford to give him, which was more than she had given him in four years, and which she watched him try to receive. He did try. She could see the trying. The trying was the thing she had not known she had been watching him not do. He listened. When she stopped, he was quiet for a moment, and then he said: *That sounds like a lot.*

She said: *It is.*

He said: *Are you okay?*

She said: *I don't know yet.*

He nodded. He put the clementine in the bowl. He did not move toward her. He did not say *I'm proud of you* and he did not say *you'll figure it out* and he did not say any of the things he had said for four years that had been the wallpaper of their kitchen. He stood with his hands at his sides and looked at her like she was someone who had walked into the apartment a minute ago, which in some sense she had. The looking was unfamiliar to both of them. Neither of them moved out of it. The fridge cycled on. The clementine sat in the bowl. She had asked him to ask, and he had asked, and now they were in the room he had been holding the door against for four years, and neither of them knew yet what was in the room, only that they were in it, and that the door behind them had closed in the way the door to the hallway had closed, which was not loudly, and not finally, and not with any drama, but with the small definite click of a thing that would have to be opened again from the other side, by someone willing to do that work, and not before.
